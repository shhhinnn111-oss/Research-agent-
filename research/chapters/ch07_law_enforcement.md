# CHAPTER 7 — LAW ENFORCEMENT, POLICING AND INSTITUTIONAL REFORM

## 7.1 Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Police: Historical Evolution, Structure and Current Strength

**Evolution.** The KP Police descends from the colonial frontier constabulary and the Punjab Police model of 1861; it operated for 156 years under the Police Act 1861 (and, in the NMDs, under the extraordinary Levies/Khasadar regime) before the **KP Police Act 2017** provided a modern statutory framework (see §7.2). The force has been reshaped by three shocks: the post-2001 insurgency, which transformed it from a law-and-order force into a counter-insurgency light infantry in all but name; the 2014 Army Public School attack and the National Action Plan, which expanded its counter-terrorism mandate and budgets; and the post-2018 merger, which added seven districts and absorbed 28,000 tribal personnel.

**Structure and strength.** The KP Police is headed by an Inspector-General (IGP) and organized into regional commands (RPOs) and districts (DPOs), with specialized units including: the **Counter-Terrorism Department (CTD)**; the **Special Branch** (intelligence); the **Elite Force** (roughly 6,000 personnel trained for high-risk operations); the **Frontier Reserve Force (FRF)** (~10,000); the **KP Bureau of Investigation (KPBI)** (~3,000, investigation); the **Tourism Police**; traffic and constabulary units; and the capital-city command in Peshawar (~8,000). Total strength: official statements place the force at approximately 70,000–90,000 personnel (the KP government reported a ratio of one police officer per 291 citizens in 2026); provincial officials have cited ~130,000 sanctioned posts in public debate, indicating a significant vacancy margin. The force's budget for FY2025-26 was reported at **Rs 170 billion — 8.7 per cent of the provincial budget**, up 37 per cent over two years (an additional Rs 46 billion), with ~Rs 17 billion for security development and ~Rs 18 billion for salaries; the government announced plans to raise the allocation to 12 per cent (Tribune 2026a). These figures mark KP as the province devoting the largest *share* of budget to policing — a reflection of need, and a long-standing political claim in Peshawar that the federal government should reimburse the cost of a "front-line province."

**The dual mandate problem.** A force trained and deployed to fight a militant insurgency struggles to deliver ordinary policing: investigation, prosecution support, dispute response, and protection of the vulnerable. This study's assessment is that KP Police has effectively been converted into a paramilitary constabulary with a policing appendage — an inversion with two consequences: ordinary crime (including extortion, land-grabbing, and organized crime) grows with impunity, and public trust in police as *police* erodes, which in turn reduces cooperation with counter-terrorism.

## 7.2 The KP Police Reforms (2013–2023): Achievements and Shortcomings

**The reform decade.** The most consequential reform period followed the 2013 provincial elections, when the PTI-led government appointed a reformist IGP (Nasir Khan Durrani) and pursued a package that included: merit-based recruitment and training reform; new training infrastructure; the establishment of **Public Safety Commissions** and **Complaint Authorities**; the **Dispute Resolution Councils** (DRCs); welfare and housing improvements; the creation of the **Elite Force** and **Rapid Response Force**; the strengthening of the CTD; and — decisively — the **KP Police Act 2017**, which: (i) gave the IGP a fixed three-year tenure (removable only through defined processes); (ii) established provincial and district public safety commissions to oversee police performance; (iii) created complaint mechanisms against police misconduct; (iv) formalized the police's duties, powers and accountability; and (v) introduced planning and professional standards provisions.

**Achievements.** Independent commentary and comparative assessments generally credit the reforms with: depoliticizing senior appointments (partially); improving training quality and facility standards; expanding the CTD; introducing community-facing mechanisms (DRCs, complaint cells); professionalizing public communications; and producing a cadre of trained officers whose performance in operations (including the 2026 Kohat clearance, where Elite Force and CTD teams retook the complex) exceeded earlier standards.

**Shortcomings.** Six are documented. (i) *Implementation gap*: the Public Safety Commissions have functioned unevenly or not at all in several districts; commission membership and activity have been politically contested. (ii) *Command turnover and politicization disputes*: the fixed-tenure provision has been eroded in practice, with frequent IGP changes and provincial-federal friction over appointments and postings; the PML-N's September 2026 "white paper" alleged political influence in police postings and counter-terrorism preparedness failures. (iii) *Resourcing gaps*: despite budget increases, the force remains short of protected mobility, counter-IED equipment, night-vision devices, forensics, and — critically — investigation capacity. (iv) *Welfare deficits*: the shuhada (martyr) compensation and family-support regimes, though improved, have been criticized as slow and uneven. (v) *Investigation quality*: the KPBI's creation notwithstanding, prosecution success remains constrained by weak crime-scene management, forensics, and witness protection. (vi) *NMD integration*: the reform design treated the merged districts as an extension of the settled-district model rather than as a distinct policing environment (see §7.5).

**Assessment.** The KP Police Act 2017 remains the legal foundation of any future reform and is, by regional standards, a progressive instrument. Its fate illustrates a general lesson: *legal reform without fiscal guarantees, personnel protection and political insulation produces partial implementation*. The 2020s' reform debate has therefore shifted from statute to capacity and protection — with the 2025–26 casualty data making the case unanswerable.

## 7.3 Counter-Terrorism Department (CTD): Capabilities, Operations and Performance

**Origins and mandate.** KP's CTD was established in 2010 (following the February 2010 attacks in Peshawar that killed dozens), initially as a specialized investigation and operations unit, with a network of police stations, intelligence wings and interrogation capacity. Under the NAP and subsequent reforms it received expanded budgets, personnel and technology.

**Documented performance (2025–26).** The CTD's own December 2025 statistics and independent reporting record: attacks on police rising from **327 (2024) to 510 (2025)**; **137 terrorist attacks in 2025** (against 147 in 2024) despite a **50 per cent rise in registered terrorism cases (1,058 → 1,588)**; **320 retaliatory operations** in response to 158 attacks; the arrest of **25 high-value terrorists** with bounties on their heads; and 124 Federal Constabulary personnel killed with 244 injured. In 2026, the CTD has been central to responses to the Swat and Kohat attacks, the disruption of drone networks (including the foiling of 341 drone attacks in H1), and multiple IBOs.

**Capability assessment.** The CTD's strengths are: dedicated personnel with operational experience; a functioning intelligence wing with informant networks; integration with IBO planning; and the legal authority (ATA, 1997) to detain, interrogate and prosecute. Its weaknesses, documented by official data and independent criticism, are: (i) *personnel shortfall* (3,844 sanctioned posts against 3,113 deployed — a 19 per cent gap; 638 field-operator recruitments incomplete as of mid-2026); (ii) *equipment shortfalls* (Rs 7.77 billion approved for equipment against Rs 7.05 billion released by June 2026; the claim that the entire province has only nine M4 rifles and eleven night-vision devices, advanced by a federal coordinator in September 2026, marked the low estimate of a contested but revealing debate); (iii) *investigation and prosecution quality* (conviction rates remain low; cases collapse on evidentiary grounds); (iv) *fragmentation* with the military intelligence architecture (Chapter 6); and (v) *human rights risk* — allegations of extrajudicial actions, of torture during interrogation, and of detention without charge have been documented by HRCP and international organizations.

## 7.4 Rapid Response Force and Elite Force: Specialized Units Assessment

The **Elite Force** (est. 2014, ~6,000 personnel) and the **Rapid Response Force / Frontier Reserve Force** constitute KP's specialized tactical capability, trained (in significant part) by the military and equipped with limited armoured and heavy weapons. Their performance in complex attacks — most visibly the September 2026 Kohat Police Lines clearance, conducted by Elite Force commandos and CTD teams with military QRF support — demonstrates genuine capability. Their limitations: (i) coverage breadth (specialized units cannot garrison the province; most police stations remain vulnerable to platoon-strength assault); (ii) dependence on military support for sustained combat; (iii) counter-IED and anti-drone capability gaps exposed by 2026 attacks (the Karak quadcopter attacks targeted three police facilities); and (iv) force protection deficits at static sites (APCs and checkposts remain the most common casualties). A capability audit — never published — would be the logical precondition of investment decisions, but no such public audit exists.

## 7.5 Levies and Khasadar Forces in Merged Districts: Integration and Challenges

**The transition.** As detailed in Chapter 5, the Levies (~15,673 personnel) and Khasadar forces were brought under KP Police command by ordinances/legislation in 2019, with a combined strength of approximately 28,000. The transition's problems fall into four categories.

*First, doctrinal mismatch.* Levies were border constabulary; Khasadars were a communal guard force with minimal training; KP Police are a settled-district force governed by the Police Act. Integrating the first two into the third without designing a *purpose-built NMD policing model* produced a hybrid: local personnel without police powers' confidence; settled-district officers without local knowledge; and neither with the equipment or training to confront TTP units.

*Second, personnel issues.* Khasadars' regularization, pensions, and grade fitment generated grievances and protests; many personnel remain uncertain of their status years after the merger. The number of new police posts created (~3,550 province-wide, including NMDs; with promises of 6,000+ initially) remains inadequate to the threat.

*Third, local legitimacy.* In parts of the NMDs, police are viewed as (a) predators (checkpoint extortion), (b) outsiders (settled-district officers), or (c) collaborators with historical abuses. Where communities trust their local force — as in some Bajaur and Kurram pockets — they provide intelligence and refuse shelter to militants; where they do not, they withhold both.

*Fourth, vulnerability.* Locally recruited officers are susceptible to coercion (threats to families), bribery, and — in some cases — ideological sympathy, particularly where recruitment standards were relaxed to meet quotas.

**The model question.** Three options exist for NMD policing: (i) continue the hybrid; (ii) build a *constabulary-style NMD force* with community-embedded units, tribal recruitment with vetting, dedicated training, and provincial oversight; or (iii) return policing functions to FC/paramilitary control (rejected by the merger's logic and by provincial politics). This study recommends option (ii) within the KP Police framework — a "NMD Wing" with its own doctrine, training, equipment and community-accountability mechanisms (Chapter 15).

## 7.6 Intelligence Infrastructure at the Provincial Level

**Actors.** Provincial intelligence has five components: (i) the KP **Special Branch** (district-level political and security intelligence); (ii) the **CTD intelligence wing** (counter-terrorist targeting); (iii) **district police** informant networks (the most embedded and the most fragile); (iv) federal agencies (IB, ISI, MI) operating in the province; and (v) *military intelligence* via FC/army formations. Coordination occurs through: the **Provincial Apex Committee** (chaired by the Chief Minister, with the Corps Commander); district intelligence coordination committees (the Sarbakaf curfew notification explicitly referenced the district intelligence coordination committee's recommendation); and national-level mechanisms (the National Security Committee; the National Intelligence Coordination Committee established in 2022 per ISPR statements, and NACTA's coordination role).

**Fusion or fragmentation?** The evidence indicates partial fusion: IBO targeting has improved (IBO volume and precision increased markedly in 2025–26), but the *Kohat Police Lines attack* (September 2026) — a complex, multi-phase operation against a facility housing CTD and Special Branch offices — suggests either intelligence failure, force-protection failure, or both. Structural fragmentation persists: police intelligence is not systematically shared with the FC/army in real time, and vice versa, as ICG (2026) documented across 94 interviews. The provincial police's intelligence priorities (local crime, political intelligence) also compete with counter-terrorism demands.

**Recommendations preview.** A joint provincial-federal *Counter-Terrorism Fusion Centre* with legal basis (provincial legislation or a federal-provincial MoU), common databases, protective intelligence for police installations, and a clear protocol for local intelligence protection would address the documented gap. The alternative — building parallel provincial intelligence capacity to rival federal agencies — is both unaffordable and politically impossible (Chapter 15).

## 7.7 Prosecution and Judicial Challenges: Anti-Terrorism Courts and Conviction Rates

**Structure.** The Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (as amended, including 2013's post-APS reforms and later amendments) establishes Anti-Terrorism Courts (ATCs) with special procedures: trials in camera, no adjournments beyond limited periods, and the admissibility of certain confessions. KP has a limited number of ATCs relative to its caseload, plus juvenile courts acting under ATA provisions in some cases.

**Performance data.** Nationally, the NAP review literature records: **657 death sentences awarded between 2019 and 2022 with no executions in that period**; **2,273 anti-terror cases pending as of November 2024**; and recurrent concerns about acquittals due to poor investigation. In KP, the case-level record suggests: (i) successful prosecutions are possible (the October 2026 Peshawar ATC conviction of five accused, including a juvenile, for a 2024 Mohmand CTD case, with five-year sentences and fines, and the declaration of five others as absconders); (ii) the charge-to-conviction pipeline is weak (evidentiary failures, witness intimidation, forensic gaps, and the collapse of cases where digital evidence's admissibility is not properly established — a limitation explicitly noted in the Islamabad ATC judgment of August 2026, where defendants were acquitted of terrorism charges for want of proof of intent notwithstanding explosives recoveries); and (iii) appeal delays undermine deterrence.

**Structural problems.** Six are identified: (a) investigation quality — police lack forensic capacity, crime-scene protocols, and trained investigators (the KPBI's creation was meant to address this, but capacity remains thin); (b) witness protection — no functioning program, so witnesses recant or refuse; (c) judge security and case volume — ATC judges are exposed and overburdened; (d) the *alternative of military courts* — which, while producing convictions, weakens the civilian system's development, raises fair-trial questions, and removes cases from public scrutiny (with documented complaints that defendants received no charge sheets or verdict copies, litigated in the PHC in 2025); (e) *legal-aid scarcity*; and (f) *preventive detention practice* (detention without charge as a substitute for prosecution) which is both legally dubious and counterproductive.

**Post-27th Amendment environment.** The 27th Amendment's restructuring (the Federal Constitutional Court; reduced Supreme Court constitutional jurisdiction; executive dominance of the Judicial Commission) has implications for the *appeal architecture* of terrorism convictions and for judicial oversight of security agencies. Whether this strengthens or weakens counter-terrorism's legitimacy in KP is a live question; the early evidence — two Supreme Court justices resigning in protest and warning of the judiciary's subordination — suggests the change is being read by informed publics as a politicization of justice (Al Jazeera 2025b; Himal Southasian 2025).

## 7.8 Community Policing and Police–Public Trust

**Instruments.** KP has deployed a distinctive set of community-facing mechanisms: **Dispute Resolution Councils (DRCs)**, composed of local notables and retired officials, which mediate civil and criminal disputes outside the courts (KP's DRC network has been widely cited, including in international policing literature, as one of Pakistan's most successful community-policing experiments); **Public Safety Commissions** at provincial and district levels; **peace committees** in the NMDs (often encouraged and partially funded by the military); **Terrorism-Free District/Community** initiatives; and school and youth outreach.

**Performance and constraints.** The DRCs do real work — resolving thousands of disputes, reducing court backlogs, and generating goodwill. But they operate at the margins of the security problem: they cannot adjudicate terrorism, sectarian conflict, or land disputes involving armed actors. Peace committees in the NMDs have been both effective (intelligence, refusing shelter, negotiations with militants) and vulnerable (members assassinated; accused of militia abuses). Two structural constraints limit all community engagement: (i) *the coercion-first environment* — curfews, raids and collective measures undermine the trust that community policing requires; and (ii) *the trust deficit* — documented in the resentment of heavy-handed tactics (ICG 2026) and reinforced by the 2024 PTM ban and accusations of disappearances.

**Assessment.** Community policing in KP is undervalued and under-resourced relative to its potential. Comparative evidence indicates that police legitimacy is a force multiplier for intelligence and public cooperation (the primary currency of counter-terrorism). A normalization strategy should therefore treat police-community relations as a strategic capability — funded, measured and protected — rather than as public relations (Chapter 15).

## 7.9 Police Martyrdom and Morale: The Human Cost

**The numbers.** KP Police has lost **more than 2,000 personnel** since 2006 (contrasting with 369 killed between 1970 and 2006). Recent years: **186 (2023), 153 (2024), 159 (2025)**; **122 killed and 175 injured in the first half of 2026 alone (+93.7 per cent and +108.3 per cent respectively)**; attacks on police rose **48 per cent in 2025 (536 attacks) and 33.9 per cent in Jan–Sep 2026 (680 attacks)**; more than **500 police killed since 2023**. The single deadliest attack was the **30 January 2023 Peshawar Police Lines mosque suicide bombing** (over 100 killed, including ~86 police), while the **September 2026 Kohat attack** was the deadliest assault on a police complex since then. Most recent casualties have occurred in Peshawar, D.I. Khan, Bannu and Lakki Marwat (Chapter 3).

**Morale and welfare.** Documented morale factors include: compensation delays and adequacy (the provincial shuhada package has been increased repeatedly but processing is slow); the practice of posting personnel to high-risk NMD locations without family accommodation or rotational relief; equipment deficits (the night-vision and rifle claims); and the perception, widely held within the force and articulated by provincial leaders, that the burden of a national counter-insurgency is being borne by a provincial force without commensurate federal financing or operational authority.

**Strategic significance.** A force suffering a casualty rate of this magnitude faces predictable pathologies: risk aversion (static, defensive postures that concede mobility to militants); corruption (survival and welfare-driven); attrition and recruitment difficulties; and intelligence passivity. Each undermines the counter-insurgency. The 2026 escalation in police casualties is thus not merely a human tragedy; it is a *capability crisis* — and one reason the province's police-focused demands (equipment, federal reimbursement, joint command) are, in security terms, well founded.

## 7.10 Technology Integration: Safe City Projects, CCTV, Biometrics and Data Analytics

**The Peshawar Safe City Project.** Conceived in 2008 and inaugurated only on 3–5 March 2026 (after 18 years), the project cost **Rs 2.23 billion** for Phase I: **711 surveillance cameras at 133 key and sensitive locations**; an AI-enabled command-and-control centre integrating surveillance, emergency response and traffic management; facial recognition and automated alerts to patrol units; e-challan capability for traffic offences; a private wireless network; **smart surveillance vehicles, drones, anti-drone systems and 68 panic buttons**; and integration with BRT corridor, Red Zone and other camera networks. Projects in **D.I. Khan, Bannu and Lakki Marwat are nearing completion**, with rollout planned for divisional headquarters and merged districts (post-June 2026), Phase II (~Rs 5 billion) covering **Mardan, Kohat, Nowshera, Swat and Abbottabad**, and a planned extension to six southern districts (D.I. Khan, Bannu, Lakki Marwat, North Waziristan, Tank, Karak). The provincial government intends to legislate a **Safe City Authority** to regulate and integrate surveillance infrastructure.

**Other technology.** Biometric verification (NADRA-linked SIM and identity systems); vehicle registration/ANPR; the police's digital reporting systems (the KP "Digital Transformation Policy and Roadmap 2030" approved in 2026 includes cybersecurity and e-governance); CTD cyber units; and military surveillance/anti-drone capability along the border.

**Assessment.** Technology is necessary but not sufficient, for four reasons. First, *coverage*: 711 cameras in a city of ~4 million facing attacks in its periphery and rural districts cannot substitute for human intelligence and patrol capability; Peshawar itself accounts for only ~4–5 per cent of provincial incidents, while the violence is rural and southern. Second, *effectiveness evidence*: there is no published evaluation of Safe City's contribution to interdiction or deterrence. Third, *rights and oversight*: facial recognition without data-protection law, oversight mechanisms, or redress creates a substantial liberty risk that could itself become a source of grievance; and the Safe City Authority, if it concentrates surveillance in executive hands, would compound the 27th Amendment's centralization. Fourth, *protection*: the technology does not protect checkposts, patrols and static sites where police are dying. A balanced capability portfolio — counter-IED, protected mobility, night-vision, drones/counter-drones, and communications — matters more than an urban camera network for the current threat profile.

## 7.11 Comparative Analysis: KP Police versus Punjab and Sindh

**Budgets and scale.** Punjab fields the largest provincial force (with substantially more personnel than KP) and has invested heavily in urban policing technology (the Punjab Safe Cities Authority in Lahore, operational since 2016, was the first and largest such system). Sindh concentrates resources on Karachi, with a large CTD and significant federal/paramilitary support. KP's distinct position is that it spends the largest *budget share* on policing (8.7 per cent, rising to a projected 12 per cent) while facing the highest per-capita casualty rates.

**Reform trajectories.** Comparative scholarship identifies the KP Police Act 2017 as the most ambitious provincial policing statute in Pakistan (fixed IGP tenure; public safety commissions; complaint mechanisms). Punjab has retained a structure closer to the Police Order 2002 model, with reforms largely executive-driven. Sindh's reform experience has been shaped by urban political competition and by the concentration of its CTD in Karachi.

**Counter-terrorism specialization.** Punjab's CTD (established in 2017) operates in a lower-threat environment but with better infrastructure; Sindh's CTD is larger and more urban-focused; KP's CTD operates under sustained attack, with the documented deficits of §7.3. The **federal dimension** is the crucial asymmetry: Punjab and Sindh's security challenges are not framed as an interstate frontier problem, and they do not carry the burden of the frontier's refugee, closure and border-management externalities.

**Lessons.** Three comparative lessons for KP: (i) *urban technology is not a substitute for rural protection* (Punjab's Safe Cities model does not map onto KP's threat geography); (ii) *statutory reform matters most when it protects tenure, budget and oversight* — KP's Act should be defended, not diluted; and (iii) *federal fiscal rebalancing* is the only way to finance a "front-line province" burden: no provincial budget can simultaneously pay for counter-insurgency and development.

## 7.12 Recommendations for Deep Institutional Reform

Ten recommendations are advanced here and integrated into the Chapter 15 roadmap:

1. **Adopt a published Five-Year KP Police Protection and Capability Plan** with audited baselines: personnel (against sanctioned strength), protected mobility, counter-IED, night-vision, medical evacuation, and communications; published quarterly to the KP Assembly's relevant committee.
2. **Create a dedicated NMD Policing Wing** with its own doctrine, training academy (in the merged districts), recruitment-and-vetting standards, community-accountability mechanisms, and a staged transfer of primacy from FC/army formations against explicit capability milestones.
3. **Legislate and fund a Police Protection Fund** for shuhada families and injured personnel: guaranteed, timed, transparent compensation; education and employment support for dependents; and a public annual report.
4. **Reinvigorate the Public Safety Commissions** with mandated annual reports, published district performance data (attacks, casualties, complaints, convictions), and civil-society membership criteria insulated from partisan capture.
5. **Establish a Joint Counter-Terrorism Fusion Centre** (military-police-civil, with federal participation) with legal authority, common databases, protection protocols and quarterly public threat summaries.
6. **Rebuild investigation capacity**: a dedicated Counter-Terrorism Investigation Wing with forensics, crime-scene protocols, digital-evidence handling (addressing the admissibility failures documented in 2026 judgments), and a witness-protection law with funded protection units.
7. **Separate the CTD's operational and investigative functions** and professionalize both, with career paths, training exchanges, and a promotions policy tied to case outcomes rather than contacts.
8. **Fund community policing as a capability**: expand and protect DRCs; fund peace committees' welfare and security; and create a police-community liaison cadre (with female officers) in every high-threat district.
9. **Adopt a data-protection and surveillance-oversight statute** before the Safe City Authority is created, with independent audit, retention limits, redress mechanisms and transparency reporting.
10. **Negotiate a federal security-financing compact**: reimbursement of the provincial security incremental cost (a "frontier burden" transfer), tied to published capability and casualty data — converting the province's fiscal grievance into a transparent, auditable arrangement.
