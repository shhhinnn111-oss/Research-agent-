# CHAPTER 3 — MAPPING THE THREAT LANDSCAPE

## 3.1 Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP): Structure, Ideology, Strategy and Current Operational Status

**Genesis and evolution.** The TTP was formed in December 2007 as an umbrella of Pakistani militant factions under Baitullah Mehsud (killed 2009), succeeded by Hakimullah Mehsud (killed 2013), Maulana Fazlullah (killed 2018) and—since 2018—Noor Wali Mehsud. Its stated objective is the establishment of an Islamic system in Pakistan through armed struggle and the expulsion of what it characterizes as foreign domination of the Pakistani state (Yusufzai 2019; SATP 2026c). Across four leadership eras, the organisation's operating doctrine has evolved from the Mehsud-era emphasis on suicide bombing and mass-casualty urban attacks to the Fazlullah period's mix of guerrilla warfare and sectarian targeting, to the Noor Wali era's deliberate "insurgency doctrine" — territorial infiltration, parallel governance (taxes, courts, checkpoints), targeting of security forces, and selective use of spectacular attacks to shape national politics (Basit 2024; EUAA 2026).

**Organizational structure and coalition dynamics.** The EUAA's June 2026 country assessment concluded that the TTP has functioned since 2018 as an *umbrella coalition* rather than a monolithic organisation, incorporating more than seventy factions, including returning Afghan Taliban-aligned factions, Pakistani al-Qaeda affiliates, and groups from Pashtun- and Baloch-majority areas (EUAA 2026). This distinguishes the TTP from ISKP. In practice the umbrella has three tiers: (i) *core* Mehsud-faction structures in South and North Waziristan, with a leadership shura and a media apparatus (Umar Media/Umar Studio); (ii) *affiliated factions* with local autonomy, notably the **Hafiz Gul Bahadur Group (HGB)** in North Waziristan (previously a state-aligned "good Taliban" militia until 2014, now a leading anti-state actor), the **Ittehad-ul-Mujahideen Pakistan (IMP)** — implicated in the Kohat Police Lines attack of 18 September 2026 — **Jamaat-ul-Ahrar (JuA)**, which announced a formal split from the TTP in July 2026, **Lashkar-e-Islam** in Khyber, and various Mohmand, Bajaur, Orakzai, Kurram and Swat-based factions; and (iii) *franchise/network* relationships with facilitators, sleeper cells and financial conduits (TRT 2026; Wikipedia 2026d; NSBRRI 2026).

**Strength.** Strength estimates vary widely and should be treated as order-of-magnitude: The Diplomat reported estimates in February 2026 of approximately 3,000 ISKP fighters, 5,000–7,000 TTP, and 400–1,500 al-Qaeda personnel in Afghanistan (The Diplomat 2026a). Former KP Chief Minister Ali Amin Gandapur claimed in December 2024 that 22,000–24,000 TTP members were in Afghanistan with 16,000–18,000 fighters inside Pakistan (Eurasia Review 2026a) — a figure that should be read as a political statement about a threat rather than a census. Officials cited by Geo News in September 2025 estimated "over 8,000 TTP terrorists present in KP, setting up checkpoints" (ACLED 2025). What is not in dispute: the TTP has regenerated the *capacity* to mount company-sized attacks on static positions (the 26 October 2025 Haidar Kandao camp attack in Tirah, Orakzai — 21 security personnel killed per GTI 2026), sustained attack rates exceeding 300 claimed operations monthly in 2026, and a geographically widening footprint.

**Ideology and strategy.** The TTP's ideology is Deobandi-jihadist in lineage but programmatically Pakistani-national: its war aims combine (a) forced renegotiation of Pakistan's relationship with the United States and its allies; (b) imposition of Sharia; (c) revenge for operations and detentions (a theme it has elevated into an explicit demand for the release of prisoners and reversal of the 2018 merger as recompense for fatalities); and (d) the deterritorialization of the tribal belt from the Pakistani state's structures (Basit 2024; SATP 2026c). Strategically, since 2022 the TTP has pursued a *dual-track* campaign: insurgency in the border districts and settled south (parallel taxation, "courts", road tolls, night letters, targeted killings of officials, police and anti-militant notables); and terror in urban centres to demonstrate reach and extract political costs (Peshawar, Islamabad periphery, Karachi — where a trained suicide bomber was arrested in June 2026). On 4 March 2026 the group announced its annual spring offensive under the name *Operation Khyber* (Ghazwa-e-Khyber), following the conclusion of its *Al-Khandaq* campaign, directing attacks at military outposts, police, and security-force personnel (Eurasia Review 2026a).

**Capabilities and tactical repertoire.** TTP attacks in 2025–2026 have included: vehicle-borne IEDs (Lakki Marwat, May 2026: 9 killed, 33 injured; Kohat, September 2026); suicide bombings (Swat/Kabal, August 2026: 17 killed); coordinated small-arms assaults on checkposts, police stations and military camps; sniper and "laser" attacks; ambushes of convoys; targeted assassinations (including of police, tribal elders, peace-committee members, polio workers, and civil officials); kidnapping for ransom and hostage-taking; bank robbery (PKR 80 million in one 2026 incident); and — the most consequential new capability — drone warfare. KP police reported foiling 341 drone attacks in the first half of 2026, with at least 100 attacks in the province, 29 security personnel killed and 89 injured; at least 13 of KP's 40 districts have experienced drone attacks, and targets have shifted from border convoys to urban police installations, including coordinated quadcopter attacks on three police facilities in Karak on 14 August 2026 (GNET 2026). Pakistan has responded with anti-drone systems, nets and electronic countermeasures.

**Finance.** TTP financing combines: extortion and "taxation" in areas of influence (documented in Swat in 2025 and in the NMDs); kidnapping for ransom; bank robberies and vehicle thefts; Zakat/ushr collection under coercion; donations through a diaspora and jihadi fundraising networks; and rent extraction from minerals, timber, and construction (Chapters 4, 11). Counter-financing under the NAP and FATF-related reforms degraded formal channels (thousands of accounts frozen; charities proscribed), but the group's shift to cash-intensive, locally-taxed underground economies has offset much of that gain.

**Assessment.** The TTP is best understood today as an *insurgent coalition with a sanctuary, an increasingly capable technological appetite, and a political-strategic logic of attrition* rather than as a purely terrorist network. Its ability to impose parallel governance — reported checkpoints in parts of the NMDs and south, and coercive taxation — is the most troubling indicator, because parallel governance is precisely what distinguishes insurgency from terrorism in the KP case. The 2026 countervailing factor is internal competition: the JuA split and TTP–ISKP clashes (Orakzai, March 2026) suggest that the umbrella's growth has costs, and the state's counter-terrorism strategy may be able to exploit fissures it has not yet systematically targeted.

## 3.2 ISIS-Khorasan Province (ISKP): Presence, Activities and Threat Level in KPK

**Presence and strength.** ISKP, the Islamic State's "Khorasan Province," established its presence in Afghanistan's eastern provinces (Nangarhar, Kunar) from 2014–15 and, after its territorial defeat in Afghanistan, has operated as a decentralized network. Estimates of its strength in the Afghanistan–Pakistan space hover around 2,000–4,000 fighters, with 3,000 a commonly cited figure (The Diplomat 2026a). In Pakistan, ISKP operates through cells — analyst Abdul Sayed's tally found ISKP claimed 19 attacks in Pakistan in 2021 (against the TTP's 257), but the pattern has shifted since: ISKP's campaign has been small in volume, high in lethality, and concentrated in KP. In 2023, ISKP claimed 26 attacks in Pakistan, *all* in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (18 in Bajaur, 7 in Peshawar, 1 in Hangu), causing hundreds of casualties (Wikipedia 2026c). In 2025, ISKP's most significant KP attack was the suicide bombing at the Darul Uloom Haqqania in Akora Khattak, Nowshera (28 February 2025), which killed at least eight people including the seminary's head, Maulana Hamid-ul-Haq Haqqani.

**The 2026 escalation.** The most consequential ISKP operation of the current phase was the 6 February 2026 suicide bombing at a Shia mosque/imambargah in Islamabad, which killed 31 worshippers and injured dozens more. Pakistan attributed the attack to ISKP and used it — alongside a security-convoy attack in Bannu and Ramadan-period attacks in Bajaur and Bannu — to justify the 22 February 2026 airstrikes inside Afghanistan against alleged TTP and ISKP camps, which in turn escalated into Operation Ghazab-lil-Haq (The Diplomat 2026a; Karmactive 2026; Wikipedia 2026a). ISKP activity in KP continued through 2026, including fighting with TTP factions (the killing of an ISKP commander and three associates in Orakzai's Ghaljo Narai area on 4 March 2026), targeted attacks on Pakistani intelligence personnel and peace-committee members, and attacks on Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam figures (SATP 2026a; Daniel Garofalo Monitoring 2026). One monitoring service observed that in August 2026 *all six* officially recorded Islamic State attacks worldwide occurred in KP — a striking concentration that analysts attribute to the group's regional contraction into the Pakistan–Afghanistan ecosystem.

**Target profile and rivalry.** ISKP's targeting logic differs from the TTP's in three ways. First, it deliberately selects *soft and sectarian targets* — Shia congregations, Sufi shrines, minority communities, and rival jihadists — where the TTP has increasingly restricted its attacks to security forces and state affiliates (Sayed, cited in RFE/RL). Second, ISKP attacks serve a *competition function*: demonstrating that the TTP is insufficiently "pure." Third, ISKP's anti-Shia campaign intersects with local sectarian entrepreneurs — an overlap that complicates attribution in Kurram, where the most lethal incidents since 2024 have involved both sectarian militias and jihadist franchises.

**Assessment.** ISKP is the most lethal *per attack* actor in KP, and its presence in Afghanistan makes it, like the TTP, difficult to eliminate from Pakistani territory alone. But its limited manpower, its hostility to the Afghan Taliban and the TTP alike, and its small attack volume mean it is best understood as a *strategic spoiler* — a group whose capacity to trigger interstate crises (as in February 2026) far exceeds its military weight. Policy that treats ISKP and the TTP as a single phenomenon risks both analytical error and strategic overreach; policy that ignores their competition misses an exploitable fault line.

## 3.3 Sectarian Militant Organizations: Lashkar-e-Jhangvi, Sipah-e-Sahaba and Others

**Organizational landscape.** Sectarian militancy in Pakistan's Deobandi–Shia conflict axis was institutionalized in the 1980s–1990s: Sipah-e-Sahaba Pakistan (SSP, 1985) as a political-religious movement; Lashkar-e-Jhangvi (LeJ, 1996) as its armed vanguard; and a host of allied and splinter formations (Lashkar-e-Jhangvi Al-Alami, the anti-Shia networks in the Hazara belt, and the militant wings of Deobandi madrasa networks). Both SSP and LeJ were formally proscribed between 2002 and 2004; both persisted as networks and as franchises within the TTP and ISKP. LeJ's historical signature was targeted assassination of Shia professionals — doctors, lawyers, businessmen, and officials — and mass-casualty attacks on Shia gatherings; its factional descendants have been implicated in attacks on the Hazara community, including the 2013 Quetta and 2019–2021 Hazara killings, and in the wave of attacks on Parachinar since 2005 (Chapter 2).

**The Kurram conflict as the contemporary sectarian frontline.** Kurram district — with the Shia-majority town of Parachinar and a sectarian geography driven by land disputes, tribal alignments and cross-border links to Khost, Paktia and Nangarhar — has been the most lethal sectarian battlefield in Pakistan in recent years. The July 2024 land-dispute clashes between the Maleekhel (Shia-majority) and Madgi Kalay (Sunni-majority) tribes killed ~46–50 people; the 21 November 2024 attack on two Shia convoys (40+ vehicles under police escort) killed at least 40–43 people including women and children, triggering retaliatory clashes that in total killed between 97 (provincial figure) and 133 (independent counts) in weeks, and 200+ over 2024 (Al Jazeera 2024; The Diplomat 2024; Independent 2024; Directus, cited in Social News 2026). Following three weeks of jirga negotiations in Kohat, a 14-point agreement was signed on 1–2 January 2025: permanent ceasefire; surrender of heavy weapons; demolition of bunkers; a ban on private armed displays; a Rs 10 million collective fine for violations; the barring of proscribed sectarian organisations from opening offices; and commitments regarding the safety of government employees and the protection of women and non-combatants (Dawn 2025a; The Nation 2025a). A further one-year *Aman Teega* (peace pact) between Bushahra, Dandar, Taida, Malikhel, Ahmadzai, Mirbakhil, Shakh Daulat and related tribes was signed at a grand jirga in Parachinar in May 2025, with Army and administration representation (Tribune 2025c).

Despite these accords, the Thal–Parachinar Road — the district's lifeline — remained subject to prolonged closure, and by early 2026 a three-month closure produced what analysts described as siege conditions: shortages of food, fuel, medicine and emergency care; an estimated 200+ cumulative deaths; and the displacement of border-area populations (Directus, cited in Social News 2026). Kurram remained one of the four districts accounting for 63 per cent of all KP violence incidents in the second quarter of 2026 (CRSS 2026b).

**The Shia militia dimension and external actors.** Because sectarian violence is reciprocal, armed Shia mobilizations matter analytically. Reports have attributed elements of the November 2024 retaliation to fighters associated with the Zainebiyoun Brigade, a Pakistani Shia militia historically recruited to fight in Syria (The Diplomat 2024). Sectarian conflict in Kurram thus includes an external patronage dimension for both sides: Iranian-linked networks on one side; Deobandi-jihadist and possibly ISKP networks on the other. This is the clearest example in KP of the regional proxy dimension operating at the sub-district level.

**Assessment.** Sectarian militancy in KP has four features that make it structurally dangerous. First, it is *self-reproducing*: each attack spawns vendettas, displacement, and arming. Second, it is *territorially specific*, which concentrates harm (the Shia of Parachinar; the Hazara and Shia of Hangu/Kohat; Ahmadi and Christian minorities in urban centres). Third, it is *networked across conflicts*: LeJ factions, ISKP, and TTP units trade personnel and expertise. Fourth, *legal remedies have failed*: the 14-point accord and Aman Teega are necessary but not sufficient; their enforcement depends on the police and courts that the district lacks. Kurram is where the argument for sequenced state-building — justice before sustained force; infrastructure before elections — is most clearly demonstrated.

## 3.4 Criminal Networks: Extortion, Kidnapping, Narcotics and Arms Trafficking

**Extortion and racketeering.** Extortion is the TTP's most accessible revenue stream and a leading source of civilian grievance. Documented patterns include: (a) night letters and telephone demands to businesses, landowners and contractors across the NMDs, Swat, Dir, Bajaur and settled districts (AIP 2026; NSBRRI 2026); (b) the "protection tax" collected from markets, transport, and mining and construction sites; (c) coercion of teachers, health workers and government staff; and (d) demands on the families of serving police and military personnel (a pattern that both generates revenue and forces cooperation). In 2025, Swat residents reported systematic extortion calls preceding the 2026 attack cycle; the presence of an extortion economy is now widely treated as the leading indicator of resurgence in a district.

**Kidnapping for ransom (KFR) and abductions.** KFR has re-emerged as a significant practice, often in hybrid criminal-militant form. Notable cases include the abduction of officials of the Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission from a mining project (per GTI 2026), multiple police and civilian abductions in the NMDs (including the reported capture of 196 security personnel per TTP's own 2025 claims), and the capture of security personnel in Swat and Bannu. KFR cases against traders and prominent families in the NMDs and southern districts provide liquidity and create communal fear; police data indicate that kidnappings, like all violent crime, have risen alongside militancy.

**Narcotics.** Pakistan's role as a transit corridor for Afghan opiates was historically concentrated in FATA, where poppy cultivation itself existed in parts of the tribal belt until the 2000s; since then, poppy cultivation has collapsed in Pakistan while transit trade and precursor trafficking have expanded with Afghanistan's poppy economy (which after the 2022 Taliban-imposed ban declined substantially, only to be partially replaced, per UNODC reporting, by methamphetamine and synthetic production). For KP, the relevant harms are: (a) transit corridors through Khyber, Kurram, Bajaur, Mohmand and Balochistan links; (b) local consumption (heroin and methamphetamine use, increasingly among youth and long-term IDPs); (c) financing for armed groups through "taxation" of trafficking; and (d) corruption of law enforcement at points of passage. Official statistics show rising anti-narcotics enforcement in KP in 2025–26, but the structural conditions are unchanged.

**Arms trafficking and the weaponization of the border.** The most consequential material supply shock of the current phase has been the availability of weapons — including NATO-pattern small arms, night-vision equipment, precision rifles and drone components — that entered Afghanistan after August 2021. Pakistani officials (including the ISPR's Director-General, Lieutenant-General Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry) have asserted that more than USD 7 billion of military equipment was abandoned in Afghanistan and that captured US-made weapons have been recovered in operations against the TTP (Eurasia Review 2026a). Independent verification is limited, but the presence of modern optics, night-fighting equipment and anti-personnel mines in TTP operations is documented in seizure reports and prosecutions (for example, the November 2024 Mohmand case in which five anti-personnel mines, three grenades and small arms were seized from a TTP cell, per the Peshawar ATC judgment of October 2026). Arms are further diffused through smuggling routes into Punjab and Sindh for criminal markets, and through the same routes used for narcotics and contraband.

**The political economy of smuggling and "undocumented trade."** The Afghanistan–Pakistan frontier hosts an enormous informal economy: fuel smuggling (in which the decanting of Iranian and Afghan petroleum products distorts provincial markets), the transit of goods under the cross-border "Transit Trade Agreement" framework (long used to launder dutiable cargo), currency exchange (the Kabul/Hawala networks), and the smuggling of electronic goods, tyres, vehicles and livestock. Official estimates of the illegal economy vary; the KP government's own assessments and federal enforcement campaigns (including 2025–26 crackdowns on the Landi Kotal/Torkham smuggling networks and the creation of anti-smuggling task forces) suggest the scale is in the tens of billions of rupees annually. The security significance is twofold: smuggling provides revenue and logistics to armed groups; and crackdowns damage the livelihoods of border communities whose economic survival depends on the same routes — a trade-off that policy has not resolved and that recurrently features in local grievances (Chapter 4).

**Assessment.** Criminal and militant economies in KP are *convergent but not identical*. The state's interest in suppressing militancy and in suppressing smuggling sometimes align (both fund the TTP) and sometimes conflict (smuggling suppression alienates border communities; corruption networks within the state protect them). A serious normalization strategy must therefore treat the border economy explicitly: neither pure suppression (which has failed repeatedly and generated grievances) nor tolerance (which finances insurgency), but a regulated, tariff-integrated, employment-substituting transition.

## 3.5 Cross-Border Militant Movements and Afghan-Origin Threats

**The border's physical and political geography.** The Pakistan–Afghanistan boundary (2,611–2,640 km by varying counts) includes approximately 1,000+ km facing KP and the NMDs. Since 2017, Pakistan has fenced the border (largely completing the KP-facing portion by 2021–2023) and established crossing points and terminals (Torkham, Ghulam Khan, Angoor Adda), while Afghanistan has consistently refused to recognize the boundary. The fence has changed the character of infiltration — from open cross-border movement to a mix of covert crossing, smuggling tunnels, and firing over the fence — but it has not stopped it: infiltration of armed groups remains documented, including at Datta Khel (North Waziristan) and Kurram's Arandu and Nari sectors, and the Pakistani military reported foiling multiple attempts in 2026 (Wikipedia 2026a).

**Sanctuary and rear basing.** The 2026 Pakistan–Afghanistan conflict made explicit what analysts had argued since 2022: TTP fighters and commanders operate from Afghan territory (Kunar, Nangarhar, Khost, Paktika, Paktia, Logar and beyond), with reported camps and infrastructure; Pakistan's airstrikes beginning February 2026 targeted alleged TTP and ISKP camps and facilities in Kabul, Kandahar, Paktia, Khost, Nangarhar, Kunar and other provinces (The Diplomat 2026a; Wikipedia 2026a). The UN Security Council's Analytical Support and Sanctions Monitoring Team's 37th report (February 2026) reportedly found an increase in TTP attacks on Pakistan from Afghanistan (Eurasia Review 2026a). Estimates of the TTP's presence in Afghanistan (thousands of fighters; the 2024 claim of 22,000–24,000 is the upper bound of political estimates) indicate the scale of the sanctuary problem.

**The cross-border population and mobility regime.** The border zone's population includes: (a) resident tribal communities on both sides of the line who have kinship across it; (b) approximately 1.5–3 million Afghans historically resident in Pakistan, with the largest concentrations in KP; (c) traders and labourers dependent on legal and informal crossings; (d) documented and undocumented Afghan refugees — including, among them, persons of security concern. Since 2023, Pakistan's Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan has driven both mass inward pressure on Afghanistan (146,000+ deported in 2026 alone; the KP government's phased plan for ~20,000 in Peshawar city; camp closures in Bannu) and a hardening of border policy that in July 2026 moved to arrest without a valid visa (HRW 2026; Dawn 2026e; Tribune 2026). The security implications are double-edged: the policy plausibly reduces the pool in which militants hide, but it also creates a coercive environment in which "militant" and "Afghan" are conflated; it degrades inter-communal trust; and it generates its own radicalization pressures (Chapter 9).

**Assessment.** The cross-border threat is best modelled as a *stack of four channels*: (1) sanctuary (safe areas for leadership and training); (2) infiltration and exfiltration (movement of fighters and matériel); (3) supply (weapons, munitions, narcotics, drone components); and (4) the population channel (refugees, returnees, and mixed migration). A normalization strategy should specify which channel it is attempting to close, with what instrument (military, border management, diplomacy, refugee policy), at what cost to the border economy and to bilateral relations. Most past policy has conflated the four — with the result that actions against the population channel have been substituted for progress on the sanctuary channel, the channel that matters most to the insurgency's persistence.

## 3.6 Lone-Wolf Attacks and Self-Radicalized Actors

**Evidence and pattern.** Pakistani counter-terrorism practice has become increasingly attentive to small-cell and self-radicalized attackers. Prosecution records from 2025–2026 illustrate the profile: in the ATC Islamabad case of August 2026, three men from Malakand, the former Khyber Agency and Karak were convicted of possessing TNT/nitroglycerine explosives, a firearm, TTP flags and propaganda, along with hand-drawn maps of sensitive installations and forged police identity cards — but were acquitted on terrorism charges for want of proof of intent (The Nation 2026a). Other examples include the 2025–26 cases of suspects trained via online networks and directed by handlers abroad; the police arrests of trained suicide bombers (for example, one caught in Karachi in June 2026 after CTD intelligence developed through cyber-monitoring); and the recurring discovery of IED-making materials in urban homes.

**Why the category matters.** Lone-wolf and small-cell attacks are the hardest to disrupt through area operations: they do not require camps, sanctuaries or large logistics. They require inspiration, technical instruction (now available online), weapons precursors (obtainable through criminal markets), and a target of opportunity. Their rise is a direct function of two KP realities: the proliferation of radicalizing content online, and the survival of a "reserve" of ideologically motivated individuals across generations of conflict.

**Assessment.** Pakistan's counter-measures have focused on targeting and surveillance (cyber units, forensics, CTD) and, residually, on deradicalization. The evidence suggests that the growth of this category is outpacing both. The policy gap is preventative: schools, mental health services, employment, and community-level referral mechanisms are the instruments with the best track record in comparable settings and the weakest presence in KP's toolkit (Chapters 8, 15).

## 3.7 Cyber Radicalization and Online Recruitment

**The ecosystem.** Online radicalization in KP operates across three layers: (1) *propaganda production* in Pashto, Urdu and Arabic — TTP media (Umar Media/Studio), ISKP's multilingual units (al-Azaim Foundation and successors), and allied channels distributing battlefield footage, sermons, and "nasheed" content; (2) *distribution and network maintenance* via encrypted platforms (Telegram, WhatsApp, Signal, and invite-only forums), and via normal social media with evasive techniques; and (3) *recruitment funnels* converting consumers into facilitators — technical guidance (IED construction, drone modification), travel facilitation, and money movement.

**Pakistani counter-measures.** Under the NAP's provisions against terrorist media, Pakistani authorities blocked over 1.3 million URLs for incitement, extremism and hate speech by 2023 (Pakistan Connect 2025). PECA (Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act, 2016, amended 2025) provides a legal basis for prosecutions of online incitement; the KP CTD operates cyber investigation capacity; and the federal government has repeatedly suspended or restricted platforms during crises. Global platform policies and takedowns have removed major channels (including ISKP's main outlets), but the ecosystem regenerates quickly through mirroring and new platforms.

**Assessment.** Two problems dominate. First, *measurement*: no publicly available, reliable KP-specific baseline for online radicalization exists; blocked-URL counts are output measures, not outcome measures. Second, *legal and rights calibration*: the same legal instruments (PECA, ATA provisions, and the proposed/expanded cybersecurity framework) are used against both genuinely inciting content and legitimate political speech, including that of journalists and activists. This weakens the credibility of counter-radicalization with the very audiences whose trust matters. A defensible policy approach would separate (a) incitement to violence — regulated through transparent, reviewed takedown processes with appeal rights — from (b) dissent, and would invest in Pashto- and Urdu-language counter-narratives produced and promoted by credible local voices rather than state-aligned outlets (Chapter 8).

## 3.8 Threat Assessment Matrix: District-by-District Analysis of KPK

**Method note.** District-level classification below draws on incident and fatality data from SATP, CRSS, ACLED and EUAA district analyses, cross-checked against attitudinal evidence. Two cautions apply: (i) official district data are incomplete for 2026 (KP police report 680 attacks for Jan–Sept 2026 across the province; the district distribution of these is not fully public); and (ii) *attacks thwarted* are not uniformly reported, so a quiet district may reflect police success rather than absence of threat. The classification is therefore a synthesis with confidence levels noted.

**Patterns.** Three structural findings emerge from the district record. First, **the geography of violence has shifted south and east**: whereas the 2008–2015 violence was concentrated in Swat/Malakand and the northern tribal agencies (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber), by 2023–2026 the "new" epicentre is the southern belt — North and South Waziristan, Bannu, Lakki Marwat, Kurram, Hangu, Tank, Kohat, Karak and Dera Ismail Khan — with 63 per cent of all KP incidents in Q2 2026 occurring in just Bannu, Lakki Marwat, Kurram and Bajaur (CRSS 2026b). Second, **the districts with the highest violence are not necessarily those with the most militancy, but those with the most *unresolved governance* issues**: the NMDs and Kurram combine post-merger institutional voids with sectarian and land conflicts. Third, **peripheral districts with no Afghan border (Swat 2026; Kohat 2026; Karak 2026) are being drawn back into violence** through a combination of TTP expansion, factional competition (post-split JuA activity), and demonstrations of reach — a warning that cordon-based containment strategies will be outpaced.

## 3.9 Emerging and Non-Traditional Security Threats

**Climate security.** KP is among Pakistan's most climate-exposed regions: the 2022 floods (in which KP was the hardest-hit province), the 2025 monsoon (504 KP deaths, 1.57 million people affected, ~100,000 displaced, Buner and Swat among the worst-hit districts), and a 2026 monsoon season forecast at 22–26 per cent above the previous season's rainfall, with accelerated glacial melt across Pakistan's 7,000+ glaciers raising glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) risk (UNICEF 2025; WHO 2025; Watchers 2026). Climate hazards interact with security in four ways: (a) disaster-driven displacement creates recruitment and trafficking vulnerability; (b) relief distribution becomes a site of political contestation and capture; (c) the destruction of schools, roads and health facilities erodes state presence precisely where normalization depends on it; and (d) water scarcity — in the Peshawar valley's irrigation systems, in Chitral's mountain streams, and along the Kabul River basin — creates conflict potential over entitlements (Chapter 10).

**Water and resource conflict.** Beyond climate, KP faces disputes over: canal water and entitlements (notably the Chashma Right Bank Canal project's implications); mining rights in the NMDs, where mineral concessions (in marble, chromite, copper and coal) have generated local contestation and, in some areas, protection rackets; and land disputes — the most common trigger of the Kurram conflict and recurrent in the merged districts and Hazara. Land conflicts are especially important analytically because they convert *governance failures* into *sectarian and tribal violence*.

**Pandemic-related instability.** COVID-19 (2020–21) simultaneously (a) disrupted schooling (learning loss concentrated among poor and rural students), (b) reduced household income, and (c) weakened border and regulatory enforcement. Its aftermath contributes to the present youth crisis. In a different register, the failure to defeat polio — despite a decades-long campaign— remains a security barometer: attacks on vaccination teams, the killing of police escorts, and community resistance rooted in distrust and conspiracy narratives directly complicate health-security cooperation.

**Digital and financial-system threats.** State-facilitated biometric and surveillance systems create new vulnerabilities (data protection is weak; surveillance capability may be repurposed against political actors); hawala and crypto-facilitated financing remain hard to monitor; and the coming deployment of AI-based facial recognition in the Safe City project raises questions of oversight and bias (Chapter 7).

**Assessment.** Non-traditional threats in KP are not side-shows; they are *multipliers* of the same grievances that sustain militancy, and they are the issues on which the state's legitimacy is most visibly tested. A normalization strategy that cannot protect flood victims, deliver vaccination, and adjudicate land cannot win the trust that counter-insurgency requires.
