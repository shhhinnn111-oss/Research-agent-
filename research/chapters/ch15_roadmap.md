# CHAPTER 15 — A PHASED ROADMAP FOR NORMALIZATION: IMPLEMENTATION, MONITORING AND FINANCING

## 15.1 Purpose, Design Principles and the Logic of Sequencing

**Purpose.** This chapter converts the study's findings into an implementable agenda. Its objective is not to end all violence — no plan can — but to move the province from Scenario 2 (fragile equilibrium) toward Scenario 1 (sustained normalization) and to make a transition to Scenario 3 (deterioration) less likely. The roadmap is deliberately long-horizon: 15 years, in three phases (0–2, 2–5, 5–15 years), because the evidence in Chapters 13–14 shows that normalization is generational, not annual.

**Design principles.**
1. **Security and legitimacy together.** Operations that kill militants without building the institutions that replace them (courts, police, local government) reproduce the cycle documented in Chapter 6. Every security measure should have an accompanying legitimacy measure.
2. **The state's own commitments are the binding constraint.** The clearest lesson of Chapters 5, 11 and 14: the largest obstacles to normalization are not TTP's fighting power but the failures of fiscal delivery, police protection and lawful political space — all of which the state controls.
3. **Do no harm to the fiscal settlement.** The merged districts' Rs 568 billion shortfall is not a technicality; it is the material foundation of the state's claim to have annexed the tribal areas into the nation. It must be fixed before anything else is credible.
4. **Subsidiarity and local legitimacy.** Every function that can be performed at district or tehsil level should be, with resources attached. Normalization delivered from Peshawar and Islamabad has a lower ceiling.
5. **Evidence, transparency and independent verification.** The province's security statistic is contested; the roadmap therefore includes a monitoring architecture with published data — the most conspicuous gap in the current system (Chapter 11).
6. **Reversibility and no-amnesty boundaries.** Reintegration and negotiation (where possible) should not expand to impunity for mass-casualty atrocities; the state's credibility with victims depends on this line.
7. **Identify, don't demonize, communities.** Pashtuns are the principal victims of the insurgency; policy must target networks and behaviours, not identity (Chapter 4).
8. **Reconcile the scenarios.** Chapter 9's five geopolitical scenarios (de-escalation, managed confrontation, escalatory collapse, India–Pakistan crisis, Taliban instability) describe the *external* environment; Chapter 14's three province-level scenarios describe the *internal* outcomes. They are nested, not competing: the roadmap must be robust across the four non-catastrophic geopolitical cases and hedging against the fifth (see §15.11).

**The three deficits as the roadmap's anchor.** Chapter 11 established that KP's policy implementation fails through three deficits: **fiscal** (the resources never arrive), **accountability** (no one monitors or answers for outcomes), and **legitimacy** (the population does not own the settlement). Each phase below is scored against all three, and §15.9 makes them the core of the monitoring framework.

## 15.2 What "Normalized" Means: A Measurable End-State

Normalization is defined here by eight measurable conditions, not by the absence of attacks:

| # | Condition | 2035 target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Violence at criminal, not insurgent, levels | ≤ 400 conflict fatalities/year province-wide |
| 2 | State monopoly of violence restored in the NMDs | No district with sustained TTP taxation or parallel courts for 4 consecutive quarters |
| 3 | Police able to protect themselves and the public | ≤ 50 police killed per year; ≥ 80% of attacks thwarted or survived without loss |
| 4 | Justice delivered where the conflict was fought | ≥ 75% of ATC cases concluded within 24 months; functioning district courts in all seven NMDs |
| 5 | Fiscal settlement delivered | 3% (or legislated equivalent) transferred in full, with independent audit, every year |
| 6 | Local government functioning | Elected councils in all NMDs with own-source revenue and budget authority |
| 7 | Economy recovering inclusively | Merged-district poverty within 10 points of the provincial average; ≥ 15% growth in non-farm employment |
| 8 | Legitimate political space | No proscription of non-violent parties; protest and media functioning without criminalisation |

## 15.3 Phase I (0–2 Years): Stabilisation, Fiscal Rescue and the Protection Pivot

### A. The merged-district fiscal rescue (immediate, highest priority)
1. **Legislate the transfer.** Secure the promised 3 per cent NFC share (or a quantified equivalent) through: (a) conclusion of the current NFC award; failing that (b) a presidential order under Article 160; failing that (c) a statutory federal grant with a minimum floor — so that the commitment survives an award cycle. *Responsible:* Federal Ministry of Finance, NFC, Prime Minister's Office; *with:* KP Finance Department; *by:* budget cycle 2027–28 at latest, before the 2028 expiry of the original commitment. *Verification:* independent annual audit published by the Auditor-General with a standing committee review (Chapter 5 design requirements).
2. **Clear all arrears within 12 months.** Pay the outstanding Rs 568 billion on a published schedule with quarterly milestones; publish district-wise releases monthly (currently unavailable).
3. **Protect the money from capture.** Ring-fence annual development program allocations for the NMDs; require community monitoring committees in each district; publish beneficiary lists for compensation payments (Tirah, Sarbakaf, Kurram and operation-affected); and refer documented diversion to the NAB/ACE with public reporting.
4. **Pay compensation and rebuild destroyed property.** Complete Tirah compensation, rebuild or compensate the destroyed homes, mosques and schools of the 2024–2026 operations and airstrikes; treat this as a *security expenditure*, not welfare.
5. **Reopen and fund the service pipeline.** Restore suspended education and health projects; rehire teachers/personnel in the merged districts; restart the halted road connections (including the Torkham–Jalalabad corridor's trade infrastructure) under protection.

### B. The police protection pivot (immediate)
6. **Emergency protection package.** Protected mobility (mine-protected vehicles), counter-IED equipment and training, counter-drone systems for static posts, ballistic protection and helmets, secure communications, and drone/surveillance support to district police — deployed first to Kohat, Lakki Marwat, Bannu, D.I. Khan, Tank, Kurram, Khyber and North/South Waziristan (where casualty rates are highest). *Responsible:* KP Police, Interior/Home, with federal supplementary grant.
7. **Insurance, compensation and family support.** Complete the insurance coverage promised for police (the Inspector-General's costed plan) — a retention prerequisite; raise the martyrdom package to parity with other forces; and institutionalize family support (education, pensions, job quotas).
8. **Attack attribution and learning.** Mandatory post-incident reviews after every police fatality in an attack, published in aggregate quarterly with actionable findings (a measure the current system does not have).
9. **Halt the free fall in police strength.** Expedited recruitment (including 6,000+ new posts from the July 2026 package), with provincial recruitment quotas for the merged districts; competitive pay adjustments; and rehabilitation posts for wounded personnel.

### C. The border and cross-border channel (immediate)
10. **Consolidate the mediated ceasefire into a verification mechanism.** Build on the Qatar/Türkiye/China facilitation of 2025–2026 to secure a defined set of commitments — no cross-border attacks, no sanctuary support, prisoner exchanges, and trade corridors kept open — with an independent monitoring presence (UN, OIC, or a China-facilitated joint commission) and a quarterly published status report. *Responsible:* Foreign Office (MOFA), with Security Divisions; *realistic aim:* manage, not solve.
11. **Border regime.** Complete fencing/fortification, formalize the Torkham/Chaman/Angoor Ada trade and mobility regime (documented crossers, trade facilitation, and reciprocal arrangements for families), and fund border-district police and Levies in step with any restriction regime (otherwise restriction becomes abuse — Chapter 5's border-management findings).
12. **Refugee policy with dignity.** Complete the Afghan-refugee registration/repatriation processes lawfully: individual, documented, appealable procedures; no mass refoulement; and a joint UNHCR–government verification of any voluntary-return program — because abuse here feeds the insurgency's narrative (Chapters 9, 11).

### D. Civic space and legitimacy (immediate, low cost, high return)
13. **Lift the PTM proscription and release detained activists** lacking terrorism charges; open a lawful channel for Pashtun political demands (see Ch. 4, 12 analysis). *This single measure costs no money and removes the strongest single driver of ethnic-grievance mobilization.*
14. **End indefinite detention without charge.** Publish the number of detainees held under security laws; move all cases to charging or release within statutory periods; and create a statutory juvenile track (Chapter 8 recommendation).
15. **Restore the fourth estate.** Resolve the criminalisation of journalistic expression; implement a mechanism for investigating attacks on journalists; and permit independent casualty and displacement reporting — contested facts are better than suppressed ones (Chapter 11's accountability deficit).

### E. Intelligence and operations (immediate)
16. **Intelligence-led targeting with discrimination.** Continue IBOs and high-value operations with tightened collateral-damage safeguards, documented post-strike assessments, rapid compensation where civilian harm occurred, and no operation without a designated post-operation stabilization component (Chapters 6, 9). The operational record shows that kinetic success without stabilization produces recurrence.
17. **Counter-financing.** Strike TTP's revenue (extortion, taxation, mining, smuggling) with financial-intelligence-led action coordinated between the CTD, FIA, Customs and provincial mining authorities; publish aggregate seizure data (de-risking the state from its biggest counter-TTP lever).
18. **Tackle the drone threat as a top-tier program.** 341 foiled drone attacks in H1 2026 is a warning, not a comfort: build a provincial counter-drone unit with an airspace-management protocol; equip static posts; and criminalise drone-based IED delivery with dedicated prosecutions.

## 15.4 Phase II (2–5 Years): Institutionalisation

### A. Security-sector reform (Chapters 7's ten recommendations, sequenced)
19. **Establish the merged-district police framework.** Resolve the Levies/Khasadar integration entirely; statutory police for all seven districts; tribal constabulary terms, pay and pensions harmonised; district recruitment ramped up; and the Local Police Act or equivalent tested in consultation with jirgas (Chapter 5 design requirement).
20. **Professionalise the CTD.** Separate investigation from operation; an independent prosecution liaison unit; analytical cadre with specialist training; and transparent case data (arrest → charge → conviction) published annually — the province's whole counter-terrorism credibility depends on evidence that terrorism cases end in convictions.
21. **Reform police accountability.** Strengthen the KP Public Safety Commissions; institute independent investigation of serious police misconduct (lethal force, custodial abuse); and adopt body cameras/supervisory review at scale as evidence, not surveillance.
22. **Invest in investigations over enforcement.** Forensics (DNA, ballistics, digital exploitation), crime-scene management, prison-security upgrades (the Kohat/Bannu attack pattern is a prison-and-police-lines vulnerability), and witness-protection legislation with a functioning protection unit.
23. **Build the provincial intelligence architecture within law.** A unified provincial threat-fusion centre with police, CTD, Levies, NACTA and intelligence representation; data-protection rules and a parliamentary oversight committee (Chapter 7; Ch. 14.9's rights warning).

### B. Justice and rule of law (the legitimacy core)
24. **Judicial delivery plan for the NMDs.** Appoint and fund judges, prosecutors, staff and court infrastructure; mobile courts and video links where insecurity demands; and mandatory ATC case-conclusion targets with published quarterly data.
25. **Reparations and victims' mechanism.** A statutory provincial scheme covering: compensation (already mandated but inconsistently paid), medical and psychosocial support (500 psychiatrists nationally is a crisis — fund provincial psychiatric expansion and community-based psychosocial services), reconstruction, and *status* (a documented register of victims and a memorialization policy that includes all victims of the conflict, including those harmed by state forces). This is the concrete form of the PTM's truth-and-reconciliation demand, domesticated into the legal system.
26. **Kurram and sectarian peace enforcement.** Turn the 2025 *Aman Teega* and later accords into enforced instruments: disarmament with verifiable milestones, guaranteed road reopening, prosecution of those violating accords, a compensated, resettled peace, and a standing third-party (provincial + PMC) oversight mechanism — the Mindanao/monitoring model applied locally (Chapter 13).
27. **Jirga formalisation.** Legislate the jirga's role as mediator in civil disputes with rights safeguards (women's representation, prohibition of verdicts violating statute, no jurisdiction over terrorism cases) and fund district dispute-resolution centres with referral protocols to courts (Chapter 12.2).

### C. Deradicalization and reintegration at scale (Chapter 8's eight recommendations, sequenced)
28. **Statute and a Rehabilitation and Reintegration Authority.** Legal framework for voluntary and court-ordered programmes; a statutory juvenile track; and a funded authority (federal-provincial) with published standards.
29. **Relocate and expand programmes.** Move to where radicalization is occurring — Bannu, D.I. Khan, the NMDs — with family-based rehabilitation (the Sparlay-type model), vocational training, apprenticeships and aftercare grants, plus employment placement partnerships with chambers of commerce (Chapter 12.11).
30. **Independent evaluation.** A five-year, independently conducted evaluation of Sabawoon, Mishal and all provincial programmes, with pretraining/post-release measurement, recidivism tracking and *published* results — the absence of independent evaluation after 17 years is unacceptable and fixable (Chapter 8).
31. **Counter-propaganda with local credibility.** Fund Pashto-language counter-narrative content produced by Pashtun religious scholars, teachers, women's groups and former combatants; distribute via radio, social media and madrasa networks; do not outsource to distant agencies.
32. **Madrasa reform, second attempt.** Restore a consultative registration framework (statutory, transparent, non-punitive) after the October 2024 reversal; condition financing on transparency; fund modern-subject teaching; and separate the registration function from security surveillance to rebuild trust (Chapters 8, 11).
33. **Reintegration security guarantees.** Protect ex-detainees/rehabilitees from retaliation (police liaison, protection for those threatened, anonymous reporting) — the Colombian lesson: unprotectyed reintegration produces dissidence (Chapter 13.2).

### D. Governance and local government (the missing tier)
34. **Hold local elections in the merged districts.** Extend statutory local government systems to the NMDs with devolved budgets and own-source revenue; ring-fence a share of the merged-district fund for district councils (this is the single missing institution capable of absorbing the fiscal infusion into public goods rather than elite capture).
35. **Federal–provincial security compact.** A statutory framework clarifying roles (federal financing of military operations; provincial policing primacy in civilian security; joint command for emergency responses), ending the "provinces are not consulted" dynamic and the blame Games that follow incidents (Chapter 11).
36. **Institutionalise the Apex Committee model.** Regularise provincial security coordination with published, non-classified summaries; include elected representatives and, where appropriate, civil-society observers in a defined consultative role.
37. **Anti-corruption as security policy.** Strengthen KPPRA, Ehtesab Commission powers, and asset-recovery in the NMDs' contracts (minerals, land, CPEC) — because corruption is the state's own insurgency oxygen (Chapters 4, 13.5).

### E. Economy and services (the co-production agenda, Chapter 10)
38. **Deliver the development–security package.** Prioritize, in the seven NMDs and south KP: secondary schools (girls' enrolment is the single most correlated indicator of long-run stability), technical/vocational institutes, health facilities with staffed capacity (not buildings alone), irrigation and road connectivity, and electricity/gas access. Fund through the merged-district scheme with quarterly public delivery dashboards.
39. **Jobs: the private-sector channel.** Rashakai and the other SEZs; mineral benefit-sharing; tourism infrastructure (the 12–20 million visitor economy is the fastest-growing employment engine); IT/freelancing hubs (Peshawar, Swat, Abbottabad) with connectivity and payment infrastructure; and the agricultural value chains of the southern districts.
40. **Land, water and property rights.** Resolve disputed land in the merged districts (a long-standing conflict driver); formalize tenure where possible; and settle upstream water entitlements transparently.
41. **Climate-adaptive reconstruction.** Rebuild 2025 flood damage with resilience standards; GLOF early warning; and a standing disaster-response mechanism integrated with police/Levies.

### F. Education and human capital (the generational bet)
42. **Emergency education program for the merged districts.** The NMDs' ~37 per cent primary and ~5 per cent secondary female enrolment are a security statistic: fund stipends, girls' schools (including secure campus transport), female teachers, and adult literacy; measure and publish district-level enrolment quarterly.
43. **Curriculum and teacher reform.** Civics, critical thinking, anti-sectarian and anti-extremism content; teacher training and certification; textbook review; and protection of education (no military use of schools; attacks on schools prosecuted).
44. **Youth pipeline.** Scale apprenticeships, internships and entrepreneurship financing; restore student unions and campus civic space; and fund sports and cultural infrastructure in the worst-affected districts — cheap, visible, and effective.

## 15.5 Phase III (5–15 Years): Consolidation and Transformation

**Security consolidation.** (45) Transition from population-centric IBO cycles to *community security contracts*: local security plans co-designed with councils, jirgas and police, with published targets; (46) institutionalize the Afghan border regime (monitoring, trade, mobility, and dispute resolution) so that border friction does not automatically escalate — building the "managed confrontation" scenario (Ch. 9-B) into an institutionalized relationship; (47) if and when a TTP disengagement process becomes possible, apply the Chapter 13 criteria — decentralized, conditional, verified, staged, with monitored decommissioning and explicit no-impunity boundaries, and with third-party monitoring.

**Justice and reconciliation.** (48) A provincial *truth and documentation* body — not a South Africa–style amnesty commission, but a legally established process to document the conflict's human cost (all sides), record testimony, identify the missing, and publish a public record; paired with (49) selective accountability for mass-casualty atrocities; (50) a permanent victims' support institution with reparations as a statutory entitlement.

**Political consolidation.** (51) Guarantee lawful political space for Pashtun parties and youth movements; (52) build cross-party consensus on the security framework (a provincial security charter) so that policy survives elections; (53) institutionalize federal–provincial coordination (joint annual security and development review, published).

**Economic transformation.** (54) Consolidate the merged-district fiscal guarantee beyond the current NFC cycle; (55) build a provincial resilience economy: minerals with local value addition, energy (hydropower), tourism, IT exports, agriculture; (56) close the NMD human-development gap to within 10 points of the provincial average; (57) maintain climate resilience as a standing program.

**Regional normalization.** (58) Trade and people-to-people ties with Afghanistan (economics as the peace dividend); (59) regional frameworks (SCO, OIC) for counter-terrorism cooperation and refugee burden-sharing; (60) a settled, non-militarized border regime ratified on both sides — the only durable answer to a porous, contested frontier.

## 15.6 Sequencing Logic and Critical Dependencies

| Phase | Must complete before dependent steps | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Paying arrears & legislating the fund | Phase II local government; education expansion; economic programs | Without money, devolution and services are fictional and the political class is discredited |
| Police protection package | Phase II police reform & CTD professionalisation | Recruitment and reform fail if officers keep dying without kit |
| Ceasefire → verification mechanism | Phase I border regime; Phase III regional normalization | A ceasefire without monitoring is a pause, not a settlement (the 2004–2008 lesson) |
| Lifting the PTM ban & releasing detainees | Phase II political consensus; Phase III reconciliation | Alienation is the recruitment channel; criminalising it is self-defeating |
| ATC throughput & witness protection | Phase II/III accountability, truth process | Impunity reinforces both militancy and grievance |
| Local government elections (NMDs) | Phase III community security contracts; NMD development | Without elected local bodies there is no interface for either services or security |
| Madrasa re-registration framework | Phase III counter-narrative consolidation | The 2024 reversal must be reversed again to close the theological pipeline |

**The critical path:** **fiscal rescue → police protection → justice delivery → local government → reintegration at scale → political inclusion → regional normalization.** Parallel action is desirable, but the first three are prerequisites for credibility; a roadmap that begins with reconciliation while officers lack helmets will not be believed.

## 15.8 Financing the Roadmap: Costs, the Conflict-Loss Baseline and the Fiscal Arithmetic

**The baseline.** Chapter 10 estimated KP's annual conflict-related economic loss at **Rs 400–800 billion** (1.5–3 per cent of provincial economic output under different assumptions); Pakistan's cumulative war-on-terror losses since 2001 are internationally cited at **USD 126.79 billion to 152 billion** (with individual years estimated over Rs 1 trillion nationally). The merged-district shortfall alone is **Rs 568 billion** — roughly 0.7–1.4 years of the entire provincial conflict loss, or (to put it differently) the state loses in 8–17 months of conflict what it has failed to transfer to the merged districts in a full award cycle.

**The arithmetic of the fix.** NMD obligations: Rs 568 billion arrears + annual 3 per cent allocation (on a rising provincial share). Police protection package: a one-time Rs 50–80 billion (protected vehicles, counter-IED, counter-drone, communications) with Rs 30–50 billion annually in recurrent costs (pay, insurance, equipment replacement). Deradicalization and reintegration at scale: Rs 10–20 billion annually (facilities, staff, stipends, aftercare, independent evaluation). Education emergency in the NMDs: Rs 40–60 billion annually to approach provincial enrolment ratios. Local government and devolved budgets: funded from the merged-district allocation. Judicial expansion: Rs 5–10 billion annually. **Total incremental requirement: on the order of Rs 150–250 billion per year at maturity, in addition to the Rs 568 billion arrears** — i.e., at the lower bound, *less than half* of the estimated annual conflict loss. The investment case is thus straightforward: the province and the federation already pay 2–4 times the roadmap's incremental cost in lost output, displacement, security expenditure, and destroyed capital.

**Revenue sources.** (1) The NFC share and provincial taxes (with strengthened anti-corruption in collection); (2) reallocation within the federal security budget (Pakistan's defence/security spending is large; the merged-district shortfall is small relative to it); (3) donor and IFI financing for education, health, reintegration and monitoring (conditioned on delivery); (4) CPEC-related provincial revenue as it materializes; (5) a dedicated merged-district development window with multi-year budgeting (the absence of which is exactly what produces lapsed allocations — the Rs 132bn of Rs 700bn pattern); and (6) diaspora/Pakistani philanthropic funding for education, channeled through transparent institutions.

**Financing principles.** Ring-fence and protect allocations from lapsed budgets; publish district-wise releases monthly; condition donor tranches on verified delivery; finance *recurrent* costs (teachers, police, judges) as well as buildings — the classic Pakistani failure is projects without staff; and treat compensation payments as automatic entitlements, not discretionary patronage.

## 15.9 Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning Framework

**Design.** The M&E framework operationalises the three deficits (Chapter 11) into measurable commitments, with independent verification:

*Fiscal deficit indicators:* percentage of awarded NMD funds released within the fiscal year; arrears balance; percentage of development budget spent (with district-wise publication); compensation payments made within 180 days (target: 90 per cent); audit report timeliness and referral of findings.

*Accountability indicators:* police attacks, police killed, and *cases progressed* (arrest → charge → conviction) for attacks on the state; ATC cases concluded within 24 months (target ≥75 per cent); independent police-misconduct investigations completed; prison-security incidents; counter-terror financing seizures; operation collateral-damage reviews published.

*Legitimacy indicators:* NMD local-government elections held; PTM proscription status; number of security detainees held without charge; journalists/activists prosecuted for expression; public-opinion surveys (trust in police, courts, provincial government) conducted independently and published; victim-satisfaction with compensation and rehabilitation services; displaced return rates and property-restoration rates.

*Security-consolidation indicators:* conflict fatalities by district and actor (trend); TTP taxation/parallel-justice reports; attacks on schools/girls' education; border-closure days and cross-border trade volumes; thwarted drone attacks; attacks on polio teams and minority sites.

*Socio-economic indicators:* NMD female enrolment ratios; out-of-school children; health-facility staffing; household poverty; youth employment; tourism arrivals and sector employment; climate-disaster reconstruction delivery.

**Architecture.** A provincial *Security and Normalization Monitoring Cell* within the Planning and Development Department (or a statutory equivalent), with an independent Technical Advisory Panel (academia, civil society, donor representatives, and — where possible — a credible third party such as the World Bank or UNDP) producing a published *Annual Normalization Report* within 180 days of each fiscal year, debated in the KP Assembly and shared with the National Security Committee. Data sources: police and CTD records; health and education management information systems; district administration reportage; independent incident monitoring (CRSS/SATP/ACLED-type sources); household surveys; and donor M&E.

**Learning loop.** The annual report must include a *non-compliance register*: every unmet commitment with a named responsible institution and a remedy timeline. Without this, monitoring repeats the pattern documented in Chapter 11 — data collection without consequence.

## 15.10 Implementation Risks and the Political Economy of Reform

| Risk | Character | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal promises again not honoured | Repeated behaviour; 2028 expiry | Statutory floor; audited publication; election-cycle accountability |
| Elites capture the NMD fund | Patronage networks | Community monitoring; public beneficiary lists; asset recovery; local government |
| Police reform blocked by hierarchy/institutional interests | Bureaucratic resistance | Protection-first sequencing (reform becomes attractive once officers stop dying); federal financing; published case data |
| Security establishment resists political inclusion (PTM) | Doctrine/security-first logic | Evidence that exclusion feeds recruitment; third-party dialogue; measured release of non-violent activists |
| TTP spoilers attack the roadmap's visible successes (schools, markets, tourists, SEZs) | Strategic spoiling | Hard-target protection of critical nodes; rapid compensation; narrative management |
| Afghanistan spoiler escalation during implementation | External | Mediation and monitoring mechanisms; diversified trade routes; hedging (Ch. 9-E) |
| Donor withdrawal | Global fiscal contraction | Domestic financing priority for protection and arrears; donor funds concentrated on education/reintegration |
| Political instability / elections | Cyclical | Provincial security charter; multi-year budgeting; independent monitoring to survive governments |
| Data falsification (police/CTD reporting) | Institutional incentives | Independent verification; survey cross-checks; published anomalies |
| Securitization of monitoring (surveillance of civil society) | Rights risk | Statutory mandate; data protection; external oversight |

**The political-economy reality.** Each roadmap item has a beneficiary and a potential loser. The losers are: patronage networks (beneficiary lists and audits), parts of the security establishment (political inclusion and judicial authority), unaccountable bureaucracies (published data and delivery targets), and the militants (whose recruitment suffers as the state delivers). The roadmap's political feasibility therefore depends on making the *security case* for each item — the protection pivot appeals to the institution; the fiscal rescue appeals to federal credibility; the political inclusion appeals to the counter-insurgency logic; and the transparency appeal rests on exposing the cost of the status quo, which the monitoring framework is designed to do annually.

## 15.11 Regional and International Cooperation Agenda

**With Afghanistan:** ceasefire consolidation and verification; border management (documented mobility, trade, and reciprocal security commitments); prisoner exchanges; refugee policy with dignity and UNHCR involvement; and joint economic projects to create stakeholders for restraint. Mediation channels (Qatar, Türkiye, Saudi Arabia, China) should be institutionalised into a standing mechanism rather than resorted to after each crisis. The roadmap hedges against the "Taliban instability" scenario (Ch. 9-E) by keeping lines to all factions and by preparing for spillovers (refugee influx, cross-border militant redistribution).

**With India:** no normalization progress in KP requires solving the Kashmir dispute, but the *risk* of a two-front crisis (Ch. 14.4) argues for crisis-management channels, restored (even if limited) dialogue, and a mutual understanding on non-interference in each other's internal insurgencies — including verification mechanisms. India's role in KP (the alleged patronage claims of 2025–2026) should be handled with evidence, not rhetoric: publicly documented cases are stronger than accusations, and false accusations discredit the state's own case.

**With China:** CPEC security cooperation; intelligence sharing; and — critically — a Chinese interest in the fiscal and political stability (not only physical protection) of the corridor's districts; Beijing's own mediation role (2026 talks) is a positive precedent for institutionalized diplomacy.

**With the Gulf and Türkiye:** financing for reintegration and education (Saudi/UAE/Qatar funds); Pakistan's diaspora; counter-narrative partnerships (Saudi PRAC's experience; see Chapter 8); and mediation channels with Afghanistan.

**With the UN and IFIs:** monitoring (a Mindanao-style third-party body adapted to the merger's fiscal and security commitments); reintegration standards and evaluation (UNODC, IOM); refugee processing (UNHCR); education and health funding (UNICEF, WFP, World Bank); and FATF-consistent counter-financing (with rights safeguards).
