# NORMALIZING THE SECURITY SITUATION OF KHYBER PAKHTUNKHWA

## Conflict Evolution, Threat Dynamics, and Pathways to Sustainable Peace

**A Mixed-Methods Policy Research Monograph**

---

**Subject:** Security normalization in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Province, Pakistan — historical evolution, threat mapping, root causes, the FATA merger, military operations, law enforcement and institutional reform, deradicalization, geopolitics, socio-economic determinants, governance, civil society, comparative lessons, scenarios, and a phased policy roadmap.

**Geographic scope:** Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Province including the seven Newly Merged Districts (NMDs) of the former Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA); the adjacent Afghanistan borderland; and relevant federal territory (Islamabad Capital Territory) where violence has spilled over.

**Temporal scope:** From the colonial frontier era (c. 1849) to the data cut-off of **4 October 2026**, with primary quantitative emphasis on 2001–2026 and the most detailed analysis of the 2018–2026 period.

**Citation style:** Chicago author–date, with full source details in the Bibliography. In-text citations use the form (Author/Institution Year); where a source is a data portal or news report, the outlet and date are given.

**Length of the full document:** approximately 73,000 words across sixteen chapters, a statistical annex and eight appendices, supported by thirty-one statistical tables and five structured figures.

**Data cut-off:** 4 October 2026. Events after this date are not reflected. Where casualty claims are contested between the Pakistan military, Afghan authorities, militant groups, and independent monitors, this is stated explicitly rather than adjudicated by assertion.

## ABSTRACT

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) is, as of 2026, the principal theatre of a renewed and escalating militant insurgency in Pakistan and — following the February 2026 outbreak of open hostilities between Pakistan and the Afghan Taliban administration — the frontier of an interstate conflict as well. The Global Terrorism Index 2026 ranked Pakistan the country most impacted by terrorism in the world for the first time, on the strength of 1,139 terrorism-related deaths and 1,045 incidents in 2025; 74 per cent of attacks and 67 per cent of fatalities occurred in KP and Balochistan jointly, with KP alone recording 2,331 violence-linked deaths in 2025, a 44 per cent year-on-year increase and the highest provincial figure since 2015 (IEP 2026; CRSS 2025b). The Pakistan Institute for Peace Studies' predecessor baseline of a 17 per cent rise in attacks in 2023 and the South Asia Terrorism Portal's count of 766 fatal terrorism-linked incidents in KP in 2025 — the highest since the 1,432 recorded in 2009 — establish the current phase not as a residual law-and-order problem but as a strategic relapse (PIPS, cited in Dawn 2024a; SATP 2026b).

This monograph asks a deceptively simple question: **what would it actually take to normalize security in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and why have successive attempts fallen short?** It answers through a mixed-methods design combining descriptive quantitative analysis of incident and casualty data (SATP, CRSS, PIPS, ACLED, GTI), qualitative documentary analysis of policy and legal texts (the National Action Plan, the National Internal Security Policy, the 25th and 27th Constitutional Amendments, the KP Police Act 2017, the Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 as amended), and structured comparative analysis of eight conflict-transition cases (Northern Ireland, Colombia, Sri Lanka, Iraqi Kurdistan, Afghanistan, Mozambique, Sierra Leone, and Mindanao, with a critical note on Chechnya).

Five findings organize the study's argument. First, **the current insurgency is a networked, umbrella-structured, cross-border phenomenon, not a single organization**: the EUAA's June 2026 assessment identifies the Tehrīk-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) as an umbrella coalition that has absorbed more than seventy factions since 2018, and 2026 saw both the emergence of new front organizations (Ittehad-ul-Mujahideen Pakistan, implicated in the 18 September 2026 Kohat Police Lines attack in which at least 31 people died) and the formal split of Jamaat-ul-Ahrar (EUAA 2026; TRT 2026; Wikipedia 2026d). Second, **military-dominant counter-insurgency has produced episodic suppression but no durable political settlement**; the 2025–26 data show that security-force operations killed a record number of militants (921 in the third quarter of 2026 alone) while failing to reduce attack frequency or civilian exposure (CRSS 2026c). Third, **the 2018 FATA merger has been fiscally and institutionally hollowed out**: against a parliamentary commitment of 3 per cent of the federal divisible pool for ten years, only Rs 132 billion of an expected Rs 700 billion had reached the merged districts by mid-2026 — a Rs 568 billion shortfall — while the commitment itself expires in 2028 (The News 2026). Fourth, **the counter-insurgency is now embedded in an interstate conflict**: Operation Ghazab-lil-Haq, launched on 26 February 2026, has produced reciprocal strikes, two failed ceasefires, and more than 1,100 civilian casualties in Afghanistan in 2026 (UNAMA, cited in Khaama 2026; Dawn 2026g). Fifth, and most consequentially for policy, **the binding constraint on normalization is not the absence of a strategy but the absence of a politically legitimate, locally owned, and fiscally resourced implementation coalition** — a civil–military–provincial–tribal compact that the current architecture, in which the army controls internal-security policy while the provincial police absorb the casualties, structurally struggles to produce (ICG 2026).

The study's central thesis is that **normalization is best understood not as the elimination of violence but as the construction of three interlocking monopolies: the state's monopoly on organized violence (security), the citizen's enforceable claim on the state (governance and justice), and the province's claim on national resources (fiscal federalism).** Where any one of the three is missing, the other two erode. KP's historical experience — colonial indirect rule, the Federally Administered Tribal Areas' century of legal exception, the military-dominant post-2001 campaigns, and the post-2018 merger-without-resourcing — is a long demonstration of that proposition.

The monograph offers a phased, costed roadmap: short-term stabilization (0–2 years) centred on police-force protection and civil–military intelligence fusion; medium-term institutional consolidation (2–5 years) centred on justice-sector completion, madrasa regulation, and the fiscal settlement of the merged districts; and long-term structural transformation (5–15 years) centred on education, employment, and a regional trade-and-mobility compact with Afghanistan. It provides a 46-item recommendations matrix with responsible institutions, timelines, indicative costs, and key performance indicators; a statistical annex; a comprehensive bibliography; and a policy brief for decision-makers. It is written to be falsifiable: each recommendation is tied to measurable indicators, and each scenario in Chapter 14 is tied to observable early-warning triggers.

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

*(A standalone version of this summary is provided as a separate deliverable.)*

**1. The threat has changed shape, not just scale.** KP's violence in 2024–2026 is best characterized as a *networked insurgency with an interstate rear base*, not a terrorist residual. The TTP umbrella now incorporates more than seventy factions and coordinates attacks across North and South Waziristan, Bannu, Lakki Marwat, Kurram, Bajaur, Khyber, Hangu, Tank, Dera Ismail Khan, Karak and — from August 2026 — again Swat, where the Kabal Police Station suicide bombing killed 17 people and was claimed by the TTP (EUAA 2026; AIP 2026; India Today 2026). The Islamic State–Khorasan Province (ISKP) operates a smaller but more deliberately civilian-targeting campaign, concentrating on Shia, Sufi and competitor-jihadist targets, including the 6 February 2026 Islamabad mosque bombing that killed 31 worshippers (The Diplomat 2026a; MEE/monitoring 2026). Militant drone warfare has crossed from novelty to routine: KP police reported foiling 341 drone attacks in the first half of 2026, with at least 100 attacks and 118 security-force casualties in the same period (GNET 2026).

**2. Human costs are borne overwhelmingly by police and civilians, and they are rising.** In the first half of 2026, 122 KP police personnel were killed (a 93.7 per cent increase on the same period of 2025) and 175 injured; between January and September 2026, 680 attacks were recorded against 508 in the comparable 2025 window (APP 2026b; Daily Times 2026a). Across 2023–2025, more than 500 KP police were killed, including 186 in 2023, 153 in 2024 and 159 in 2025 (The News 2026a). The 18 September 2026 Kohat Police Lines attack — a vehicle-borne IED, a suicide bombing and an assault on a district complex housing CTD and Special Branch offices — killed at least 31 people, most of them police at Friday prayers (TRT 2026). Civilian fatalities fell 24 per cent nationally in 2025 but remained the second-largest category in KP in 2026, at 187 in the second quarter alone (CRSS 2025b; CRSS 2026b).

**3. The war has become regional.** On 21–26 February 2026, cross-border clashes escalated into Operation Ghazab-lil-Haq, described by Pakistan's defence minister as "open war." Repeated airstrikes, drone strikes and artillery exchanges through October 2026 have killed hundreds of combatants on both sides by official claims that cannot be independently verified, and UNAMA recorded 764 civilian casualties in Afghanistan between 26 January and 31 March 2026 alone, with more than 1,100 civilian deaths and injuries by late September (Dawn 2026g; Hindustan Times 2026; Khaama 2026). Two Eid ceasefires (March and May 2026) failed; mediation by Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Türkiye and China has produced mechanisms without settlement. For KP, the war's consequences are direct: border closures, trade disruption at Torkham, refugee and returnee flows, and an emboldened TTP claiming a "spring offensive" (*Operation Khyber*) since 4 March 2026 (Eurasia Review 2026a).

**4. The FATA merger is unfinished at exactly the moment it matters most.** The 25th Amendment (2018) ended a century of legal exception, extended the superior judiciary and police to the tribal districts, and added 23 seats to the KP Assembly (now 145 members). But the fiscal promise — 3 per cent of the divisible pool for ten years to bring the merged districts to parity — has been met at roughly one-fifth of its value (Rs 132 billion of Rs 700 billion by 2026), and expires in 2028; the Peshawar High Court has repeatedly pressed the federal government on the shortfall, and in June 2026 the Prime Minister assured the KP Chief Minister of an NFC revision process within 180 days (The News 2026; PHC reporting 2025–26; Business Recorder 2026). Meanwhile the justice chain remains skeletal, the Levies/Khasadar-to-police transition is widely judged incomplete, and a hybrid legal order persists in practice (Haq 2024; ASPIRE-KP 2025).

**5. Socio-economic deterioration and violence are now mutually reinforcing.** KP's poverty rate rose from 28.7 per cent to 35.3 per cent in the FY2024-25 estimates — the second-highest provincial rate after Balochistan — while national poverty reached 28.9 per cent and inequality rose to a 27-year high (Planning Ministry 2026; Economic Survey 2025-26). KP's Human Development Index fell from 0.650 (2019) to 0.623 (2023) in the SDPI district-level series, with health and living-standards sub-indices declining (SDPI 2025). Thirty-one per cent of educated youth are jobless, and in 2025 more than 866,000 candidates applied for 16,454 teaching posts (KP Youth Policy 2026). In 2025 floods, KP recorded 504 deaths and 1.57 million people affected — the highest provincial toll — compounding the strain (UNICEF 2025; WHO 2025). Development and security are not sequential in KP; they are simultaneous and inseparable.

**6. What has worked, and why it has not persisted.** The 2009 Swat campaign (Rah-e-Rast) and the 2014–2017 Zarb-e-Azb/National Action Plan period achieved genuine suppression, and post-2014 recovery — rebuilt bazaars, returned tourism (12.4 million visitors to KP in 2025; 416,794 to Swat alone over Eid-ul-Adha 2026) — demonstrates that normalization is possible (KP Tourism Authority, cited in TFT 2026; DGIPR 2026). But suppression was not converted into settlement: negotiations with the TTP collapsed in 2021–2022; the 2022–2024 mid-term saw progressive deterioration; and by 2025 Pakistan recorded its deadliest year since 2015 (CRSS 2025b). The lesson across the province's history is consistent: **kinetic gains decay at the speed at which political and fiscal follow-through is absent.**

**7. Five structural drivers explain persistence.** (i) *Sanctuary economics*: the Afghanistan borderland provides rear bases, recruits, and weapons for groups that cannot hold terrain inside Pakistan; (ii) *institutional bifurcation*: military-led counter-insurgency with provincially funded, locally recruited police absorbing the risk produces intelligence gaps, legitimacy deficits and blame-shifting between Islamabad and Peshawar (ICG 2026); (iii) *justice vacuum*: in the merged districts, formal courts are shallow and informal jirga/collective-responsibility practice persists, so communities experience "normalization" as impunity plus coercion; (iv) *political economy of conflict*: smuggling, narcotics, extortion, mining rents and the war economy create constituencies for disorder; (v) *ideological reproduction*: an unregulated madrasa sector, online radicalization and sectarian entrepreneurs continue to generate new fighters faster than rehabilitation programs (Sabawoon/Mishal/Sparlay) can absorb them (NUST NJIPS 2021; JD 2023).

**8. Scenarios through 2035.** Three scenarios are developed: *Sustained Normalization* (requires a negotiated cross-border modus vivendi, fiscal settlement of the merged districts, and a police-justice build-out — probability assessed at 15–20 per cent under current trajectories); *Fragile Equilibrium* (continued grind: periodic mass-casualty attacks, ~2,000–3,500 annual violence-linked deaths, governance stagnation — 50–60 per cent); and *Deterioration/Fragmentation* (interstate escalation, urban mass-casualty terrorism, and the collapse of the merger agenda — 25–30 per cent). The decisive variables are the Pakistan–Afghanistan security channel, the merged districts' fiscal settlement, police capacity and protection, and the political settlement between the federal centre and KP's provincial government.

**9. The strategic recommendation.** Normalization requires a shift from a *counter-terrorism campaign* to a *state-building compact* with four pillars: (a) **Protect and professionalize the police** — the force that is actually dying — through protected budgets, protected tenure, equipment and intelligence fusion with the military; (b) **Complete the merger** — constitutionally guaranteed fiscal flows, functioning district courts, and a professional policing model for the NMDs, with a political settlement that re-empowers legitimate local intermediaries (jirgas, elected local councils) rather than bypassing them; (c) **Make peace with the border** — a verifiable, mediated security arrangement with Kabul, coupled with a managed, lawful cross-border mobility and trade regime rather than closure-driven policy; and (d) **Invest in the peace constituency** — education (especially girls'), employment, deradicalization at scale, and community-based prevention, financed by the fiscal settlement. Without (d), (a) is a treadmill; without (b), (c) has no foundation; without (c), (a) has no endgame.

## METHODOLOGY AND READER'S GUIDE

### Research design

This study employs a **convergent mixed-methods design**. The quantitative strand compiles and triangulates incident and fatality data from five independent monitors — the South Asia Terrorism Portal (SATP), the Centre for Research and Security Studies (CRSS), the Pakistan Institute for Peace Studies (PIPS), ACLED, and the Institute for Economics and Peace's Global Terrorism Index — deliberately *without* collapsing their divergent counts into a single figure. Different monitors count different things (SATP counts fatal incidents and combatant categories; CRSS counts violence-linked fatalities across attacks and state operations; GTI uses its own inclusion criteria); presenting ranges and definitions is more honest than manufacturing false precision. Where sources conflict (for example, the 2025 national fatality toll is reported as 3,417 by CRSS while SATP's KP-specific counts imply lower totals), both are given.

The qualitative strand analyses primary policy and legal texts; statements and press releases from the Inter-Services Public Relations (ISPR) and provincial authorities; parliamentary and court records; and the reporting of credible national and international outlets, flagging official claims as claims. The comparative strand uses a structured, theory-guided case comparison (Chapter 13).

### Analytical frameworks

Nine frameworks are applied explicitly rather than decoratively: the UNDP **human security** framework (Chapters 10, 12); **conflict transformation** (Lederach) versus conflict *management* (Chapter 15); **counter-insurgency doctrine** (Galula's primacy of political action; Kilcullen's "accidental guerrilla" and population-centric models) as a diagnostic of Pakistani practice (Chapter 6); **state-building and governance theory** (Chapter 11); **securitization theory** (Copenhagen School) to analyse how "the Pashtun belt" and the TTP have been framed, with consequences for civil liberties (Chapters 4, 12); the **political economy of conflict** and the **greed-versus-grievance model** (Collier–Hoeffler) to explain war-economy persistence (Chapter 4); the **whole-of-nation approach** (Chapter 15); and **resilience/community-based security** models (Chapters 8, 12).

### Ethics and limitations

This research involves communities that have suffered enormously, and it treats contested issues — military conduct, civilian casualties, the PTM ban, the merger's outcomes — by presenting evidence on all sides and identifying what cannot be verified. Three limitations are material. First, **access**: there is no independent, systematic, publicly available audit of military operations' civilian harm in KP; claims by ISPR, by militant media (Umar Media), and by affected communities diverge, sometimes irreconcilably. The study marks these as contested and where possible reports third-party verification (UNAMA, UN OHCHR, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, HRCP). Second, **data politics**: Pakistani officials have, at various points, restricted or contested casualty data; monitors themselves differ methodologically. Third, **non-transparency of counter-terrorism policy**: exact scale, targeting rules and oversight of intelligence-based operations are classified, as are the internal deliberations of the National Security Committee. Where the study extrapolates, it says so.

### Note on the reporting period

The commissioner's brief specified coverage "up to 2024–2025." Because the study is being produced in October 2026, and because 2026 produced changes of historic consequence — the outbreak of open conflict with Afghanistan, the largest police mass-casualty event since 2023, the first year in which Pakistan ranked first globally on the GTI, and the political transition in Peshawar from Ali Amin Gandapur to Sohail Afridi — the study extends coverage to 4 October 2026 while fully preserving the 2024–25 analytical baseline. This is noted here so that readers understand the data vintage of each chapter.
