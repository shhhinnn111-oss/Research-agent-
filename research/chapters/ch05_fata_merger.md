# CHAPTER 5 — THE FATA MERGER AND ITS SECURITY IMPLICATIONS

## 5.1 Historical Context of FATA's Special Constitutional Status

The Federally Administered Tribal Areas comprised, on the eve of the merger: seven agencies (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Orakzai, Kurram, North Waziristan and South Waziristan) and six Frontier Regions (FR Peshawar, FR Kohat, FR Bannu, FR D.I. Khan, FR Lakki Marwat and FR Tank), covering 27,220 km² with a population of approximately five million (2017 census). FATA's constitutional status was sui generis: governed directly by the federal executive through the Governor of KP as agent to the President; administered by Political Agents; regulated by the Frontier Crimes Regulations 1901 (Article 247 of the 1973 Constitution preserved the arrangement); represented in the National Assembly and Senate but not in the provincial assembly; and excluded from the jurisdiction of the superior judiciary, from ordinary criminal and civil law, and from the federal taxation and development framework in any normal sense.

**The institutions of exception.** Four institutions defined FATA's political life. (i) *The Political Agent* — combining executive, judicial, revenue and intelligence functions, accountable to the federal government and largely unaccountable to residents. (ii) *The FCR* — permitting collective punishment (including the notorious "40 FCR" joint-liability clause under which entire communities could be fined, blockaded, or have property seized for individual offences); excluding appeal and lawyers' representation; and empowering the Political Agent to order detention and demolition. (iii) *The maliki system* — the network of state-recognized tribal notables receiving allowances and performing brokerage functions (including raising lashkars and providing intelligence), whose fate under militancy would shape the post-merger order. (iv) *The jirga* — recognized as a dispute-resolution and, in practice, criminal-adjudication mechanism, with the state's authority mediated through it. Alongside these sat the *Khasadar* (paid tribal constabulary, locally recruited, lightly armed) and *Levies* (a more regularized frontier force) — forces of the frontier, not of the ordinary citizen.

**Consequences of the exception.** Empirical assessments of FATA's condition in 2018 were unambiguous. The region had: the lowest literacy rates in Pakistan (with female literacy in the low single digits in parts of the belt); the worst health indicators (few functioning hospitals; high maternal and infant mortality); the fewest kilometres of paved road per capita; minimal electricity; no banking in most areas; and no systems for property registration, which would later bedevil the merger's land and mineral governance. The FCR also produced specific human rights pathologies documented over decades: arbitrary detention, collective punishment, exclusion of women's rights under customary practice, and, in the post-2001 period, the use of FATA as the primary theatre of war (Chapter 6). It is against this baseline that the merger's promises — and its shortfalls — must be judged.

## 5.2 The FATA Reforms Committee and the Road to the 25th Amendment

**The committee.** The FATA Reforms Committee, constituted in 2015 and chaired by the Adviser to the Prime Minister on Foreign Affairs, Sartaj Aziz, was mandated to propose a durable governance solution. Its 2016 report presented options — (a) full merger with KP; (b) maintaining the status quo with reform; (c) creating a separate province/administrative unit; (d) phased integration — and recommended: **full merger with KP**, to be implemented over time; the **discontinuation of the FCR**; the extension of the superior judiciary and of provincial services; a **transitional "FATA Development Authority"**; and — decisively for fiscal politics — a **3 per cent share of the National Finance Commission divisible pool** for ten years to bring the region to parity. The committee reported having consulted in depth with more than 3,000 local elders/jirga representatives, with a clear majority favoring merger (FATA Reforms Committee 2016; Dawn 2016).

**Political contestation.** The reform process was contested across three axes. First, *within the tribal areas*: some elders opposed merger as the loss of a distinctive identity and of the maliki entitlements; youth and reformist networks (later heavily associated with the PTM) supported it as the end of civic exclusion. Second, *between the centre and KP*: the federal government, having administered FATA directly, would transfer both responsibility and cost to Peshawar; KP's government (then PTI-led) supported merger as a historic expansion of the province's territory and representation. Third, *among political parties*: JUI-F, whose networks were strongest in FATA, contested the terms; ANP and PTI competed for credit; and the Pashtun-nationalist current was itself divided.

**The 25th Amendment (May 2018).** On the committee's recommendation, Parliament enacted the 25th Constitutional Amendment, merging FATA and the Frontier Regions into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; repealing Article 247; extending the constitution, the superior courts' jurisdiction and provincial law to the merged districts; providing 23 general seats in the KP Assembly (bringing it to 145, of which 16 are reserved seats and 4 for non-Muslims); and guaranteeing representation in the National Assembly (12 NA seats) — a step that also increased KP's share of national legislative seats. The amendment passed with overwhelming cross-party support — a rarity in Pakistan's fractious politics — and was widely celebrated as the most significant constitutional reform since the 18th Amendment.

**The fiscal promise.** Attached to the merger through parliamentary resolutions and committee recommendations was the commitment to allocate 3 per cent of the divisible pool — approximately Rs 90–90+ billion annually by some early estimates, rising as revenues grew, and amounting, by later accounting, to a cumulative expectation of roughly Rs 700 billion over the first seven years (The News 2026). This commitment was framed as the *quid pro quo* for integration: without it, the merged districts would remain the poorest in the federation and merger would be a transfer of legal labels rather than of resources.

## 5.3 The Merger Process: Legal, Administrative and Security Dimensions

**Legal transition.** The legal extension required: (i) the replacement of the FCR with the ordinary criminal and civil law of KP; (ii) the creation of district courts and prosecution services in the seven districts; (iii) the extension of the KP Police Act 2017 and the absorption of the Levies and Khasadar forces; (iv) the application of provincial revenue, excise, education, health and local government law; and (v) the dismantling of the Political Agent system in favor of Deputy Commissioners — the administrative emblem of the merger.

**Administrative transition.** Deputy Commissioners replaced Political Agents; "Tribal District" administrations were established; and provincial departments extended (schools, health, agriculture, forests, roads). In practice, transition occurred on a spectrum: Khyber, Bajaur, Mohmand and Kurram continued to host large military/FC deployments and the writ of civilian authority remained thin; the Frontier Regions were absorbed into adjacent settled districts (Peshawar, Kohat, Bannu, D.I. Khan, Lakki Marwat, Tank), creating a patchwork of district boundaries; and the province's *administrative* capacity to supervise seven new districts was limited by finance and by the security environment.

**Security transition.** The most consequential and least resolved dimension. In June 2019 the KP government promulgated Levies and Khasadar ordinances (subsequently legislated in September 2019), bringing approximately 28,000 personnel of the two forces under police command: the Levies (about 15,673 personnel against 16,053 sanctioned posts before transition) as an "essential service," and the Khasadars — whose legal basis had been executive orders, not statute — brought under police powers. The KP government committed to recruiting about 6,000 additional police for the merged districts (in phases; the first tranche of several hundred posts proposed in 2019), and to establishing district police structures. The transition, however, produced a hybrid: large numbers of locally recruited, lightly trained personnel integrated into a force whose doctrine, discipline and accountability systems were built for settled districts; command from distant district police officers; and local legitimacy deficits because the "new" police were sometimes seen as the old Khasadars under a new label — or, worse, as outsiders. The 2019 legislation itself passed amid opposition protest and Levies/Khasadar demonstrations outside the assembly, an early indicator that personnel were being managed as a *fiscal and employment* problem rather than a security-capability problem.

**Representative transition.** The first provincial elections in the merged districts (2019, in the 16 NA/23 PK seats) enfranchised the tribal population in provincial politics for the first time. PTI and independent candidates performed strongly; subsequent 2024 general elections confirmed PTI's organizational dominance in the belt. But representation without fiscal authority or local government produced a "seats without services" outcome: MPs and MPAs could advocate, but the administrative and financial machinery to implement remained absent.

## 5.4 Governance Challenges Post-Merger: Institutional Vacuum and Capacity Gaps

**The vacuum.** Independent assessments five to eight years after the merger converge on a set of findings. The GRR Journal's 2024 study of post-merger "administrative chaos" concluded that the transformation had been implemented hastily, without adequate planning or capacity-building; that the promised administrative structures remained incomplete; and that "the merger's promises have not been fulfilled, and the new administrative structure still struggles to gain public trust." The ASPIRE-KP symposium (2025–26) found deficient funding and unpreparedness; the failure to post capable personnel; weak coordination between federal and provincial governments; incomplete devolution to local government; and the resurgence of TTP violence aggravating all of the above (GRR Journal 2024; ASPIRE-KP 2025).

**Specific governance deficits.**
1. **Local government**: The merger's implicit promise of local self-government for the tribal districts was not implemented at scale; the KP Local Government Act's application to the NMDs is partial, and provincial governments have repeatedly deferred local elections and (where elected) local budgets and functions.
2. **Land and minerals**: FATA's customary land tenure and the absence of cadastral records created a legal vacuum; the province's efforts to regularize mining (marble, chromite, coal) generated disputes over who owns and who benefits — with reports of provincial-federal contestation and of interest from foreign investors, including Chinese and (reportedly) US firms, over critical minerals, amid unresolved ownership rules (ResearchGate/GRR assessments 2025–26).
3. **Civil service capacity**: The merged districts are not attractive postings; allowances are limited; security constraints reduce mobility; and provincial departments have filled only a fraction of sanctioned posts.
4. **Revenue**: With no tax base and limited provincial fiscal space, the districts depend on transfers — which are delayed, contested, and shrinking as a share of need.
5. **Coordination**: The federal government retains significant roles (SAFRON; federal PSDP projects; the FC's security role), while the province holds administrative responsibility — producing the "two masters" problem noted in the literature.

**The FCR's afterlife.** Perhaps the most damaging finding of the post-merger assessments is that FCR-style practices survived the FCR. Reports documented detentions and punishments under the logic of collective responsibility (the "40 FCR" clause) even after the regulation's repeal; and there are credible accounts of punitive demolitions, curfews, and collective fines. This is not legal formalism: a community that experiences the "new" law as the old exception under a different name learns to distrust the merger itself.

## 5.5 Security Force Transition: From Frontier Corps to Police and Levies

**Structure of forces in the merged districts (2026).** Four security actors operate: (1) the **Pakistan Army and Frontier Corps (FC North)**, holding border positions, conducting operations, and providing the *de facto* security backbone; (2) the **KP Police**, with district structures, CTD presence and the absorbed Levies/Khasadar personnel; (3) **local militias/peace committees/lashkars**, partially armed and paid, operating with state encouragement in some districts; and (4) **Federal Constabulary**-type units deployed for specific tasks (protests, campaigns, patrols), which suffered 124 killed and 244 injured in 2025 according to the KP CTD's own data.

**Capability assessment.** Independent and parliamentary assessments identify nine recurring problems: (i) personnel shortfalls against sanctioned strength (with the CTD at roughly 81 per cent of sanctioned posts province-wide in 2026 and the NMD forces worse); (ii) equipment deficits (the official claim that the entire CTD has only nine M4 rifles and eleven night-vision devices — contested, but indicative of the debate over resourcing — was made by a federal coordinator in September 2026); (iii) insufficient training for counter-insurgency roles (police are trained for law-and-order and investigation, not for platoon-level combat); (iv) absence of protected mobility and counter-IED capacity (routine APC losses); (v) delayed salaries/compensation and inadequate life insurance, which undermines morale; (vi) command and control fragmentation (DIGs, DPOs, FC, army sectors); (vii) intelligence-sharing gaps between the military and police (ICG 2026); (viii) the double burden of counter-terrorism and ordinary policing; and (ix) "insider" risk from locally recruited personnel coerced by militants.

**The core dilemma.** Two models compete. *Model A* (police-led): a well-resourced provincial police, trained, equipped and accountable, assuming primacy in the merged districts with federal paramilitary support at the border — the model implied by the merger's logic and demanded by provincial politicians. *Model B* (military-led): continued army/FC primacy in counter-insurgency, with police relegated to static protection and investigation — the de facto status quo (ICG 2026). Each has a coherent rationale; what fails is the *combination* — military primacy without police protection and without a transfer-of-authority pathway. The study's assessment (Chapter 7, Chapter 15) is that a *sequenced* Model A, beginning with protection and intelligence fusion and progressing to police primacy in districts as capability thresholds are met, offers the only pathway consistent with both the merger's promises and counter-insurgency doctrine — but it requires resources currently not committed.

## 5.6 Judicial System Extension to Merged Districts

**What was supposed to happen.** The 25th Amendment extended the jurisdiction of the Peshawar High Court and the Supreme Court to the merged districts, abolished the FCR's appellate exclusions, and contemplated the establishment of district courts, prosecution services, and the extension of the KP judicial system.

**What happened.** The record indicates partial, slow and shallow extension. District and sessions courts were established in the seven districts, but with deficiencies in: physical infrastructure (court buildings, residential accommodation, secure transport); judicial officers (shortfalls, and reluctance to serve in insecure areas); prosecution capacity; police investigation quality (the binding constraint on conviction); legal aid; and family/consumer/anti-corruption jurisdiction. The Anti-Terrorism Act applies, but ATC capacity is province-wide and congested (Chapter 7). Two further complications are specific to the merged districts: (a) the persistence of informal justice (*jirga*, *riwaj*) both as a service that communities prefer (speed, accessibility, local legitimacy) and as a system that departs from constitutional rights (collective punishment, exclusion of women, coercive enforcement); and (b) the coexistence of *qisas/diyat* and other Islamic-law provisions with statutory law in a context where communities expect the *jirga*'s restorative logic. Independent scholarship documents the "skeletal" state of the formal system and the continued functioning of hybrid orders (Haq 2024; GRR Journal 2024).

**Why it matters for security.** In security terms, the justice gap has three consequences: (i) *impunity* — militants and their facilitators are rarely convicted, so the coercive message of law is weak; (ii) *grievance* — citizens detained under security laws without trial, or fined collectively, feel the state's coercion without its protection; and (iii) *substitution* — communities turn to armed actors or tribal militias for dispute resolution, which entrenches the very structures that sustain conflict (Chapter 4). The Peshawar High Court's sustained attention to merged-district rights litigation (including the NFC shortfall and Sehat Card cases) is one of the few institutional counterweights documented in 2025–26.

## 5.7 Development and Rehabilitation in Merged Areas

**The promise and the balance sheet.** At merger, financing for the merged districts was to come through three channels: (i) the **3 per cent divisible-pool commitment** (via the NFC framework), approximately Rs 700 billion cumulatively over the first seven years; (ii) the **Accelerated Implementation Programme (AIP)**; and (iii) annual federal and provincial PSDP allocations. The actual delivery, as documented in a July 2026 account by a former KP finance secretary and in PHC proceedings: **Rs 132 billion delivered against Rs 700 billion expected — a Rs 568 billion shortfall**; no new NFC award since 2010 (the 7th NFC Award predates the merger); a promised National Assembly resolution endorsement (September 2021) of a 3 per cent allocation (which then-CM and others estimated at up to nearly Rs 1 trillion annually in future terms) never implemented; and no provision in the FY2026-27 budget for the roughly Rs 192 billion a year owed. The commitment expires in 2028 (The News 2026; Tribune 2025d; PHC reporting 2025–26).

**Consequences for services.** The shortfall is directly visible: incomplete health facilities and closures; teacher shortages and school closures (compounded by insecurity-driven closures in Kurram, Bajaur, Khyber, Mohmand and North Waziristan); the suspension of Sehat Card health entitlement coverage for tribal districts at one point (litigated); stalled road and electricity projects; and the inability to fund the ~6,000 new police posts promised. Provincial fiscal stress is compounded by the KP government's claim to withheld federal dues.

**The political economy of the shortfall.** Two structural causes stand out. First, the **NFC structure**: provincial shares are determined by an award that requires consensus of all provinces, and no province has agreed to fund the merged districts' 3 per cent out of its own share. The commitment therefore functions as an unfunded federal mandate. Second, **budgetary politics**: the federal government's fiscal consolidation under the IMF programme (FY2026-27) constrained discretionary development spending; and in 2026 all provinces reportedly agreed that FBR collection above the previous year's base would accrue to the federation — a formula that further squeezes the merged districts.

**Assessment.** The fiscal shortfall is the single most damaging failure of the merger, because it converts a promised *peace dividend* into its opposite: a demonstration that state promises are unreliable. It is also the most fixable: the instruments exist (a presidential order under Article 160; a dedicated statutory fund; ring-fenced AIP allocations; a legislated floor for merged-district spending). What is required is political will — and this study treats the fiscal settlement of the merged districts as the central near-term test of normalization seriousness (Chapter 15).

## 5.8 Community Perceptions and Political Dynamics Post-Merger

**Perceptions.** The available evidence — academic studies, civil society consultations, and journalistic accounts — indicates a trajectory from *hope* (2018) to *disappointment* (2020–22) to *anger and fear* (2023–26). Survey research in the immediate post-merger period found overwhelming support for merger (one study reported 92 per cent support among educated respondents) alongside pessimism about implementation. Later assessments describe the prevailing view as: legal integration is welcome; economic integration has failed; security has deteriorated; and the state's promises (compensation, jobs, return guarantees) are not credible. ASPIRE-KP's civil-society forum recorded particular frustration with delayed fund disbursement, and the "neglect by the KP government" in ensuring robust governance mechanisms — a criticism that cuts across party lines.

**Political dynamics.** Three trends are visible. First, PTI's dominance of the merged districts (2019, 2024 elections) gives the belt a distinct political voice — but also places it at the centre of the federal-PTI conflict, with consequences for service delivery and for the tone of security policy. Second, the *malik system's* collapse left the old patronage politics without an institutional successor; the elected representatives have no local fiscal apparatus; and new intermediary figures (peace-committee chairs, contractors, militia commanders) have emerged in an unregulated fashion. Third, the *youth* cohort (the majority in the NMDs) is simultaneously the most integrationist and the most alienated: it demands rights, mobility, and jobs, and it is the segment most receptive both to rights movements (including the proscribed PTM's earlier mobilizations) and, at the margins, to militancy's offers. The 2026 Tirah displacement episode — 33,800 families registered, return windows negotiated for October 2026, and provincial pushback against federal characterizations of the displacement — illustrates the volatility of the politics of return (Countercurrents 2026; EUAA 2026).

## 5.9 Cross-Border Security Management Along the Durand Line

**The fence and the crossings.** Pakistan's border-management program along the KP-facing section of the Durand Line produced: a fence along the great majority of the KP/NMD frontier (largely completed by 2021–23); formal crossing terminals at Torkham, Ghulam Khan and Angoor Adda; and border forts and surveillance systems. The program has documented effects: reduced large-scale infiltration; greater state capacity to detect movement; and increased friction with Afghanistan (whose government rejects the fence and has fired on its construction and on border posts).

**Recurrent closure as policy.** Border closures — including the prolonged closure of Torkham in 2025 over a border-post dispute, and repeated closures during the 2025–26 hostilities — have become a default instrument. Their costs are borne by border communities: loss of trade and labour income, price spikes, stranded patients and students, and separation of kin. Their security benefits are episodic: the CRSS data showed a measurable decline in attacks after the October 2025 closure, followed by resumption (CRSS 2025b). Closures thus function as a *policy of harm to the population to influence the state*, and their repeated use erodes the border communities' stake in the state.

**Assessment.** Border management along the Durand Line requires three elements that Pakistan has not yet assembled: (a) *legality and predictability* — a border regime (permits, trade facilities, movement corridors) that is stable and lawful rather than reactive; (b) *intelligence-led interception* rather than blanket closure; and (c) *diplomatic framework* — a modus vivendi with Kabul on border management, acknowledging that Afghanistan's refusal to recognize the line is a durable condition that must be managed, not wished away (Chapter 9).

## 5.10 Assessment: Has the Merger Contributed to or Complicated Security Normalization?

**Verdict.** The merger has been *constitutionally successful, administratively partial, fiscally failing and—on security—ambiguous*. Six findings support this assessment.

**First, juridical integration is real.** Four million-plus citizens gained the protections (in principle), rights and representation they lacked; the superior courts' jurisdiction is a genuine remedy; and the 23-seat provincial allocation altered KP's politics. The merger's counterfactual — the continuation of the FCR under 2026 conditions — is worse.

**Second, fiscal non-delivery has converted a promise into a grievance.** The Rs 568 billion shortfall is not an accounting detail; it is the difference between an integration narrative and a betrayal narrative in the districts where the insurgency recruits.

**Third, security transition without capability transfer has left the police exposed and unreformed.** The merged districts remain *de facto* military-led, while the police absorb casualties without the means to protect themselves — a combination that satisfies neither doctrine nor legitimacy.

**Fourth, the justice vacuum has preserved the very practices (collective responsibility, informal enforcement) the merger was meant to end.**

**Fifth, the merger has *complicated* the security picture in three specific ways**: (i) it created new expectations and new political actors with claims on the state, precisely as violence resurged; (ii) it transferred administrative responsibility without transferring resources; and (iii) it coincided with — and is sometimes blamed for — the TTP's re-infiltration, even though the causal driver was the Afghan sanctuary (Chapter 9).

**Sixth, and most importantly for policy, the merger remains the correct framework.** The failures are failures of implementation, not of the design principle. The alternative — reversion to exception, or partial reversion under security cover — would repeat the historical error that produced the insurgency.

**Consequent design requirements** (developed in Chapter 15): a statutory, ring-fenced merged districts fund with a mandated floor and audit; completion of district court and prosecution capacity with a reform of informal justice that preserves legitimacy; a staged police-capability transfer with protection-first sequencing; local government with budgets; land and mineral regularization with benefit-sharing; and a public, measurable *Normalization Compact* against which progress is reported annually.
