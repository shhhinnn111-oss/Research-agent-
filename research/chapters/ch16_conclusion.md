# CHAPTER 16 — CONCLUSION: THE THREE MONOPOLIES AND THE POLITICS OF NORMALIZATION

## 16.1 The Argument Restated

**Main thesis.** This study's central claim is that the security situation of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa cannot be normalized by the accumulation of force alone, because what KP lacks is not primarily coercive capacity but **three interlocking monopolies**: the state's monopoly on organized violence, the citizen's enforceable claim on the state (justice and governance), and the province's claim on national resources (fiscal federalism). Each is constitutive of the others. Where violence is unmonopolized, rights are unenforceable; where rights are unenforceable, communities shelter the violent; and where the province's fiscal claim is denied, the institutions that would monopolize violence — police, courts, schools, local government — cannot be built. KP's history is a natural experiment in each failure mode: the tribal areas' century of legal exception (no enforceable claim), the post-2001 military-dominant period (no police-led institutionalization), and the post-2018 merger-without-money (no fiscal claim). The three monopolies model is the study's original analytic contribution, and it organizes its empirical conclusions.

**The model's test against the evidence.**
- **1925–1947:** formal monopoly (British Indian law) coupled with structural exception (FCR, indirect rule) — no citizen's claim in the tribal belt.
- **1947–1979:** inherited exception, minimal state investment, jirga-mediated order, and a stable but shallow equilibrium.
- **1979–2001:** the Afghan wars militarize the frontier and embed the *mujahideen* economy; the Soviet withdrawal is followed by neglect (all three monopolies weak).
- **2001–2014:** the state responds with force; the Taliban insurgency and its suppression kill tens of thousands; every operation is followed by recurrence where state-building does not follow (Chapter 6's seven-criteria analysis shows proximity-to-force, not victory).
- **2014–2021:** the Radd-ul-Fasaad/NISP period combines counter-terrorism, CFT and partial merger; violence declines (2014–2021) — the best evidence for the model is that *decline coincided with the period when the state simultaneously deployed force, began legal integration, and sustained fiscal investment* (CPEC, development packages, the NMD fund's early years).
- **2021–2026:** the Taliban's Kabul victory restores the sanctuary (external variable) and the merger's fiscal failure denies resources (internal variable); violence rises for five consecutive years; and the state answers with another campaign name — Ghazab-lil-Haq (2026).

The pattern is not cyclical by accident. It is cyclical because each cycle addresses one monopoly — usually force — and leaves the other two unrepaired.

## 16.2 Answering the Twelve Research Questions

**1. Drivers.** KP's insecurity is driven by a four-layer causal architecture (Chapter 4): *structural conditions* (frontier geography, cross-border kinship, colonial legal exception, poverty, weak local government, sectarian demography, and the Pakistan–Afghanistan rivalries); *proximate triggers* (the Afghan sanctuary, operation-related displacement and grievances, the 2021 regime change in Kabul, and policy shocks such as the 2024 PTM proscription); *accelerants* (weapons availability, extortion economies, jailbreaks, ISKP's sectarian strategy, social media); and *sustaining mechanisms* (the war economy, impunity, and the recruitment pipeline of unaddressed grievances). The relative weight of these layers has shifted: the *structural* layer has been constant, while the *sanctuary* and *grievance* layers have become the dominant proximate drivers in the 2021–2026 period.

**2. Kinetic efficacy.** Operations have demonstrably reduced militant capacity (Swat 2009, North Waziristan 2014) and killed or displaced thousands of militants, but none has produced self-sustaining security: the seven-criteria analysis (Chapter 6) shows that operations succeed militarily, fade institutionally, and reverse where stabilization and justice do not follow. The gains' decay follows a predictable sequence — clearance → decline → complacency → militant regeneration → recurrence — with the regeneration period shortened when a sanctuary exists (2021–2026). Kinetic action remains necessary (there is no evidence the TTP would decline to attack absent pressure) but is not sufficient, and the sequence's weakest point is the transition from military clearing to police and civilian control.

**3. Merger impact.** The 25th Amendment's constitutional achievement is real; its administrative and fiscal delivery is not. The findings: legal extension succeeded; administrative integration is partial; judicial capacity is minimal; policing remains in transition; and the fiscal transfer — the merger's material promise, the 3 per cent NFC share — is Rs 568 billion short as of 2026. The *security* consequence is direct: the NMDs' weak state presence gives the TTP space; compensation delays generate grievances; and land/water disputes (Kurram, Khyber, Tirah) find violent expression partly because the courts are not there. The counterfactual is not that the merger failed — it is that it has not yet been completed.

**4. Development–security nexus.** The relationship is not linear but *co-productive and conditional* (Chapter 10). Development produces security only where (a) physical security persists (roads/markets/schools must be protected); (b) the gains reach local populations rather than patronage networks; and (c) justice institutions mediate the disputes that growth creates. Evidence from KP: tourism recovery in Swat shows development's dependence on (and contribution to) security; NMD data show that where security is absent, development does not persist. The practical corollary: design development as security infrastructure (protection + staffing + local accountability), and expect the dividend to lag by years, not quarters.

**5. Deradicalization efficacy.** Weak evidence base, modest scale, and no independent evaluation (Chapter 8). Sabawoon's cohort (226 juveniles, ~143 reintegrated, with documented relapses) is a fraction of the demand; Mishal and Sparlay's claims are contested or unverifiable; and the disappearance of Sparlay illustrates programme fragility. But the *instruments* — family-based rehabilitation, religious counter-narrative, vocational reintegration, and community reacceptance — are plausible and internationally supported; the deficiency is in resourcing, evaluation and legal framework, not concept. Deradicalization is *not* an alternative to counter-insurgency: it is the part of normalization that converts the state's coercion into something durable.

**6. Afghanistan factor.** The causal role is established and central (Chapter 9): the TTP's sanctuaries, leadership, training and financing have operated from Afghanistan since 2021, and the 2025–2026 escalation — Doha ceasefire, Istanbul mechanism, the February 2026 bombings, and Ghazab-lil-Haq — demonstrates both the depth of the problem and the weakness of force alone against it. No comprehensive settlement is realistic in the near term; what is realistic is a *verifiable management regime*: monitored commitments, prisoner exchanges, border regimes that facilitate rather than only restrict, and third-party (Chinese/Qatari/Turkish/Saudi) institutionalized mediation. Sanctuary erosion, not a peace treaty, is the achievable near-term goal.

**7. Governance and rule of law.** The KP record identifies four governance factors associated with violence reduction: *continuity of police leadership and reform*; *judicial functionality* (a working court where the conflict was fought); *local government* (the missing tier in the NMDs); and *fiscal delivery* (the material proof of government's presence). Conversely, the strongest correlates of violence escalation are: policy shocks that criminalize a population (the PTM ban), impunity (the absence of convictions), and the suspension of political channels (protest bans). The legal architecture (ATA, 27th Amendment, military courts) matters not only for human rights but for counter-insurgency effectiveness: where the justice system loses legitimacy, community cooperation — the actual source of intelligence — degrades.

**8. Comparative lessons.** Northern Ireland teaches that police reform is load-bearing; Colombia that reintegration without protection produces dissidence; Mindanao that fiscal devolution plus third-party monitoring sustains settlements; Sierra Leone that hybrid justice (truth + selective accountability) can consolidate peace; Sri Lanka that military victory without political settlement leaves normalization unfinished; the KRI that party-fragmented security entrenches instability; Chechnya that coercion can suppress violence while destroying the state's legal foundation and exporting the conflict; and Afghanistan that rented security forces and corrupted governance collapse when the external patron leaves. The transferable core is: political framework + security-sector reform + economic dividend + legitimacy mechanisms, over a decade, with monitoring (Chapter 13).

**9. Civil society and peace.** KP's civil society, media, jirgas, elders, women's organizations, youth movements and religious leaders are simultaneously the state's most valuable peace resource and its most constrained partners (Chapter 12). The most consequential policy fact in this domain is negative: the proscription of a non-violent political movement (the PTM) and the criminalization of journalists removed the legitimate channels through which Pashtun grievance could be expressed, thereby increasing the recruitment environment. Restoration of lawful political space is the cheapest security intervention available to the state.

**10. Futures.** Three futures are set out (Chapter 14): sustained normalization (15–20 per cent), fragile equilibrium (50–60 per cent), and deterioration (25–30 per cent). The distinguishing variables are fiscal delivery, police protection, and political inclusion; the external variable (Afghanistan) can accelerate any path. The early-warning indicators that would distinguish deterioration from simple continuity — sustained TTP taxation, police attrition, border closure, sectarian massacre, ATC collapse — are observable quarterly, and the state currently tracks almost none of them systematically.

**11. Gender.** Gender dynamics are not marginal but structural to normalization (Chapters 8, 10, 12). Female education is simultaneously the strongest long-run indicator of stability in the NMDs and the target of the insurgency's most ideological violence; women's organizations are among the most effective community-level preventers; and women are largely absent from the jirga and security architecture. Any credible normalization agenda must include girls' schooling, women's participation in mediation, female policing, and women-led PVE programming as security measures — not as social add-ons.

**12. Economic cost and dividend.** KP's annual conflict-related economic loss is estimated at Rs 400–800 billion (1.5–3 per cent of provincial output), with cumulative national losses since 2001 in the range of USD 126.79–152 billion (Chapter 10). The incremental cost of the roadmap — roughly Rs 150–250 billion per year at maturity plus the Rs 568 billion arrears — is thus a fraction of what insecurity already costs. Normalization is not a humanitarian expense; it is the highest-return investment available to the federation, and its financing is almost entirely a matter of reallocation and political will.

## 16.3 Theoretical Contribution: Reframing Normalization

Three theoretical contributions emerge.

**(1) Normalization as institutional co-production.** The literature on counter-insurgency and state-building treats security and governance as sequential (clear → hold → build). KP's evidence suggests they are *co-productive*: security without justice (the merged districts' policing) produces hollow control; justice without security (courts in the absence of protection) is a target; and economic development without either is a subsidy to the conflict economy. The policy implication is to design every intervention with three components — protection, adjudication, and resources — in the same geography and timeline.

**(2) The sanctuary-grievance nexus.** The study argues that the sanctuary variable and the grievance variable are not substitutes but complements: a sanctuary supplies capability, while grievance supplies recruitment and information. Counter-insurgency that degrades capability (strikes, operations) without reducing grievance (political exclusion, compensation failures) simply lowers the insurgency's price of reconstitution: this is the mechanism that explains KP's repeated cycles and the 2021–2026 escalation. Conversely, grievance-reduction without sanctuary pressure (e.g., development programs in Swat while TTP leadership sits in Afghanistan) buys quiet but not durability. Only simultaneous action on both variables produces sustainable stability — a proposition that predicts relapse wherever either is neglected (a testable prediction for the 2026–2030 period).

**(3) The three monopolies as a normalization index.** The model can be operationalized as a measurable index (Chapter 15's monitoring framework): *monopoly of violence* (state-share of coercive incidents; absence of parallel taxation/justice), *monopoly of justice* (case throughput, conviction rates, restitution payments, absence of indefinite detention), and *monopoly of resources* (fiscal transfer fulfilment, per-capita spending in the merged districts, local government budgets). The index's value is that it measures what normalization requires, not merely what violence does — and that it can be computed annually from data the state already possesses but does not publish.

## 16.4 Policy Synthesis: Ten Findings and Six Commitments

**Ten findings of the study.**
1. Violence is a five-year rising trend (2021–2026) with the TTP as the dominant actor and the Afghan sanctuary as the dominant enabling factor.
2. Kinetic operations have degraded but never defeated the insurgency; the decay of gains is institutional, not military.
3. The FATA merger's fiscal failure is the largest self-inflicted wound in the province's security policy.
4. Police are the front line and the weakest-protected link: 122 killed in H1 2026 alone, with a 93.7 per cent year-on-year rise.
5. The justice system's throughput failure sustains impunity, which sustains both militancy and grievance.
6. Deradicalization programmes are small, unevaluated and under-resourced relative to the recruitment pipeline.
7. The Afghanistan channel requires management and verification, not maximalism — and management is achievable.
8. The proscription of non-violent politics and the shrinking civic space are counter-insurgency self-harm.
9. Gender-exclusion and the failure to educate girls are structural security deficits, not social policy questions.
10. The economic cost of inaction exceeds the cost of normalization by a factor of two to four.

**Six commitments (the implementation core).**
1. **Pay the money** — legislate the merged-district transfer, clear arrears, publish district-wise delivery.
2. **Protect the police** — fund the protection package, insurance, compensation, and recruitment now.
3. **Staff the courts** — judicial presence in the merged districts, ATC throughput, witness protection.
4. **Elect the councils** — local government in the NMDs with devolved budgets.
5. **Restore lawful politics** — lift the PTM ban, release those without terrorism charges, protect journalists, and negotiate with verified commitment frameworks where possible.
6. **Verify what is done** — an annual, published, independently scrutinized normalization report.

## 16.5 Limitations of the Study

**Data limitations.** This study relies on open sources, with the attendant risks: militant casualty claims are inflated; official incident counts are uncorroborated; CRSS/SATP/ACLED series use different definitions and counts diverge; and some of the most important data — ATC outcomes, NMD fiscal absorption, police postings, operation civilian-harm assessments, deradicalization outcomes — are simply unpublished. Where the record is contested (the 2026 war's casualties; Mishal's numbers; the Sparlay centre's existence), the study reports the contest rather than resolving it. A primary-research program inside the province (household surveys; police and prosecutor interviews; jail-based recidivism studies) would materially improve on what is presented here and is a direct implication of this study for future work.

**Causal-inference limits.** The dynamics analysed are simultaneously determined (violence affects development affects recruitment affects violence), and the study's methods are structured comparisons and process tracing, not identification. The roadmap's expected effects are therefore argued from mechanism and comparative experience, not from a controlled evaluation; it should be treated as a hypothesis-bearing design whose implementation must itself be evaluated.

**Access and security.** The province's most violent districts cannot be safely researched at scale; militant and intelligence perspectives are, by definition, unavailable in verifiable form; and government cooperation is conditional. The study compensates with source triangulation and disaggregated reporting, but no claim to completeness is made.

**Normative position.** The study takes an explicit position: the purpose of security policy is the protection and flourishing of the population, including those who have been displaced, bereaved, abused, or radicalized. This is a human-security framework, adopted consciously over the state-centric alternatives, and it shapes the weight given to civilian harm, compensation, justice and political rights. Readers who prefer a purely coercive framework will find the analysis's conclusion — that coercion without justice and resources fails — unwelcome; but the evidence, particularly the cyclical record of Chapters 2 and 6, is difficult to read otherwise.

## 16.6 Directions for Future Research

**(1) Primary data collection.** A provincial security observatory with verified incident data; independent casualty monitoring; police and prosecution administrative data; and jail-based recidivism studies. **(2) Fiscal absorption research.** District-level tracking of NMD transfers from award to expenditure — the single largest evidentiary gap in the merger literature. **(3) Deradicalization evaluation.** A properly designed five-year study (with a control/comparison group) of reintegration outcomes, family acceptance and relapse — the field's most pressing knowledge need. **(4) Political economy of violence.** Research on the war economy (extortion, mining, smuggling, kidnap) and its interdependencies with elites, including the capture of the NMD fund. **(5) Sub-national climate-security research.** GLOF/displacement and conflict interactions in Chitral, Dir, Swat and the southern districts. **(6) Comparative follow-up.** A structured comparison of the Pakistan–Afghanistan borderlands with Myanmar's borderlands and the Sahel — three contemporary cases of sanctuary-driven insurgency with weak peripheral fiscal settlement. **(7) Gender-disaggregated conflict research.** The recruitment of women and girls; women's roles in peace networks; and the security economics of girls' education in the merged districts.

## 16.7 Closing Observations

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa presents the paradox of a province that has borne the highest costs of Pakistan's security policies while being granted the least control over them, and that has, despite everything, retained an enormous reservoir of resilience: declining levels of public support for militancy; communities that fought back; police that kept reporting to their posts after 680 attacks in nine months; parents who continued sending their daughters to school; an economy whose tourism sector recovered against all expectation; and a diaspora that sends home both resources and ideas.

The province's security will not be normalized by finding the right operation or the right general. It will be normalized — if at all — by the accumulation of unglamorous, verifiable acts of statehood: paying teachers in Bajaur; staffing a court in Landi Kotal; replacing a constable's damaged vehicle in Lakki Marwat; restoring a compensation payment in Tirah; registering a madrasa without treating its neighbourhood as an enemy; opening a legal route to protest in Waziristan; and publishing, every year, the record of what was promised and what was done.

The study's final judgement is a conditional one. On present trends (Scenario 2, with deterioration risks), KP's future is more of the past: a decade of episodic campaigns, cumulative displacement, a police force bled white, a missed generation, and a province asked to defend the nation while being denied the resources to govern itself. On the alternative path (Scenario 1), the province's three monopolies are built — violence is constrained by professional forces under law, the citizen has enforceable claims in functioning courts and elected councils, and the province receives what the constitution and the federation's own promises commit to it — and KP becomes what it has the potential to be: a gateway economy on the edge of Central Asia, with a young population, an old culture, and a hard-won peace.

The choice between these futures does not rest with the Taliban, or the TTP, or India, or the United States. The binding constraints are domestic: the willingness to fund, to protect, to adjudicate, to include, and to be measured. That is the study's central finding, and its central hope.
