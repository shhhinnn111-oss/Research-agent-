## APPENDIX C — MILITANT AND ARMED GROUP PROFILES

**Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP).** Ideology: Deobandi-jihadist, Pakistani-state-focused; umbrella coalition incorporating 70+ factions since 2018. Leadership: Noor Wali Mehsud (emir); shura-based; Federation of 2020. Area: Afghanistan-based leadership; operational networks across KP/NMDs (Bannu, Lakki Marwat, N/S Waziristan, Khyber, Bajaur, Kurram) and occasional Punjab/Sindh operations. Tactics: suicide/IED/VBIED, drones, sniper/ambush, targeted killings, extortion and taxation. Strength: several thousand fighters (estimates vary; the 2024 claim of 22,000–24,000 in Afghanistan and 16,000–18,000 inside Pakistan is the upper bound of political estimates and should be treated as such). Financing: extortion, "taxation," mining, smuggling, kidnapping, donations. Status: proscribed; the group's own annual claims are propaganda and are attributed as such.

**Hafiz Gul Bahadur Group (HGB).** North Waziristan faction with separate command; historically a state-negotiated militia that turned against the state after 2017; operates in North Waziristan and border districts; significant in cross-border dynamics.

**Islamic State–Khorasan Province (ISKP).** Salafi-jihadist; anti-Shia, anti-Sufi, anti-TTP; estimated strength ~3,000 in Afghanistan; targets soft/sectarian targets (imambargahs, Sufi shrines, minorities) and recruits from the borderlands; competition with the TTP (including a March 2026 clash in Orakzai).

**Ittehad-ul-Mujahideen Pakistan (IMP).** New front organization implicated in the Kohat Police Lines attack (September 2026); represents the ongoing proliferation of TTP-aligned fronts.

**Jamaat-ul-Ahrar (JuA).** Factional split from the TTP (announced July 2026), with a history of mass-casualty sectarian attacks; its separation illustrates coalition fragmentation as an operational event and a potential future negotiation partner.

**Lashkar-e-Islam (LI)** and **Ansarul Islam (Mangal Bagh networks)**: historical Khyber/Tirah groups, degraded but occasionally resurgent locally.

**Sectarian organizations (LeJ, SSP variants).** Anti-Shia networks with recurring involvement in Kurram and other sectarian incidents; overlapping personnel with the TTP.

**Baloch insurgent networks (BLA and others).** Distinct theatre (Balochistan) with episodic linkage to KP violence (Q3 2026: Balochistan overtook KP in fatalities). Included for completeness of the threat picture; not a KP-based actor.

*Assessment note.* The proliferation of front organizations is analytically important: it allows the coalition to claim deniability, complicates proscription and negotiation, and enables each faction to pursue divergent operations (including potentially the JuA's sectarian specialization).

## APPENDIX E — KEY POLICY DOCUMENTS AND LEGAL INSTRUMENTS

1. **Frontier Crimes Regulation 1901** — the legal architecture of FATA's exception; repealed for the merged districts in 2018.
2. **Nizam-e-Adl Regulation 2009** — sharia-based legal arrangement in Malakand Division; validated and contested.
3. **National Action Plan (NAP), 2014; revised 2021** — 20 points covering militancy, executions, madrasas, financing, refugees, communication, and FATA reform.
4. **National Internal Security Policy 2022–2026 (NISP)** — the framework document for internal security.
5. **Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (as amended)** — ATCs, proscription, preventive measures; the primary counter-terrorism statute.
6. **NACTA Act 2013** — the coordination authority's mandate (and limits).
7. **Pakistan Protection Ordinance/Act 2013** — preventive detention framework (expired).
8. **KP Police Act 2017** — provincial policing reform statute (role, tenure, accountability, PSCs).
9. **KP Levies and Khasadar legislation, 2019** — integration of tribal forces into the provincial police system.
10. **Constitution (Twenty-Fifth Amendment) Act 2018** — FATA merger and provincial seat adjustment.
11. **Constitution (Twenty-Seventh Amendment) Act 2025** — Federal Constitutional Court, Chief of Defence Forces coordination, and judicial restructuring.
12. **Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act 2016 (as amended 2025)** — cyber-speech offences (repeatedly criticized as over-broad).
13. **Fair Trial Act 2013** — surveillance warrants framework.
14. **National Finance Commission Award framework (including the 3% merged-districts commitment)** — the fiscal instrument central to the merger's delivery.
15. **KP Youth Policy (draft, 2026)** — provincial youth and employment framework.
16. **Madrasa registration framework (2019) and its October 2024 statutory reversal** — the regulatory arc for religious education.
17. **Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan (2023–2026)** — the refugee policy instrument.
18. **Paigham-e-Pakistan (2018)** — the state-sponsored religious counter-narrative declaration.
19. **Bangsamoro Organic Law (Philippines, 2018) and Malaysia-facilitated agreements** — comparative reference.
20. **Good Friday Agreement (1998) and Patten Report (1999)** — comparative reference.

## APPENDIX H — DISTRICT SECURITY CLASSIFICATION (2026) AND THEMATIC SECURITY MAP (DESCRIBED)

The study uses a four-tier classification (Chapter 3), with confidence levels, and a described map rather than a rendered cartographic product (the study's rendering environment cannot produce a geographically accurate basemap; the classification below is therefore the operative "map" and should be used in its place).

**Tier 1 — Severe, sustained insurgent violence (highest confidence).**
*North Waziristan, South Waziristan (Upper and Lower), Bannu, Lakki Marwat.* Continuous TTP operations; attacks on security forces at high frequency; taxation/parallel-governance reports; the highest police and military casualty rates. These districts form the southern insurgency core. Confidence: high (multiple independent monitors).

**Tier 2 — High and episodic violence (high confidence).**
*Khyber (incl. Tirah), Bajaur, Mohmand, Kurram, Dera Ismail Khan, Tank, Karak, Hangu, Orakzai.* Recurring attacks, displacement episodes (Tirah; Sarbakaf), sectarian flashpoints (Kurram), border-related violence. Confidence: high.

**Tier 3 — Sporadic/urban violence with high-impact events (moderate confidence).**
*Peshawar, Mardan, Charsadda, Nowshera, Swat, Shangla, Malakand/ Dir cluster.* Lower frequency but high-impact urban/soft-target attacks (Kabal, Kohat is Tier 2-adjacent; Peshawar attacks including the 2023 Police Lines bombing). Swat's re-entry to violence in August 2026 is the most significant change in this tier.

**Tier 4 — Low recorded violence but strategic exposure (moderate confidence).**
*Abbottabad, Mansehra, Haripur, Chitral, Upper/Lower Dir and the Hazara districts* — strategic infrastructure, tourism routes, CPEC corridors, and Chinese-national exposure; lower recorded incidents but potential for high-value targeting and for cross-border spillover (Chitral, Dir).

**Note on the map.** A geographically accurate security map would show: (i) the southern cluster (Waziristan–Bannu–Lakki–D.I. Khan–Tank) as the highest-intensity zone, contiguous with the Afghanistan border; (ii) a western border arc (Bajaur–Mohmand–Khyber–Kurram–Orakzai) of medium-high intensity and high displacement; (iii) the urban quadrilateral (Peshawar–Mardan–Charsadda–Nowshera) as the terrorism-target belt; (iv) the northern/northeastern districts as lower intensity but high strategic exposure; and (v) the Afghanistan borderland as the enabling geography for the entire pattern (Chapter 9). This description should be used as the study's district map of record in the absence of a rendered cartographic figure.
