# CHAPTER 6 — MILITARY OPERATIONS AND COUNTER-TERRORISM CAMPAIGNS

## 6.1 Overview of Major Military Operations in KPK and FATA (Comprehensive Timeline)

Pakistan's counter-insurgency in KP and the tribal areas has been conducted through an unusually large number of named operations — a fact of analytical significance in itself, since each name implies a distinct campaign and each has often been followed by another within a few years. The following timeline distinguishes *major manoeuvre operations* (division-scale, sustained, displacing populations) from *sector operations* (brigade/battalion-scale, district-specific) and from *standing campaigns* (national frameworks under which operations and intelligence-based raids continue).

| Operation | Period | Area | Stated objective | Displacement (est.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Al Mizan | 2002–2006 | Waziristan | Clear foreign fighters; dismantle al-Qaeda infrastructure | Localized |
| Zalzala | 2008 | South Waziristan (Mehsud areas) | Pressurize Baitullah Mehsud | Tens of thousands |
| Sherdil | Aug 2008 onward | Bajaur | Break militant control of Bajaur | ~200,000–300,000 |
| Rah-e-Haq I & II | Nov 2007; 2008 | Swat/Malakand | Clear TNSM/TTP presence | Hundreds of thousands |
| Rah-e-Rast | May–Jun 2009 | Swat, Buner, Dir, Shangla | Defeat insurgency; restore state authority | 2–2.5 million |
| Rah-e-Nijat | Oct–Dec 2009 | South Waziristan | Destroy TTP's main base | ~300,000+ |
| Brekhna | 2011–12 | Mohmand | Clear border sanctuaries | Tens of thousands |
| Khyber I–III | 2014–15 | Khyber (Bara, Tirah) | Clear LEI/TTP infrastructure | Hundreds of thousands |
| Zarb-e-Azb | Jun 2014–Jul 2017 | North Waziristan (later others) | Eliminate militant sanctuaries across FATA | ~1 million |
| Khyber IV | Jul–Aug 2017 | Rajgal Valley (Khyber) | Seal high-altitude crossing routes | Localized |
| Radd-ul-Fasaad | Feb 2017–present | Nationwide | Consolidate; dismantle residual networks | Localized |
| Azm-e-Istehkam | Jun 2024–present | Nationwide (KP/Balochistan focus) | "Reinvigorated" CT campaign; kinetic + diplomatic | Anti-displacement framing |
| Sarbakaf | Jul–Aug 2025 | Bajaur (Lowi Mamund) | Clear re-established TTP presence | ~100,000 (relief agencies) |
| Ghazab-lil-Haq | Feb 2026–present | Afghanistan (cross-border) | Punish/coerce Afghan Taliban over TTP sanctuary | Cross-border refugee flows |

Sources: ISPR statements as reported (Dawn, The News, Tribune 2008–2026); Wikipedia operational summaries (2026a, 2026d); Paradigm Shift 2025; CRSS 2025b; UN/OCHA displacement figures as cited.

**Analytical observation.** The timeline reveals a repeating structural pattern: operations are launched when violence becomes politically intolerable; they achieve temporary clearing; the state consolidates with *some* development spending; violence returns within 4–8 years; and a new operation is named. The pattern is not evidence that force fails, but that force *alone* is a depreciating asset.

## 6.2 Operation Rah-e-Haq (Swat, 2007–2009): Objectives, Execution and Outcomes

**Context.** The Swat insurgency developed from the TNSM's moral-reform campaign (2007) into an armed movement controlling much of the valley by 2008, with Maulana Fazlullah's FM broadcasts, attacks on barber shops and music shops, the destruction or closure of hundreds of schools (with a documented campaign against girls' education), beheadings of opponents, and attacks on police. Two earlier operations (Rah-e-Haq I in November 2007 and a follow-on in 2008) failed to restore state control: the forces were too few, the political will fluctuated, and each operation was followed by negotiation — including the February 2009 Nizam-e-Adl Regulation, which conceded Sharia-based adjudication in Malakand in return for peace, and collapsed within weeks as militants expanded into Buner and Dir.

**Significance.** Rah-e-Haq's failure is instructive for three reasons. First, it demonstrated that *partial operations followed by concessions* signal weakness and accelerate mobilization — the militants' expansion after the February 2009 deal was rapid. Second, it established the pattern of "operation–peace deal–collapse" that recurs in KP's history (Chapter 2). Third, it created the political conditions — the flight of 2 million people, the Buner crisis, the collapse of public tolerance for the TTP — that made a decisive operation possible in May 2009.

## 6.3 Operation Rah-e-Rast (Swat, 2009): The Liberation of Swat Valley

**Execution.** Launched in early May 2009 after the collapse of the Nizam-e-Adl arrangement, Rah-e-Rast employed approximately 20,000+ troops (with FC and police support) using artillery, helicopter gunships and airstrikes; the operation cleared Mingora (the valley's capital) within two weeks and continued for months into the valley's periphery and adjoining districts. Approximately 2–2.5 million people fled — the largest displacement in Pakistan since 1947 — into camps in Mardan, Swabi, Charsadda and Peshawar, and into host communities.

**Outcomes (assessed).** *Military*: the operation broke the TNSM/TTP's territorial control; Swat's insurgency as an organized force was defeated, and many commanders fled to Afghanistan (Kunar/Nuristan), where they remained. *Political*: the operation was accompanied by a consensus parliamentary resolution and was broadly popular in KP — a critical legitimacy asset that enabled subsequent operations. *Human cost*: hundreds of civilians were killed; displacement produced a humanitarian crisis; and there were credible reports of extrajudicial killings and disappearances. *Reconstruction*: the Malakand recovery, though slow at first, eventually produced rebuilt bazaars, roads, and a remarkable restoration of tourism (Chapter 10), and the "Swat model" became internationally studied as a case of counter-insurgency that combined force with governance, albeit imperfectly.

**Assessment against doctrine.** Rah-e-Rast came closest of any Pakistani operation to population-centric counter-insurgency (Kilcullen 2009; the Walden case study of the campaign finds that governance was deliberately integrated with military action). Its weaknesses were in the *sequencing* of the post-conflict phase: compensation and reconstruction lagged by years; the displaced returned to destroyed infrastructure; and the state did not build institutions capable of preventing re-infiltration across the thirteen years that followed — a failure made manifest in August 2026, when a TTP suicide bomber killed 17 people at a police station in Swat's Kabal area and militants re-entered the valley (Chapter 3).

## 6.4 Operation Rah-e-Nijat (South Waziristan, 2009): Strategy and Impact

**Execution.** Following the Swat operation, the army turned to South Waziristan, launching Rah-e-Nijat in mid-October 2009 with roughly 28,000 troops against the Mehsud heartland, supported by FC and air power. The operation dislodged the TTP's central leadership structure from its base area (Baitullah Mehsud had been killed in August 2009; Hakimullah Mehsud relocated), destroyed infrastructure, and displaced several hundred thousand people (Bannu, D.I. Khan and Tank hosting most).

**Impact and limits.** The military achieved clearing but not holding, in the doctrinal sense: TTP units dispersed into North Waziristan, Orakzai, Kurram, Khyber and Afghanistan; and the pattern of "clear, withdraw, re-infiltrate" was set. The operation also began the construction of the "military-first" model in the tribal areas: FC and army presence in populated areas; curfews and checkpoints; and a dependent local economy — a model whose legitimacy costs would compound over time (ICG 2026).

## 6.5 Operation Zarb-e-Azb (North Waziristan, 2014): The Game-Changer?

**Execution.** Launched in June 2014 after the collapse of the peace process with the TTP and the Karachi airport attack, Zarb-e-Azb targeted North Waziristan's sanctuaries — including the Haqqani network's space (a point of significant US–Pakistan friction, since the operation had been demanded for years and its timing was partly a response to US pressure and to the collapse of negotiations) and those of the TTP and foreign fighters. The operation was notable for: pre-operation warnings to civilians; an announced displacement and de-radicalization plan; sustained duration (formally concluded in July 2017 but effectively continuous); and the scale of forces committed.

**Outcomes.** By official accounts, more than 3,500 militants were killed and hundreds of hideouts destroyed; approximately one million people were displaced (many to Bannu's camps); and the TTP's ability to organize mass-casualty attacks from North Waziristan was substantially degraded. Violence across Pakistan fell markedly in 2015–2018, with 2018–2020 producing the lowest terrorism tolls in over a decade, coinciding with the National Action Plan's instruments (military courts, intelligence operations, counter-financing) and Radd-ul-Fasaad. Militarily, Zarb-e-Azb plus Khyber I–IV was the most consequential campaign in Pakistan's counter-insurgency history.

**Limits and critiques.** Five are documented. First, the operation *pushed* rather than *captured* much of the adversary: many TTP and allied fighters crossed into Afghanistan (Kunar, Nangarhar, Khost), where a sanctuary was available, a fact of decisive importance after 2021 (Chapter 9). Second, *the presence of the Haqqani network* was not decisively addressed, sustaining the argument that Pakistan's counter-terrorism remained selective. Third, *displacement and reconstruction* were poorly sequenced: TDP returns began in 2015 but housing and compensation continued for years; many never fully recovered. Fourth, *the de-radicalization provision* (the promised centres) was never implemented at scale. Fifth, *the operation had no political component*: no negotiations, no political settlement, no institution-building plan for the districts after clearance — precisely the gap Galula's theory identifies as fatal to counter-insurgency.

**Was it a game-changer?** For a period of roughly six years (2015–2021), yes: Pakistan experienced its lowest violence in a decade. But the sustainability test — do the gains hold when pressure is released? — was failed: within four years of the American withdrawal, Pakistan had returned to 2015-level violence, with KP absorbing the majority of it. Zarb-e-Azb changed the *scale* of the threat, not its *structure*.

## 6.6 Operation Radd-ul-Fasaad (2017–Present): Consolidation and Nationwide Approach

**Design.** Launched on 22 February 2017, Radd-ul-Fasaad ("elimination of discord") was announced as a countrywide operation to consolidate Zarb-e-Azb's gains and eliminate the residual terrorist infrastructure, with an explicit "whole-of-nation" framing: military operations, intelligence-based operations, police and Rangers actions, counter-financing, and a call for public cooperation. Unlike previous campaigns, Radd-ul-Fasaad was not a manoeuvre operation with a defined area and timeline but a standing framework under which operations and IBOs continue.

**Outcomes and critiques.** The framework's first years coincided with declining violence. However, from 2021 onward the campaign's limits became visible: it had no capacity to address the sanctuary in Afghanistan, no political mechanism for the FATA merger's fiscal delivery, and no answer to the provincial-federal estrangement that followed the 2022 political crisis. Independent assessments conclude that Radd-ul-Fasaad's document-based "whole-of-nation" design was implemented as a *security-forces-first* program, with negligible investment in the soft components (rehabilitation, education, political inclusion) — the mirror image of its design intention.

## 6.7 Intelligence-Based Operations (IBOs): Precision Strikes and Targeted Operations

**What they are.** IBOs are small-unit, intelligence-driven raids and strikes against specific individuals, cells and infrastructure, conducted by the military (including special forces and aviation), the FC, and provincial CTDs, often in coordination. Since 2017 they have been the dominant modality of counter-terrorism, and in 2025–26 they constitute the bulk of operational reporting: dozens of engagements per month across the NMDs and southern districts, killing hundreds of militants annually (CRSS data record 392 security operations in 2025 killing 2,060 "outlaws" — the highest since 2015, and 80 operations in Q2 2026 alone killing 453).

**Strengths.** IBOs: (a) minimize displacement relative to manoeuvre operations; (b) exploit human and technical intelligence; (c) generate a continuous attrition pressure against commanders; and (d) are politically less costly than announced operations.

**Weaknesses.** (a) *Attrition without endgame*: as 2026 data confirm, killing hundreds of militants per quarter has not reduced attack frequency; the CTD recorded 137 attacks in 2025 despite 320 retaliatory operations and a 50 per cent increase in registered cases; and the third quarter of 2026 saw militant fatalities rise 103 per cent while civilian and security-force deaths rose as well (CRSS 2026c). (b) *Human rights risk*: IBOs conducted with incomplete verification of identity and without judicial process have been associated with civilian casualties, including airstrikes (notably the reported 22 September 2025 Tirah Valley airstrike that killed 30+ civilians, including women and children, and the 13 August 2025 Bajaur mortar incident that killed a woman and her two children). (c) *Local intelligence degradation*: each civilian-harm incident reduces the flow of local intelligence, which IBOs depend upon — a self-limiting dynamic. (d) *Recruitment*: killed militants' funerals and grievances become recruitment events, especially where compensation is absent.

**Assessment.** IBOs are a necessary instrument, not a strategy. Their effectiveness is conditional on (i) high-fidelity intelligence; (ii) avoidance of civilian harm (both for ethics and for effectiveness); (iii) follow-through in investigation/prosecution; and (iv) political accompaniment. Pakistan's IBO record in 2025–26 satisfies (i) partially, (ii) unevenly, (iii) weakly and (iv) not at all.

## 6.8 Assessment of Military Effectiveness: Kinetic Success versus Political and Social Outcomes

**A seven-criteria framework.** The study assesses operations against criteria drawn from counter-insurgency theory and evaluation practice: (1) *Violence reduction* (immediate); (2) *Durability* (measured after 3–5 years); (3) *Territorial control transfer* (to civilian/police authority); (4) *Displacement and recovery*; (5) *Civilian harm and legitimacy*; (6) *Political settlement* (a settlement that addresses the conflict's participants); (7) *Institutional legacy* (security, justice, governance capacity).

| Operation | Violence ↓ | Durability | Transfer | Recovery | Legitimacy | Political settlement | Institutional legacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sherdil (2008) | Partial | Low | No | Poor | Mixed | No | Limited |
| Rah-e-Rast (2009) | High | Medium (13 yrs) | Partial | Medium–high (Swat's recovery) | Medium | No | Some (police, tourism) |
| Rah-e-Nijat (2009) | Medium | Low | Low | Poor | Low–medium | No | Limited |
| Khyber I–III (2014–15) | High | Low–medium | Low | Poor | Medium | No | Limited |
| Zarb-e-Azb (2014–17) | High | Medium (6 yrs) | Partial | Medium | Medium | No | Limited–medium |
| Radd-ul-Fasaad (2017–) | Medium | Low | Partial | n/a | Medium | No | Limited |
| Sarbakaf (2025) | Local | Not yet assessable | Low | Poor | Low (protests) | No | Negative (grievance) |
| Azm-e-Istehkam (2024–) | Mixed | Not yet assessable | Low | n/a | Low (KP opposition) | No | Unknown |
| Ghazab-lil-Haq (2026–) | Negative (retaliation) | n/a | n/a | n/a | Contested | No | Interstate escalation |

**Findings.** First, *the correlation between immediate violence reduction and durability is weak*: the operations with the largest immediate effects (Zarb-e-Azb; Rah-e-Nijat) decayed within a half-decade; the one with the best durability (Rah-e-Rast) benefited from an accompanying governance effort and was anyway overtaken by cross-border re-infiltration. Second, *no operation produced a political settlement* — a striking uniformity that reflects the absence of a negotiating framework with the insurgency that is compatible with the state's constitutional order. Third, *civilian harm and legitimacy were inversely related to outcome quality*. Fourth, *no operation transferred security primacy to a capable civilian institution*, meaning that military gains had no custodian.

**Doctrinal reading.** Against Galula: Pakistan's campaigns inverted the political-military ratio, treating politics as an announcement rather than a process. Against Kilcullen: the "accidental guerrilla" mechanism — operations converting neutrals into enemies — is visible in the Kurram convy attack aftermath, Sarbakaf's protests, and the post-2018 deterioration in local intelligence in parts of the NMDs. Against Trinquier: Pakistan has repeatedly practiced a "warfare of reprisal" (collective fines, curfews, demolitions) whose costs fall on the civilians the campaign is meant to protect.

## 6.9 Civilian Casualties, Human Rights Concerns and Accountability

**Data problem.** There is no independent, systematic casualty-tracking mechanism for state operations in KP. Available estimates are partial and contested, including: (a) the *South Asia Terrorism Portal*'s civilian fatality counts for terrorism (not state operations); (b) *Amnesty International*'s statements (June 2025) criticizing rising drone strikes in KP and the failure to protect civilians; (c) *UNAMA*'s Afghanistan-side counts for cross-border strikes (764 civilian casualties between 26 January and 31 March 2026; more than 1,100 civilian deaths/injuries by September 2026, including at least 10 killed where most were children in the 1 October 2026 Kunar/Helmand strikes); (d) *Human Rights Watch, Amnesty and HRCP* documentation of enforced disappearances, extrajudicial killings, and restrictions on civil society in KP, including PTM-affiliated activists held without charge in 2025–26; (e) specific incidents documented by local and international media: the 13 August 2025 Bajaur mortar strike (three civilians killed), the 22 September 2025 Tirah airstrike (30+ civilians reported killed), the killing of civilians in the Orakzai/Arandu operations, and allegations surrounding several IBOs. The *absence* of an official casualty registry is itself a policy finding: accountability mechanisms cannot function without data.

**Legal and normative issues raised by documented practice.** These include: collective punishment (fines on villages; "40 FCR" style measures) contrary to international humanitarian law and Pakistan's own constitutional guarantees post-merger; demolitions of homes and businesses (the November 2025 Surkhab camp demolitions in Balochistan illustrate the practice); prolonged detention without charge; trial of civilians by military courts (the 21st Amendment's military courts and the continuing use of FGCMs for protest-related offences, including against KP residents for the May 2023 episodes, with documented complaints that defendants received no charge sheets or verdict documents and were convicted after appeal deadlines — matters litigated in the Peshawar High Court in 2025); and restrictions on movement and protest. The 27th Amendment (November 2025) further insulated military decision-making from judicial scrutiny (Chapter 11), which will make independent accountability harder.

**The strategic cost of unaccountability.** Counter-insurgency's most valuable non-lethal currency is intelligence from the population. Accountability failures — and their perception — are therefore not merely normative lapses; they are *operational losses*. This is the empirical content of the ICG's finding that KP residents "reject jihadism but resent the military's heavy-handed tactics" (ICG 2026).

## 6.10 The Civil–Military Relations Dynamic in Counter-Terrorism

**The division of labour (de facto).** The military (Army and FC) leads internal-security strategy in the provinces most affected — the National Security Committee, the Army GHQ, corps commands, and the ISPR set policy and conduct operations; the provinces fund police; and the police implement static protection, investigations and casualties. ICG's 2026 report documents three pathologies: (i) *coordination deficits* between military operations and police, including intelligence-sharing gaps that cost lives; (ii) *political externalities*: provincial governments, particularly after 2022, have opposed operations (Azm-e-Istehkam; Sarbakaf), creating a public rift while the police absorb the risk; and (iii) *narrative competition*: federal/military communiqués frame the problem as externally sponsored militancy requiring more force, while provincial statements frame it as the failure of federal policy and the withholding of resources (Wikipedia 2026e; Dawn 2024a; PARADIGM Shift 2025).

**Constitutional dimension.** The 27th Amendment (passed 11–13 November 2025) formalized a stronger executive-military role: the Chief of Army Staff became Chief of Defence Forces with command over all services, a new National Strategic Command was created, and the top military leadership received lifelong immunity from prosecution, while the Supreme Court's constitutional jurisdiction was transferred to a new executive-appointed Federal Constitutional Court and the Judicial Commission of Pakistan was restructured to give the executive a decisive role (Al Jazeera 2025b; Himal Southasian 2025; TFT 2025). For counter-terrorism governance, the immediate consequences are: (a) reduced judicial oversight of security policy and of military courts; (b) fewer institutional channels for provincial or parliamentary input; and (c) greater reliance on *internal* military accountability mechanisms for operational conduct.

**Assessment.** Pakistan's counter-terrorism model conflates *command* with *accountability*. A defensible alternative — used by many counter-insurgency campaigns, including in contexts of federalism — is a *joint, published, accountable framework*: a provincial-federal security committee with a legislative mandate, civilian primacy over policing and investigations, military primacy at the border and in manoeuvre, fused intelligence, and public annual reporting (Chapters 7, 15). The current model produces neither civilian ownership nor military accountability, and its legitimacy cost grows with each mass-casualty attack on the police.

## 6.11 Post-Operation Stabilization: Successes and Failures

**Successes.** (i) *Swat/Malakand recovery*: infrastructure rebuilding (roads, the Swat Expressway), the restoration of tourism, and the return of schools — the single clearest instance of post-operation normalization in Pakistan. (ii) *Market and transport recovery* in parts of Bajaur, Mohmand and Kurram (interrupted repeatedly by violence). (iii) *Police expansion* with new posts and facilities, the Elite Force and Rapid Response Force, and the Safe City project (Chapter 7). (iv) *Return and rehabilitation programs* in the NMDs (TDP returns from 2015; AIP projects; Sehat Card coverage where funded). (v) *Economic reconnection*: the Peshawar–Torkham corridor's eventual reopening, and CPEC-related infrastructure (Rashakai SEZ).

**Failures.** (i) *Compensation*: documented delays in payments to the displaced and injured across multiple episodes (Tirah's return package of Rs 10m/killed and Rs 2.5m/injured was reported delayed into 2026; earlier programs were similarly slow). (ii) *De-radicalization at scale*: promised centres and programs were never resourced adequately (Chapter 8). (iii) *Justice and prosecution*: no post-operation justice process; few prosecutions relative to detentions or to incidents. (iv) *Local governance*: local government in the NMDs remains unimplemented in any meaningful fiscal sense. (v) *Institutionalization*: no transition of cleared areas to effective civilian policing; FC/army presence persists indefinitely. (vi) *Political settlement*: none attempted, beyond episodic deals with individual commanders and tribal jirgas.

**The stabilization gap.** The recurring failure is not the absence of programs but their *architecture*: Pakistan's model finances development through federal programs administered provincially, with no mechanism tying infrastructure and services to the security transition or to local political accountability. International practice suggests a different model: a single stabilization agency with a defined mandate, budget and reporting line; sequenced security-transfer milestones; and community-level accountability (Chapter 13).

## 6.12 The Question of Sustainability: Can Military Gains Hold Without Political Solutions?

**The empirical answer from KP's own record** is contained in the operations table above: in every case, gains decayed. The mechanism is now well understood analytically: military operations (a) destroy or displace armed structures but (b) do not resolve the recruitment pool, the sanctuary, the war economy, the justice vacuum, or the political grievance; and (c) they create new grievances through displacement, harm and coercion. When external pressure (ISAF/US presence in Afghanistan; Pakistani force concentrations in FATA) that constrained militancy is removed, the surviving structures regenerate.

**Five conditions for sustainability**, derived from KP's comparative record:
1. **Denial of sanctuary or a durable cross-border modus vivendi.** Without it, operations in a territorial corner of a networked insurgency merely relocate the conflict.
2. **Transfer of security responsibility to a capable, protected, professionally accountable civilian force**, with a realistic timeline and resourcing (the police problem).
3. **A justice dividend** — visible prosecutions of those who commit atrocities, and relief for those wrongly detained — as the most credible demonstration that the state has ended the conflict.
4. **A fiscal and political settlement with the periphery** (the merger's completion; local government; land and mineral regularization) that changes the terms of the state's relationship with the border belt.
5. **A peace constituency strategy**: education, employment, and protection of the moderates, women, and community leaders who form the social base of normalization.

**The unanswered question.** Can a military-led campaign *deliver* these conditions? The comparative evidence (Chapters 13) suggests that militaries can deliver conditions 1 and partly 2, but that conditions 3–5 require civilian institutions that KP currently lacks and that the 27th Amendment's centralization of authority may further weaken. The strategic implication is uncomfortable but unavoidable: **without a political process, kinetic operations will continue to produce temporary suppression at rising human and financial cost** — which is precisely the pattern the 2026 data record, with the highest quarterly militant fatality count (921) coexisting with rising civilian and security-force losses.
