# CHAPTER 14 — SCENARIOS, PROJECTIONS AND RISK ASSESSMENT

## Methodological Note on Scenario Construction

The scenario method used here is *driver-based* rather than forecasting: rather than extrapolating trend lines, the analysis identifies the four variables with the greatest demonstrated causal weight on KP's security trajectory (established in Chapters 3–11) — (i) the Afghanistan security channel; (ii) the federal–provincial security compact; (iii) the merged districts' fiscal settlement and governance build-out; and (iv) the police protection and capability trajectory — and constructs alternative futures from their interactions. Probabilities are *subjective assessments* derived from historical base rates (KP's record of relapse; the failure rate of ceasefires; the repeat of operation cycles) and from current trend evidence (2026 data). They are offered to discipline thinking, not to claim precision.

## 14.1 Scenario 1: Optimistic Trajectory — Sustained Normalization and Peace Consolidation

**Probability: 15–20 per cent over 2026–2035 (under current leadership and resource conditions).**

**Narrative.** In this scenario, a conjunction of three favourable developments occurs within 24–36 months. First, the Pakistan–Afghanistan channel stabilizes: a mediated arrangement (Chinese/Qatari/Turkish/Saudi facilitation) establishes a monitoring mechanism with reciprocal commitments — TTP relocations away from the border, verified curbs on cross-border movement, prisoner exchanges, and trade/mobility normalization (Chapters 9, 15). The TTP's sanctuary dividend erodes gradually, and its operational tempo declines as leadership losses (already significant: 921 militants killed in Q3 2026 alone) accumulate without replacement from safe rear areas. Second, the fiscal settlement of the merged districts is delivered: following the Prime Minister's June 2026 assurance, the NFC revision process (or a presidential order under Article 160) creates a statutory, ring-fenced transfer of the promised 3 per cent (or an equivalent quantified package), and the 2028 expiry of the original commitment is pre-empted by a successor framework. The merged districts immediately see payrolls funded, ~6,000 promised police posts created, courts staffed, and road/health/school projects resumed. Third, the federal–provincial compact is repaired through a statutory coordination framework and a security-financing arrangement that eschews political blame games; the police receive protection equipment, counter-drone and counter-IED capability, and the casualty rate declines from the 2026 peak.

**Outcome indicators by 2030.** KP violence-linked fatalities fall below 500/year (vs 2,331 in 2025); police killed fall to double digits annually; attacks on the NMDs decline 60 per cent-plus; tourism exceeds 20 million provincial visitors; Rashakai and the NMDs' mineral sector generate visible employment; the merged districts' fiscal absorption reaches 80 per cent of allocated funds; and a functioning district judiciary is reported in all seven NMDs.

**Requisites (each necessary).** A verifiable Afghanistan arrangement; fiscal settlement legislated; leadership continuity across at least two electoral cycles; sustained external (donor) financing for reintegration and education; internal TTP fragmentation exploited; and no major India–Pakistan crisis.

**Assessment.** This scenario's low probability is a function of the number of independent conditions, not of their impossibility. Notably, its *most* contingent element is the fiscal settlement — the one item requiring no adversary's consent. That asymmetry is the strongest argument in the study for prioritizing it.

## 14.2 Scenario 2: Status Quo — Fragile Equilibrium with Periodic Violence

**Probability: 50–60 per cent — the central case.**

**Narrative.** Hostilities with Afghanistan oscillate between episodes of relative calm (ceasefires, mediation cycles) and strikes-and-retaliation cycles; the TTP retains its sanctuary and enough capacity to sustain 300–600 attacks per month across KP, with periodic spectacular attacks (a Kohat- or Swat-scale event every 6–18 months, an Islamabad-scale event every 1–3 years). The police continue to absorb losses (100–150 killed annually) with incremental equipment and technology improvements; IBOs continue to kill several hundred militants annually; the merged districts' fiscal shortfall is partially addressed (piecemeal releases; political bargaining) but not structurally resolved; local government remains stalled; and economic recovery proceeds unevenly (tourism resilient; investment cautious).

**Outcome indicators.** Annual KP violence-linked fatalities steady in the 1,400–2,500 band; deaths among security forces 300–500 annually; attacks on the police 600–900 annually; displacement episodes of 20,000–100,000 people recurring in the frontier districts; education and health indicators stagnant; radicalization pipelines partially replaced by ongoing recruitment.

**Why this is the central case.** It requires no change in any party's behaviour — it is the continuation of observable trends (2024–2026), smoothed. It is "fragile" because the equilibrium can tip (Scenario 3) on any of several shocks, and it is "equilibrium" because none of the core drivers is self-correcting: the sanctuary persists, the fiscal shortfall persists, and the police protection deficit persists.

## 14.3 Scenario 3: Deterioration — Resurgence of Militancy and State-Erosion Risk

**Probability: 25–30 per cent by 2035 (with sub-scenarios below).**

**Narrative components (each independently plausible).**
(a) *Interstate escalation trap*: repeated mass-casualty attacks inside Pakistan trigger larger strikes on Afghanistan; Kabul retaliates with sustained border fire and drone attacks; Torkham and Ghulam Khan close for months; cross-border trade collapses; border populations (already displaced 33,800 families in Tirah alone) radicalize further; and the TTP's Operation Khyber framing gains traction (Chapters 3, 9).
(b) *Urban mass-casualty terrorism*: an ISKP or TTP attack on a soft urban target in Peshawar or Islamabad (a mosque, hospital, market, or school) produces political panic, curfews, and possibly a nationwide crackdown that compounds alienation.
(c) *Police collapse dynamic*: if police casualties continue at 2026 rates (+93.7 per cent H1/2025→H1/2026), recruitment, retention and morale degrade to the point where static posts are abandoned and rural policing collapses in parts of the south — handing some districts to TTP's parallel governance (taxation, courts), a threshold-crossing event that would make the NMDs resemble pre-2009 Swat.
(d) *Escalation in sectarian conflict*: a Kurram-scale or larger incident (a major attack on a Shia procession, or a Hazara massacre) ignites sustained sectarian warfare; the state's capacity is diverted; and both Iran-linked and ISKP-linked escalatory pressures intensify.
(e) *Two-front stress*: an India–Pakistan crisis (e.g., a repeat of 2019 or 2025-style exchanges) draws forces eastward, creating space for TTP/BLA operations in the west.

**Outcome indicators.** Annual KP fatalities exceed 3,500; the TTP holds "no-go" pockets by night in ≥3 districts; parallel taxation is routinized in ≥5 NMD districts; police recruitment shortfalls exceed 20 per cent; provincial revenue-strapped government fails to pay salaries on time; provincial elections (or their postponement) become a legitimacy crisis.

**Assessment.** The constituent risks are not hypothetical; several have already occurred in partial form (the 2026 war; the Kohat attack; Tirah displacement; Kurram's siege-like closure). What distinguishes Scenario 3 from the status quo is *threshold crossing*: the transition from episodic violence to durable loss of state control in parts of the province — which would require not a new set of causes but the removal of the current counterweights (police presence, tribal resistance, intelligence penetration, international attention).

## 14.6 Early Warning Indicators for Security Deterioration

A functional early-warning system (currently absent) would track, quarterly: (1) attack frequency and fatality trends by district, disaggregated by actor and target type; (2) police casualties and attacks, and *thwarted* attacks, by district; (3) TTP taxation/"checkpoint" reports by district; (4) drone-attack attempts and counter-drone successes; (5) compensation-payment arrears and displaced-return rates; (6) school closures and girl-enrolment by district; (7) conviction rates and pending cases in ATCs; (8) TTP/ISKP propaganda volume and recruitment narratives (media monitoring); (9) cross-border trade volumes and border-post closure days; (10) local-government budget flows (or their absence); (11) police recruitment/retention rates; and (12) sectoral indicators of state presence (health facilities functional; courts sitting; teachers present). Thresholds on several indicators (e.g., a district where TTP taxation is reported for two consecutive quarters; three consecutive months of rising police attacks; failure to pay compensation within 180 days) would trip escalation of the response — from development acceleration to security surge.

## 14.7 The Role of Climate Change and Environmental Degradation in Future Security

**The risk profile.** KP faces: accelerated glacial melt (Pakistan has 7,000+ glaciers, mostly in KP and Gilgit-Baltistan); increased monsoon intensity (the NDMA forecast 22–26 per cent above-normal rainfall for 2026); GLOF risk in Chitral, Swat, Dir and Gilgit-Baltistan's border areas; flash flooding (the 2025 disaster: 504 KP deaths, 1.57 million affected, ~100,000 displaced, 437 schools affected, 60 health facilities damaged); landslides; and prolonged drought cycles in the southern districts. These are *security-relevant* through five channels: (i) displacement (recruitment, trafficking, and lost livelihoods); (ii) the destruction of state infrastructure precisely in contested districts; (iii) relief distribution becoming a site of capture and conflict (flood relief in KP has been politically contested); (iv) water conflict (canals, entitlements, and the trans-boundary Kabul River); and (v) economic contraction (agriculture losses driving rural poverty, which is already increasing).

**Scenario relevance.** Climate shocks in Scenario 2 are recurrent stress; in Scenario 3 they are accelerants. Policy must integrate climate resilience into the security framework: flood-resilient roads and schools (double-purpose: state presence and disaster protection), upstream GLOF early warning, and relief distribution through transparent, community-audited mechanisms (avoiding the patronage capture that feeds grievance).

## 14.8 Demographic Trends and Their Security Implications (Youth Bulge, Urbanization)

**Trends.** KP's population (~40 million) is growing at roughly 2 per cent-plus annually, with about half under 25. Urbanization is accelerating: Peshawar's metropolitan area, Mardan, Swat, Abbottabad and D.I. Khan are growing, driven by rural-to-urban migration, displacement, and conflict. The combined effect by 2035: a labour force expanding by ~400,000–600,000 people per year against formal job creation that in recent years has been a fraction of that figure.

**Security implications.** (i) *Urbanization of violence*: as cities grow, so does the target set for terrorism (markets, transport, mosques, government offices) — the pattern already visible in Peshawar, and threatened in the Kohat/Swat episodes. (ii) *Informal settlements*: rapid urbanization produces unplanned peripheral settlements (the natural habitat for anonymity, criminality and radicalization networks). (iii) *The youth bulge*: a cohort with weak job prospects and strong connectivity will generate recruitment pressure and mobilization potential for both militant and protest movements — the PTM's peak mobilization (2018–2020) was demographically driven. (iv) *Elderly/veteran problem*: decades of conflict produce a large cohort of injured, traumatized and disarmed men needing reintegration.

**Policy implication.** Any normalization plan that does not include an explicit *urban and youth strategy* (housing, transport, skills, sports/culture, and urban policing) will be overwhelmed by demography. Conversely, urbanization can be an advantage: services are cheaper to deliver in dense settlements, and city governance — if empowered — can be a laboratory for accountable security provision.

## 14.9 Technology, AI and the Future of Security Management in KPK

**Applications now arriving.** (i) *Surveillance*: the Peshawar Safe City system (711 AI cameras, facial recognition; D.I. Khan, Bannu, Lakki Marwat in progress) plus mobile surveillance vehicles, drones and anti-drone systems. (ii) *Biometrics and identity*: NADRA-linked verification, SIM registration, border-crossing biometrics. (iii) *Military technologies*: anti-drone electronic warfare; night-vision and precision optics; ISR drones. (iv) *Data analytics*: police data systems; the provincial "Digital Transformation Policy 2030." (v) *Militant technologies*: quadcopter IED delivery (341 attacks foiled in H1 2026), commercial drones, encrypted communications, online propaganda.

**Emerging risks and opportunities.**
*Opportunities*: predictive policing (with safeguards); automated threat alerts at checkpoints; rapid forensic identification; counter-drone systems; digital payment tracking (anti-financing); and e-governance improving service delivery and reducing corruption contact points.
*Risks*: (i) *rights and oversight* — facial recognition without data protection (there is no Pakistani data-protection law of general application) becomes a tool of political surveillance; the Safe City Authority, if executive-controlled, would compound the 27th Amendment's centralization; (ii) *technological overconfidence* — cameras cannot solve a rural insurgency in which the state lacks presence, intelligence and legitimacy; (iii) *the adversary adapts* — drone/dark-web/AI-enabled propaganda will evolve faster than procurement cycles; and (iv) *digital exclusion* — merged-district connectivity gaps mean the digital dividend accrues unevenly, deepening the core-periphery gap.

**Policy direction.** (a) Legislate data protection and surveillance oversight *before* expanding the Safe City network; (b) treat counter-drone capability as a frontline police protection requirement (the Karak attack pattern); (c) invest in counter-propaganda AI tools only within a rights framework with published rules; and (d) prioritize *investigative* technology (forensics, digital evidence chain of custody) over mass surveillance — the former is what converts detentions into convictions, the latter is what produces grievance.

## 14.10 Strategic Foresight: KPK Security in 2035

Three 2035 snapshots, corresponding to the scenarios above:

**2035-A (Normalization, ~18 per cent).** KP is a middle-income provincial economy with functioning district courts in the NMDs, a professionalized police force with an NMD wing, a legislated merged-districts fund delivering on schedule, 20 million annual tourists, a minerals sector with community benefit-sharing, and a border regime with Afghanistan managed through monitoring agreements. Violence is at criminal levels (200–400 fatalities/year), and the FATA merger is regarded regionally as a success model. This future requires the fiscal settlement and a border détente — nothing else.

**2035-B (Fragile Equilibrium, ~55 per cent).** KP has absorbed another decade of violence: cumulative 200,000+ displaced, tens of thousands dead, a police force hollowed by casualties, an economy that grows slowly while the merged districts remain the poorest in Pakistan, periodic border wars with Afghanistan, and a political system in which security remains the dominant issue. Tourism continues; the province holds; but its institutions remain below the level required to break with the past.

**2035-C (Deterioration, ~27 per cent).** The eastward shift of the insurgency continues to the settled districts; the TTP exercises parallel governance in some NMD pockets; sectarian conflict has erupted at scale in at least one district; the state's response has produced further centralization and repression; a PTI-era political crisis has hardened Pashtun alienation; and Pakistan ranks among the world's most terrorism-affected countries for a decade running. This future's seeds are visible in 2026's data — a 93.7 per cent increase in police fatalities, a Rs 568 billion fiscal betrayal, and an interstate war with no exit.

**Foresight conclusion.** The difference between these futures is not primarily the number of militants Pakistan kills; it is the delivery of three state commitments that are entirely within Pakistan's control: money for the merged districts; protection for the police; and a lawful political channel for Pashtun grievance. The external variable — the Afghan sanctuary — can be mitigated or worsened by diplomacy, but it cannot be waited out. **KP's 2035 hinges on whether Peshawar and Islamabad treat the province as a constitutional partner or as a war zone with a budget.**
