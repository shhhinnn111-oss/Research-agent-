# CHAPTER 9 — GEOPOLITICAL AND CROSS-BORDER DIMENSIONS

## 9.1 The Afghanistan Factor: Taliban Government, Bilateral Relations and Security Spillover

**The structural transformation of 2021.** The Taliban's return to Kabul in August 2021 removed the principal external constraint on the TTP — a hostile Afghan government allied to the US-led coalition — and replaced it with an ideological kin government that Pakistan had supported for decades. The TTP's leadership relocated to Afghanistan (Kunar, Nangarhar, Khost, Paktika, Paktia and beyond); its fighters gained freedom of movement, training space, medical care, and access to newly abundant weaponry; and its families gained a sanctuary in which Pakistani pressure could not reach them directly. The International Crisis Group concluded in January 2026 that Pakistan was "the country worst affected by the fall of Kabul" — a striking inversion of Islamabad's expectations (ICG, cited in Wikipedia 2026d).

**Bilateral relationship, 2021–2025: from expectation to estrangement.** Pakistan's toolkit toward Kabul comprised: diplomatic engagement (repeated delegations, the "Afghan Inter-Ministerial Committee" and special envoys); border closures and trade restrictions (Torkham, Chaman); expulsions of Afghan refugees; and — ultimately — unilateral military action. Each instrument was tried; none produced compliance. The Afghan Taliban's position was consistent: it denies harbouring the TTP and characterizes cross-border attacks as Pakistan's internal problem; it refuses (as a matter of stated principle) to be seen acting against fellow jihadists under foreign pressure; and elements within it — particularly the Haqqani network, whose leaders dominate the Interior Ministry — maintain longstanding ties to the TTP and to Pakistani Taliban factions. Meanwhile, the TTP's own demands (prisoner releases; reversal of merger; compensation for casualties) make any Afghan-mediated deal hard to land.

**The escalation ladder to war (October 2025 – February 2026).** The sequence is documented: (i) *October 2025*: major cross-border clashes and Pakistani strikes inside Afghanistan, followed by a **Qatari/Turkish-mediated ceasefire (Doha, October 2025)**; (ii) *November 2025*: **Istanbul talks** produced agreement on a monitoring and verification mechanism — without a political settlement; (iii) *December 2025 – January 2026*: continued (if reduced) cross-border violence; Pakistan's air campaign inside Afghanistan resumed in late 2025 and continued through 2026 (reported strikes in Kabul and the eastern provinces); (iv) *6 February 2026*: the ISKP suicide bombing at an Islamabad imambargah (31 killed) — plus attacks in Bannu and Bajaur during early Ramadan — hardened Islamabad's position; (v) *21–22 February 2026*: cross-border clashes escalated; Pakistan launched airstrikes against alleged TTP/ISKP camps in Afghanistan; (vi) *26 February 2026*: **Operation Ghazab-lil-Haq** was announced, with the Defence Minister declaring "open war" (Wikipedia 2026a; Dawn 2026g; The Diplomat 2026a; Hindustan Times 2026).

**The war (February – October 2026).** Both sides deployed air power (Pakistan) and drones, artillery and border assaults (Afghanistan). Contested claims proliferated: Pakistan said it had killed 583 Afghan Taliban personnel, injured 795 and destroyed 242 checkposts by early March (later figures higher); Afghanistan claimed 55–110 Pakistani soldiers killed and dozens of posts captured; ISPR reported 274 Taliban/terrorists killed for 12 Pakistani troops in the first days (Dawn 2026g; Indian Express 2026; Hindustan Times 2026). Two Eid ceasefires — Eid al-Fitr (19–24 March, at Saudi, Qatari and Turkish request) and a TTP-announced pause over Eid al-Adha (late May) — briefly reduced hostilities; fighting resumed after each. On 24 September 2026, Pakistani strikes hit ten sites in Kandahar, Paktia and Khost; on 1 October 2026 strikes hit Kunar and Helmand, with UNAMA confirming at least 10 civilians killed (mostly children). Cumulatively, UNAMA recorded **764 civilian casualties (372 killed, 392 injured) in Afghanistan between 26 January and 31 March 2026 alone**, and **more than 1,100 civilian deaths and injuries by late September 2026** (UNAMA, cited in Khaama 2026; Dawn 2026g).

**Why it matters for KP.** The war has four direct effects on the province: (i) *force and attention diversion*: military resources shift to border and cross-border operations; (ii) *militant mobilization*: the TTP exploits the war to frame itself as an anti-state force in a national struggle, and announced its *Operation Khyber* spring offensive on 4 March 2026; (iii) *humanitarian flows*: displacement, return flows and border closures affect KP's border districts; and (iv) *escalation risk*: each cycle of strikes and retaliation hardens both governments' domestic political constraints against de-escalation.

**Assessment.** The Afghan sanctuary is the single most important *external* variable in KP's security equation, and the 2026 war has demonstrated that force alone cannot close it — Pakistan's airstrikes can punish and disrupt, but they cannot occupy Kunar or compel the Taliban's compliance. Sustained de-escalation would require a *verifiable* arrangement (monitoring, defined commitments on TTP movement/training, incentives such as trade and refugee cooperation), which the Istanbul mechanism attempted and the war destroyed. Rebuilding a credible channel — with regional guarantors — is a strategic necessity, not an act of goodwill.

## 9.2 The Durand Line: Historical Dispute, Fencing Project and Border Management

**The dispute.** The Durand Line (demarcated 1893–1896) has never been recognized by any Afghan government as a permanent international boundary. Afghanistan's position — that the line separated Pashtun and Baloch communities without their consent and is therefore void — has persisted through monarchic, republican, communist and Taliban governments. Pakistan's position — that the line is a settled international boundary, whatever its colonial origins — has been consistent since 1947. The dispute has practical consequences: Afghanistan's refusal to accept border posts and fencing as legitimate; periodic firing at construction and at posts; the non-regulation of cross-border movement in Afghan law; and the political costs of any Pakistani concession (which would be framed domestically as capitulation).

**Fencing and border management.** Pakistan began fencing the Durand Line in 2017 and reported substantial completion (of the KP/NMD-facing sectors and much of the Balochistan frontier) by 2021–2023, coupled with new terminals (Torkham, Ghulam Khan, Angoor Adda and others), border forts, and surveillance systems. Effects: reduced large-scale infiltration and a formalization of crossings; but also: increased friction with Kabul; disruption of traditional cross-border social and economic life (kin mobility, grazing, labour, and trade); and — crucially — a *diversion effect*: infiltration shifted to covert crossings, tunnels, and unfenced or barely monitored stretches, and firing over the fence became a recurring tactic.

**Closure as instrument.** Since 2021, both sides have repeatedly closed crossings (Torkham in early 2025 over a border-post dispute; Torkham and Ghulam Khan during the 2025 and 2026 hostilities). Closures damage both economies, particularly Afghanistan's (dependent on Pakistani transit) and KP's border districts (trade, transport, labour, and consumer prices).

**Assessment.** A fenced line without a *border regime* — lawful crossings, permits, trade facilities, dispute mechanisms and a hotline for incidents — is an incomplete solution: it hardens the border's security function while degrading its economic and social functions. The correct objective is not an impermeable seal (unattainable over 2,600 km of mountains) but a *managed border* that denies sanctuary transit while preserving the lawful movement on which border communities depend.

## 9.3 Afghan Refugees in KPK: Security Implications, Policy Dilemmas and Repatriation

**The scale.** Afghanistan's displacement crisis and four decades of conflict-related migration produced one of the world's largest protracted refugee situations. Pakistan hosts (by various estimates) 1.5–3.7 million Afghans, including UNHCR-registered Proof of Registration (PoR) holders (~1.3 million), Afghan Citizen Card holders, undocumented migrants, and new arrivals since 2021. **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa hosts the largest share — roughly one-third to one-half of the population of concern**, concentrated in Peshawar, Bannu, Nowshera, Swat, Mardan, Kohat, and camps (Nasir Bagh, Kacha Garhi, Jalozai closure, and others).

**The policy trajectory.** Phase one (2023–24): the Interior Ministry's **Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan (IFRP)** targeted undocumented Afghans from 1 November 2023; hundreds of thousands returned. Phase two (2025): **April 2025** deadlines led to 80,000+ expelled within weeks, with 1.3 million PoR holders (many with decades of residence) told to relocate out of Islamabad/Rawalpindi; UNHCR reported over 910,000 deported since September 2023. Phase three (2026): a **June 2026 review meeting** directed "expedited repatriation/deportation" including visa overstay cases; from **10 July 2026**, any Afghan found without a valid visa was to be arrested immediately; Bannu's camps (Bizan Khel, Ghoriwala, Mamand Khel) were vacated (525 families repatriated in one reported tranche); and KP prepared the "final phase" of repatriation for **~20,000 Afghans in Peshawar city**, with lists compiled by the Special Branch, District Security Branch and police, and Federal Constabulary support (Dawn 2026e; Tribune 2026b; Al Jazeera 2025; HRW 2026; NYT 2025).

**Evidence of abuses.** Human Rights Watch (April 2026) documented: arrests of Afghans while shopping, at school, or seeking day labour; confiscation of phones and cash; demands for bribes; detention and expulsion of persons at risk in Afghanistan (journalists, human rights defenders, former government officials); the forcible return of at least nine Afghan journalists including some with valid visas (Reporters Without Borders); and the November 2025 Surkhab camp operation in Balochistan in which more than 1,000 people were detained and homes and businesses bulldozed (HRW 2026).

**Security arguments and counter-arguments.** The security argument: Afghan nationals — including some linked to militant networks, and some in mixed-migration flows that criminals exploit — are present in enormous, poorly documented numbers; identity systems are incomplete; and camps have historically been used as cover (the October 2026 ATC conviction in Peshawar involved an abandoned house in a refugee camp in Mohmand used as a militant safe house). The counter-arguments are equally weighty: (i) the overwhelming majority of Afghans are not combatants, and mass expulsion is a collective measure that violates non-refoulement obligations for at-risk groups and generates profound resentment among the wider Afghan and Pashtun population; (ii) the policy is *substitutionary*: it addresses the population channel (Chapter 3) rather than the sanctuary channel; and (iii) it has been repeatedly shown to correlate with *rising*, not falling, violence in the periods of most intense expulsions.

**Assessment.** Pakistan has legitimate security, fiscal and demographic interests in a managed Afghan presence. The policy instruments available to serve those interests — registration, biometric documentation, work permits, encampment reform, and case-by-case enforcement against specific threats — are all more effective and lawful than mass arrest-and-expel campaigns. The current trajectory is producing three harms: humanitarian (refoulement); diplomatic (eroding Pakistan's standing and feeding Afghan irredentism); and security (creating grievance, disrupting information flows, and enabling extortion networks within the state apparatus).

## 9.4 Cross-Border TTP Sanctuaries and the Afghan Taliban's Relationship with the TTP

**The relationship's complexity.** The Afghan Taliban–TTP relationship is neither sponsorship nor estrangement; it is a set of competing and overlapping interests: (i) *ideological affinity* — shared Deobandi-jihadist lineage, personnel exchanges since the 1990s, and familial ties between borderland communities; (ii) *strategic dependence* — the TTP provided fighters for the Afghan Taliban's war and hosts fighters' families in Afghanistan; (iii) *divergence* — the Afghan Taliban govern a state whose interests now include (a) Pakistani trade, (b) not being attacked, and (c) keeping the TTP from importing Pakistan's war into Afghan politics; (iv) *fragmentation* — the Taliban's internal factions (Kandahar core; Haqqani network; military commissions) differ in their tolerance of the TTP, and some commanders function as intermediaries; and (v) *leverage* — the TTP is useful to Kabul as leverage in negotiations with Pakistan, and dangerous as an autonomous actor.

**Practical patterns.** Relocation and dispersal: repeated Pakistani requests and Afghan promises have produced some movement (relocations away from the border, shifts between provinces) without dismantling. Mediation attempts: the Afghan Taliban hosted and at times imposed ceasefires (including the November–December 2021 truce) and attempted to broker talks (2021–22). Enforcement: at no point has Kabul moved decisively against TTP infrastructure, and Afghanistan's position publicly treats the TTP as Pakistan's problem.

**Options for Pakistan.** The realistic set is: (a) *coercive pressure* — cross-border strikes (as in 2026) and economic restrictions — which disrupted but did not dismantle the sanctuary and produced escalation; (b) *conditional engagement* — trading on Afghanistan's dependencies (trade transit, currency, refugees, connectivity) for verifiable actions on specified armed groups; (c) *mediation* — using regional guarantors (China, Qatar, Türkiye, Saudi Arabia) to establish a monitoring mechanism with consequences; and (d) *decoupling* — recognizing that statelessness and fragmentation in Afghanistan mean that even a cooperative Kabul cannot perfectly control the TTP, and building internal defenses (border management, intelligence, deradicalization) accordingly. The study's assessment is that a combination of (b) and (d), with (c) as the process vehicle, is the only durable approach — and that (a) alone has demonstrated a poor cost-benefit record.

## 9.5 Role of Regional Powers: India, Iran, China, Russia and Central Asian States

**India.** Pakistan's persistent allegation that Indian intelligence networks support militant activity (principally in Balochistan, and occasionally claimed for KP) remains formally unverified; India denies it. Regardless of the factual dispute, the *strategic dynamic* is real: India–Pakistan tension (the 2019 and 2025 military exchanges, including Operation Sindoor in May 2025) shapes Pakistan's threat perception and force posture; the Taliban's relationship with India (diplomatic and humanitarian engagement post-2021) is read in Islamabad as encirclement; and any Pakistan–India crisis has historically coincided with heightened cross-border pressure in KP and Afghanistan. This study treats Indian involvement in KP's insurgency as *unproven*, while noting that the *perception* of it influences Pakistani policy and that Pakistan's own record of using non-state actors complicates its claims.

**Iran.** Iran's interests in Afghanistan (water rights, the Helmand basin; Shia and Persian-speaking populations; the Chabahar–India–Central Asia corridor) and its sectarian ties to Pakistan's Shia communities (including in Kurram and Hangu) make it a party to KP's security in ways that are rarely acknowledged. Allegations of Iranian-linked support to Shia militias in Kurram (including Zainebiyoun-linked fighters) and of Iranian involvement in Balochistan are contested; what is documented is Iran's mediation and its hosting of Afghan refugees (returned en masse during 2023–25). Iran's most significant structural impact is the sectarian equilibrium of the borderland: a reciprocal sponsorship dynamic in which each side's sponsorship is cited to justify the other's.

**China.** China's interests are threefold: CPEC security (including the safety of Chinese nationals — the March 2024 Shangla attack killed five Chinese engineers travelling to a Dasu project in KP, and attacks on Chinese nationals continued in 2024–26); counter-terrorism cooperation (China has supplied drones, surveillance technology and training support, and pressed Pakistan on TTP/ETIM threats); and mediation (China brokered Pakistan–Afghanistan talks in April 2026 and hosted a trilateral process). China also has economic interests in KP: the Rashakai SEZ (with 22 enterprises in progress by 2026, ~Rs 68 billion in planned investment, and ~3,000 expected jobs), the Karakoram Highway realignment, and mineral interest in the merged districts.

**Russia and Central Asia.** Russia's counter-terrorism concerns (ISKP; Central Asian fighters) have driven pragmatic engagement with the Taliban and with Pakistan; Central Asian states (Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kazakhstan) share the ISKP threat and trade interests in a stable Afghanistan (trans-Afghan connectivity: railways, power). Their interest in KP is indirect but real: instability in Pakistan's borderland constrains their trade routes and increases ISKP's reach westward.

## 9.6 The US Withdrawal from Afghanistan (2021): Impact on KPK Security

**Direct effects.** (i) *Sanctuary restored*: the withdrawal removed US ISR (intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance) and strike capability that had degraded TTP/AQ leadership after 2001; Pakistan's own later operations operated without that enabler — a factor explicitly noted by analysts contrasting the Zarb-e-Azb environment with the current one (Dawn 2024a). (ii) *Weapons proliferation*: the Afghan National Security Forces' collapse released vast quantities of small arms, night-vision equipment, vehicles and munitions; Pakistan officially estimates USD 7 billion-plus of equipment was abandoned (Eurasia Review 2026a). (iii) *Refugee and migration pressures*: 2021–2022 saw new arrivals and, later, international resettlement pipelines that left thousands stranded in Pakistan when third-country admissions were suspended in 2025. (iv) *Diplomatic vacuum*: no external actor with leverage over the Taliban remains willing to invest in constraining the TTP on Pakistan's behalf, and the US–Pakistan relationship remains transactional (a dynamic visible in the 2026 US statement of support for Pakistan's "right to defend itself" while providing no material support).

**Second-order effects.** The perception of abandonment in Kabul's neighborhood (including Pakistan's sense that it paid the price for US policy) shaped Pakistan's more unilateral posture in 2025–26; and the lack of international funding for stabilization in the borderlands (contrasting with the AF-PAK era's development dollars) shifted the fiscal burden entirely to Pakistan's own strained budget.

## 9.7 Intelligence Cooperation and Failures: ISI, NDS/GDI, CIA Dynamics

**The triangle.** The ISI–CIA relationship (2001–2021) was essential to targeting al-Qaeda and produced the capture of numerous senior figures, but was chronically strained by divergent priorities (Pakistan's focus on India and on Afghanistan's post-settlement order; the US's demands regarding the Haqqani network and sanctuary). The ISI–NDS (Afghan National Directorate of Security) relationship pre-2021 was openly adversarial. Post-2021, the ISI deals with the Taliban's General Directorate of Intelligence (GDI) and military intelligence — counterparts with ideological ties to the adversary (TTP) that Pakistan wants them to act against. The institutional outcome is an intelligence relationship in which Pakistan lacks the leverage that a shared adversary provides.

**Documented failures and cooperation.** Failures: the TTP leadership's reconstitution post-2021; advance warnings missed for major attacks (including the Kohat Police Lines attack of September 2026, which struck a facility housing CTD and Special Branch offices — suggesting either a critical intelligence gap or a protection failure); and the ISKP's ability to strike inside Islamabad in February 2026. Cooperation: Pakistan's provision (and China's contribution) of intelligence to Kabul regarding ISKP; exchanges with Iran and China; and counter-financing cooperation via NACTA and the FATF context.

**Assessment.** In the absence of a cooperative Afghan counterpart, intelligence-led operations become the *entire* counter-terrorism strategy — with the self-limiting problems described in Chapter 6 (civilian harm erodes sources; attrition refills ranks). A sustainable approach requires either an intelligence-sharing channel with Kabul (which the 2025 Istanbul mechanism attempted) or a border-management and reconnaissance architecture comprehensive enough to compensate for its absence (which the fence-plus-drone model does not achieve).

## 9.8 CPEC Security and the Geopolitics of Economic Corridors

**CPEC in KP.** The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor's western and Hazara alignments pass through KP (Karakoram Highway realignment; the Rashakai SEZ; energy projects; the Swat Expressway's linkage). Estimated investment in KP under CPEC remains modest relative to on-paper commitments; Rashakai's progress (22 enterprises in progress; Rs 68 billion planned investment; the September 2026 inauguration of a mobile-phone manufacturing unit) illustrates both the promise and the slow pace.

**Security politics.** The security of Chinese nationals (and projects) has become a central domestic political issue and a diplomatic constraint: attacks on Chinese personnel (Dasu in 2021; Shangla in March 2024 killing five engineers; subsequent incidents) have prompted Beijing to demand stronger security guarantees, occasionally linking to demands for joint security arrangements — an unsettling prospect for Pakistani sovereignty. Chinese pressure also contributes to the political economy of operations (analysts have argued the timing of Azm-e-Istehkam reflected, in part, Chinese concerns) and to Pakistan's willingness to escalate against Kabul (Chinese mediation in 2026 notwithstanding).

**Assessment.** CPEC's security burden in KP is substantial: it requires protection of long linear infrastructure through contested terrain, generates grievance where land acquisition and labour practices are perceived as unfair, and creates high-value targets. The economic dividend (jobs, connectivity, energy) is real but smaller and slower than promised, and it cannot substitute for the fiscal settlement of the merged districts in producing a peace dividend. Moreover, the geopolitics of corridors means KP's security is now an element of Sino-Pakistani and Sino–Central Asian relations, further reducing Pakistan's freedom of action.

## 9.9 The Balochistan–KPK Security Nexus

**Connections.** The two western provinces' insurgencies are distinct (ethno-nationalist and separatist in Balochistan; jihadist and Pashtunist in KP), but they interlock in five ways: (i) *personnel and networks*: TTP factions operate across Balochistan (TTP claimed the Quetta and Ziarat attacks of July 2026, with the BLA claiming the Lasbela attack); the TTP's Baloch reach and BLA's Pashtun recruitment overlap in the border districts; (ii) *logistics*: arms and narcotics routes cross both provinces; (iii) *state response*: Azm-e-Istehkam's national framing, ISPR's aggregated casualty reporting, and the federal claim that "hostile agencies" sponsor both insurgencies; (iv) *politics*: the PTM's Balochistan bans and the Pashtun–Baloch solidarity framing in rights movements; and (v) *data and attention*: in Q3 2026, Balochistan (829 deaths) surpassed KP (556) as Pakistan's most violent region — a shift with national political consequences, including the July 2026 Quetta attacks and the Apex Committee response.

**Strategic implication.** KP and Balochistan jointly oblige Pakistan's military to fight on two fronts with different adversaries, distinct terrain, and separate political dynamics — a force-allocation reality that has historically produced discontinuous pressure in each province. Resource-constrained strategy should therefore be explicit about acceptable risk in each theatre rather than responding reactively to the latest attack, or the state will continue to oscillate between theatres.

## 9.10 The Role of International Organizations: UN, NATO, SCO, FATF

**United Nations.** UN instruments matter to KP through: (i) *Security Council sanctions* (1267 and 1988 regimes; the Analytical Support and Sanctions Monitoring Team's assessments, whose 37th report in February 2026 documented increased TTP attacks from Afghanistan); (ii) *UNAMA's* civilian-casualty documentation for the cross-border strikes; (iii) *UN human rights mechanisms* (the High Commissioner's and Special Rapporteur's statements; the Secretary-General's ceasefire appeals during the 2026 war); (iv) *UNHCR and IOM* for refugee and returnee management; and (v) *UN Women and UNODC* for PVE and narcotics programming. The UN's leverage is limited by the absence of enforcement mechanisms in the Afghanistan context and by the politics of the Security Council.

**NATO.** NATO's role ended with the withdrawal, but its legacy is material (weapons in circulation; the coalition support fund's fiscal dependence; the "Af-Pak" framework's collapse).

**SCO.** Pakistan's membership (and its hosting of the 2024 SCO summit, during which security was emphasized) provides a platform for counter-terrorism cooperation with China, Russia, and Central Asian states — including the Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS). SCO declarations routinely commit members to non-support for terrorism against each other — a commitment relevant to Afghan–Pakistan disputes.

**FATF.** Pakistan's exit from the FATF grey list (2022) was consequential for its international financial standing and for the seriousness with which it pursued counter-financing and madrasa financial oversight — indeed, FATF compliance pressure was the principal driver of madrasa regulation efforts, and its relaxation (following the 2024 statutory reversal) is a cautionary example of how external pressure can produce both progress and its reversal.

## 9.11 Geopolitical Scenarios and Their Implications for KPK Security (to 2035)

**Scenario A: Regional de-escalation and a verifiable border modus vivendi (low probability, high impact).** A Pakistan–Afghanistan agreement (brokered by China/Qatar/Türkiye/Saudi Arabia) with monitoring, prisoner exchanges, and reciprocal limits on cross-border activity; TTP pressure reduced but not eliminated; Pakistan's military returned to internal focus; fiscal space for the merged districts' settlement. Probability: low (15–20 per cent) absent a change in Taliban incentives; consequence for KP: the single largest improvement available.

**Scenario B: Managed confrontation (current trajectory).** Periodic cross-border strikes and ceasefire cycles; continued insurgency at 2025–26 levels; growing interdependence with China's security interests; gradual erosion of Pakistan's border-region economy and legitimacy. Probability: 45–55 per cent. Consequence: continued high casualties; a slow fiscal and institutional attrition.

**Scenario C: Escalatory collapse (interstate war intensifies).** Repeated mass-casualty events inside Pakistan (Islamabad/KP) trigger larger strikes; Afghanistan responds with cross-border attacks; border trade collapses; refugee flows reverse; TTP and ISKP both gain from the chaos; the TTP's operational reach expands to central Punjab and Karachi. Probability: 20–25 per cent. Consequence: KP becomes a full war economy with the highest civilian cost in a generation.

**Scenario D: Regional realignment (India–Pakistan crisis interlinked).** Any major India–Pakistan confrontation pulls Pakistani forces eastward, creates opportunity structures for both TTP and BLA, and produces a two-front crisis. Probability: variable (10–20 per cent for a major crisis). Consequence: severe; force allocation and political attention shift away from KP precisely when it is most fragile.

**Scenario E: Taliban internal instability.** Factional conflict or succession crisis in Kabul fragments control over TTP sanctuary areas, producing local deals and local chaos — potentially reducing some threats (command fragmentation) while increasing others (uncontrolled zones; ISKP opportunity). Probability: 25–35 per cent over a decade; consequence: unpredictable, possibly regionally serious.

**Cross-cutting analysis.** In four of the five scenarios, KP's security is determined in large part by decisions taken in Kabul, Islamabad, Beijing, and Doha rather than Peshawar. This is why the study treats "diplomatic and border strategy" as an integral part of the normalization roadmap, not an external footnote: a province cannot normalize its security while a neighbouring state hosts the force that attacks it.
