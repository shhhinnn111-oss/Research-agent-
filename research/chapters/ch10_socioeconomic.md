# CHAPTER 10 — SOCIO-ECONOMIC DIMENSIONS OF SECURITY NORMALIZATION

## 10.1 Poverty and Inequality in KPK: Statistical Overview and Spatial Analysis

**The provincial picture.** In the FY2024-25 poverty estimates released by the Planning Ministry in 2026, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's poverty rate rose from **28.7 per cent to 35.3 per cent** — the second-highest provincial rate after Balochistan (47 per cent) and far above Punjab (23.3 per cent) and the national average of 28.9 per cent (Economic Survey 2025-26). The increase is among the sharpest of any province: the causes officially cited include fiscal consolidation under the IMF programme, energy tariff adjustments, exchange-rate depreciation, inflation, disaster losses, and weak growth (Planning Ministry 2026; Indrastra 2026). The World Bank's provincial estimate for KP was lower (~29.5 per cent in 2025, per the draft KP Youth Policy), illustrating the sensitivity of poverty measurement — but the direction of change is unambiguous (KP Youth Policy 2026).

**Inequality.** The inequality index for KP rose from 24.8 to 29.4 — the largest proportional increase of any province (Punjab 28.4→32; Sindh 29.7→35.9; Balochistan 21→26.5). Nationally, the inequality reading was the highest in 27 years. In KP, inequality has a specific spatial form: the Hazara and Peshawar divisions concentrate industry, services, education and health; the southern districts and the merged districts are structurally poorer, with the NMDs showing the lowest human development performance in the province (SDPI 2025).

**Spatial analysis.** SDPI's district-level Human Development Index series (2024 report, covering 2019–2023 changes) found that KP's overall HDI fell from **0.650 to 0.623**, with only 2 of 29 tracked districts improving on score and 27 declining; health and living-standards sub-indices declined (education improved in 13 districts; health in 5; housing/living standards in 7). Reading the same series for *ranking*, districts including Swabi, North Waziristan, Abbottabad, Mardan, Mansehra, Peshawar, Malakand, D.I. Khan, Swat, Bajaur, Mohmand, Hangu, Kohat, Chitral and Nowshera improved their *relative* position — a reminder that rankings and scores can diverge when all districts decline (SDPI 2025).

**Interpretation.** The poverty and HDI data establish four facts relevant to security. First, KP's socio-economic position has *deteriorated* during the very period in which violence resurged — the causal arrows run in both directions (conflict destroys livelihoods; poverty recruits). Second, the deterioration is *policy-sensitive*: the macro-fiscal mix (tariffs, taxation, subsidy withdrawal) and disaster shocks, not conflict alone, drove it. Third, the geographic concentration of poverty maps onto the geography of violence (NMDs; southern KP), though the relationship is not mechanical (Swat's literacy and relative prosperity did not prevent its 2026 attack). Fourth, the *perception* of deprivation — relative inequality, visible elite wealth, and the failure of merger promises — matters as much as absolute measures.

## 10.2 Unemployment and the Youth Bulge: A Ticking Time Bomb?

**The labour market.** Pakistan's labour force reached 83.1 million with 5.9 million unemployed (7.1 per cent) in the FY2024-25 estimates — the highest unemployment in 21 years (Economic Survey 2025-26). KP's unemployment has run above the national average (8.8 per cent in 2020–21 in the Labour Force Survey series, one of the highest provincial rates); the *youth* dimension is more severe: **31 per cent of educated youth are jobless (PIDE, March 2025)**; women constitute 51 per cent of the unemployed; roughly **40 per cent of youth are not in employment, education or training (NEET)** per recent surveys cited in the KP Youth Policy process; and the ratio of aspirants to formal posts is extreme — **866,000 candidates applied for 16,454 teaching vacancies in mid-2025** (more than 52 applicants per seat).

**The youth bulge.** Approximately half of KP's population is under 25 (KP Government 2024). A cohort of this size entering the labour market with weak absorption capacity produces predictable outcomes: long unemployment spells; migration (internal and external — Gulf labour migration is a major safety valve); informality; and, at the margins, vulnerability to armed-group recruitment (which offers wages, status, and purpose). The KP Youth Policy 2026's own drafting acknowledges that "youth programmes cannot catch up with demand," and that graduates face "widespread unemployment and outdated curricula."

**Programs and their adequacy.** Provincial instruments include: the Youth Impact Challenge grants (expanded to Rs 2 billion); digital internship schemes; the KP Youth Employment Program (IT skills); the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Rural Economic Transformation Project (KP-RETP); and the Climate Action Plan's youth and women components. These are worthwhile but quantitatively insufficient: no plausible increase in these programs can absorb a cohort of this scale. The binding constraint is *private-sector job creation*, which requires electricity reliability, credit, security, regulatory predictability and connectivity — i.e., the whole investment climate.

**Assessment: "ticking time bomb" is the wrong metaphor.** The evidence does not show that unemployed youth automatically radicalize; it shows that they are *available* — and that armed groups recruit from that availability at moments of mobilization (operations, funerals, sectarian incidents). The policy implication is not simply "create jobs" (a general development agenda) but *targeted* employment and engagement in the specific districts and cohorts where recruitment occurs, coupled with deradicalization aftercare (Chapter 8) and community programs (Chapter 12).

## 10.3 Education: Access, Quality and the Battle for Minds

**Access.** KP's literacy rate is 51 per cent (female 37 per cent; male 65 per cent), below the national 60 per cent (Economic Survey 2024-25). KP has one of the country's largest out-of-school populations; girls' enrolment lags, and in the merged districts the pipeline collapses early: approximately 37 per cent of girls attend primary school and only about 5 per cent reach secondary (KP Youth Policy 2026). Schools have been destroyed or closed by militants; hundreds were closed in Kurram, Bajaur, Khyber, Mohmand and North Waziristan for extended periods (EUAA 2026); and the 2025 floods damaged hundreds of schools in KP, disrupting education for thousands (UNICEF 2025).

**Quality.** Learning outcomes are weak nationally, and KP is no exception: high enrolment-to-learning gaps, teacher absenteeism, outdated curricula (particularly the absence of critical thinking, civic and digital skills), and inequitable teacher deployment. The university sector (Peshawar, Malakand, Swat, Kohat, Gomal, Hazara, and the NMDs' emerging campuses) produces graduates for a saturated public-sector market.

**The battle for minds.** Education is simultaneously the long-term prevention instrument and a contested space: the curriculum's treatment of religious diversity; the presence of sectarian content; the madrasa sector (Chapter 4); and the question of who teaches. The 2026 Swat attack's timing (during a "Pasoon" protest against militancy) and the 2012 shooting of Malala Yousafzai frame the stakes: girls' education is a front line, and the state's ability to protect it is a legitimacy test. The provincial "Education Emergency" declarations (2023–26) have not been matched with financing at the scale required (a commonly cited requirement: 4 per cent of GDP on education, versus ~2 per cent).

**Assessment.** No single intervention is more consequential for the 2035 security horizon than the education system — but only if it addresses quality (learning), girls' access (protection plus proximity), and school safety simultaneously. The merged districts require a *specific* program: school reconstruction, female teacher recruitment and safe accommodation, transport stipends, and community compacts (jirga- and cleric-endorsed) guaranteeing non-interference.

## 10.4 Healthcare Infrastructure and Its Security Implications

**Baseline.** KP's health system faces: workforce shortages (particularly female physicians and specialists in rural districts); uneven facility distribution (with tertiary care concentrated in Peshawar); drug shortages and out-of-pocket costs; and security threats to health workers (attacks on polio teams and their police escorts; the killing of health staff; and the withdrawal of services from insecure areas). The 2025 floods damaged 60 health facilities in KP (including 3 destroyed) and 68 Lady Health Worker houses (WHO 2025); the merged districts' indicators remain the weakest in the province (skilled birth attendance; road access to facilities) (ResearchGate/GRR 2025).

**Programs.** The Sehat Card Plus (a health insurance scheme covering provincial residents) was a flagship achievement — but its application to the merged districts was suspended at one point and became the subject of litigation (a 2022 PHC petition), illustrating how fiscal disputes translate into service withdrawal in the most vulnerable districts. Polio eradication remains a security-dependent program, and KP remains an endemic zone with recurring campaign disruptions.

**Security implications.** Three are direct: (i) attacks on vaccination teams and health workers represent attacks on state legitimacy in its most benign form; (ii) clinics and hospitals are among the few state presences in insecure districts, so their closure is a *governance* outcome, not merely a service one; (iii) conflict-related trauma and mental illness (see §10.7) burden a system with almost no psychiatric capacity — the WHO estimates that Pakistan has roughly 500 psychiatrists for 240 million people.

**Assessment.** Health is an under-used stabilizer. Mobile health units, maternal-child services, and mental-health outreach are low-cost, high-legitimacy instruments that can be delivered in the same districts where counter-insurgency seeks intelligence and cooperation. Their protection (via community escort arrangements and police protection where needed) should be treated as a security task.

## 10.5 Infrastructure Development: Roads, Electricity, Connectivity and Their Normalizing Effect

**Roads.** KP's road network expanded significantly in the last two decades: the Islamabad–Peshawar Motorway (M-1); the Hazara Motorway; the Swat Expressway; the Lowari Tunnel (connecting Chitral year-round); and various NMD roads. Roads moved markets, tourism and state presence; they also moved militants and smuggling, and their construction created both employment and grievance (land acquisition, contract capture). The NMDs' connectivity remains the weakest in the province, with projects stalled for lack of funds.

**Electricity and energy.** KP's electricity situation includes: chronic load-shedding in some areas; high losses (theft and non-payment — the PESCO problem); huge hydel potential (KP hosts a significant share of Pakistan's hydropower potential, with projects like Diamer-Bhasha downstream on the Indus and smaller run-of-river schemes); and community grievances about the province's energy exports without commensurate local benefit — a recurring political theme. Electricity reliability is the single most cited constraint in manufacturing surveys; in the merged districts, grid extension is incomplete.

**Digital connectivity.** Mobile coverage is broad but data quality is uneven; the merged districts had restricted mobile-data service for years (a security measure with enormous economic cost), gradually eased. Digital connectivity matters for: e-governance; the digital economy (freelancing — a genuine KP success story, with Peshawar and Swat producing substantial freelance IT exports); and the surveillance/security architecture.

**Assessment.** Infrastructure's normalizing effect is real but conditional: roads and electricity restore markets and state presence, but without accompanying security (protection from extortion and attack) and justice, they can be captured by militants or by elite networks. Empirical KP evidence — Swat's recovery, Bajaur's market revival — supports the proposition that *infrastructure plus restored security plus services* reduces support for militancy. Infrastructure alone does not.

## 10.6 The IDPs Crisis: Displacement, Return and Rehabilitation

**The scale over time.** Displacement in KP and the FATA since 2008 has been among the largest in the world: **over 6 million people displaced between 2003 and 2014** (EUAA, citing sources); 2–2.5 million in Swat/Malakand (2009); ~1 million from North Waziristan (2014); several hundred thousand from other operations; and recurring waves since — Kurram (November–December 2024: thousands in temporary shelters); **Bajaur (Operation Sarbakaf, mid-2025: ~100,000 displaced)**; and **Tirah Valley (Khyber district), where a second wave began in January 2026 and had reached 33,800 registered families by September 2026**, with a return window agreed for 10–15 October 2026 and disputed by displaced representatives under the banner of Tehreek Mutasireen Tirah (Countercurrents 2026; EUAA 2026; Paradigm Shift 2025).

**Return and rehabilitation.** Pakistan's return-and-rehabilitation architecture has included: TDP return programs (managed by the KP government with FDMA, army and UN support); cash grants for reconstruction (typically Rs 100,000–400,000 per family in various phases); food and NFI distributions; and RDP infrastructure. The documented weaknesses are consistent across episodes: compensation delays and under-coverage (the Tirah package — Rs 10 million for families of the killed, Rs 2.5 million for the injured — was reported delayed into 2026); inadequate verification and exclusion (IDP card fraud and political capture); poor sequencing in harsh seasons (the October return window vs Tirah's early winter); unexploded ordnance risk; and insufficient psychosocial support.

**The security IDP agenda.** Displacement is not merely humanitarian: it (a) removes people from their livelihoods and social protection networks (recruitment vulnerability); (b) generates grievances that armed groups exploit ("the state destroyed your house and promised compensation it never paid"); (c) reduces the population's *stake* in the state; and (d) creates information vacuums in emptied areas that militants exploit. Conversely, *successful return* — with real compensation, services and protection — is one of the most powerful normalization indicators available. The Tirah case in late 2026 is therefore a policy test case: if the return process fails the population (winter, delayed funds, insecurity), the counter-terrorism rationale for the operation will be undermined by its own aftermath.

## 10.7 Psychosocial Trauma and Mental Health in Conflict-Affected Communities

**The burden.** Two decades of exposure to suicide attacks, airstrikes, displacement, bereavement, torture and economic shock have produced a mental-health burden that is almost entirely undocumented and untreated. Available indicators: the national scarcity of psychiatric services (~500 psychiatrists nationwide, with very few in KP's public sector outside Peshawar); the documented trauma among children in Khyber, Darra Adam Khel and Swat (multiple studies; Save the Children and academic surveys); the mental-health consequences of the 2025 floods (WHO reported affected populations with elevated distress); and the specific pathology of *witnesses to atrocity* — including the APS generation.

**Programmatic response.** KP has a government mental-health policy and hospital psychiatric units in Peshawar (including at the Lady Reading Hospital); NGOs (including the Sabawoon program itself) provided counselling; international organizations have run psychosocial support programs after major displacements. But as a system, the response is: urban, clinic-based, underfunded, and disconnected from schools, primary care, and religious institutions where distress is first encountered.

**Why it matters for security.** Untreated trauma: (a) impairs child development and educational outcomes; (b) raises vulnerability to radicalization (revenge motivations and reduced impulse control); (c) degrades community resilience; and (d) is transmitted across generations. Psychosocial support is therefore a *security investment* with an unusually favourable cost-benefit ratio, particularly in schools (trauma-informed teaching), primary care (basic mental health integration), and mosques (pastoral counselling training).

## 10.8 Women's Security, Gender-Based Violence and Female Participation in Peacebuilding

**Security realities.** Women in KP face: high rates of domestic and honour-based violence (under-reported); restricted freedom of movement; limited access to justice (the *jirga* system's exclusion of women; legal pluralism; and the cultural premium on family reputation); economic exclusion (female labour force participation far below male rates; inheritance denial); and conflict-specific harms (widowhood; displacement; sexual violence in displacement settings; and the targeting of female students, teachers and health workers, including the attempted killing of Malala Yousafzai and attacks on girls' schools). Baseline data: female literacy 37 per cent vs male 65 per cent; women constitute 51 per cent of the unemployed; and the merged districts show the province's lowest female schooling (KP Youth Policy 2026; KP Government 2024).

**Policy instruments.** KP has a Women Empowerment Policy Framework (2017) and continuing programs (UN Women's gender-responsive justice work in KP; gender-inclusive service rules reform; women's police stations and desks in some districts; the KP Commission on the Status of Women). The National Security Policy (2022–26) articulated gender security ("full and meaningful participation of women in decision-making, law enforcement, justice, and peacekeeping") — a commitment whose implementation in KP remains limited.

**Women in peacebuilding.** Documented roles: PAIMAN's mothers' networks; UN Women's engagement of **228 Afghan and Pakistani women peace activists** in cross-border dialogue processes (2025); women's organizations (Aurat Foundation, Khwendo Kor, Aware Girls, women's wings of political parties); and female health workers and teachers whose daily work in insecure districts is itself a peacebuilding function. The constraints: cultural gate-keeping; security threats; under-funding; and shrinking civic space.

**Assessment.** The study's gender findings are three: (i) women's *security* deficits are a measurable drag on normalization (a province in which half the population cannot move, work, or seek justice freely has not normalized; the 5 per cent secondary-school completion rate for girls in the NMDs is a structural emergency); (ii) women are *effective* peace actors whose networks reach where police cannot; and (iii) the policy architecture treats gender as a cross-cutting accessory rather than a security-relevant capability — with a corresponding loss of effectiveness in both development and prevention programs.

## 10.9 Tourism as a Normalization Indicator: Swat, Chitral, Dir and the Tourism Revival

**The record.** Tourism in KP has become a genuine and measurable indicator of normalization: **12.4 million tourists visited KP in 2025** (KP Culture and Tourism Authority); over Eid-ul-Adha 2026, KP received **more than 1.26 million tourists — the highest recorded — including 416,794 in Swat** (the single highest provincial destination count), with Naran (187,954), Galiyat (148,828), and 394,682 visitors to merged-district destinations including South Waziristan (144,000) and Orakzai (92,112); tourism revenue over the holiday was estimated at **Rs 8–12 billion** (DGIPR 2026). Comparable holidays in 2025 recorded 932,000 visitors (Eid-ul-Adha) and 306,215 (Eid-ul-Fitr). The government attributes the growth to security improvement, tourism-police deployment and infrastructure.

**But: is it a leading or lagging indicator?** Tourism measures *perceived* security among travellers and depends on mobility, infrastructure and marketing: it can thrive in districts with acute rural insecurity elsewhere (Swat's 2026 attack killed 17 people weeks after record visitor numbers). The correct reading is that tourism revival demonstrates *recoverability* — the social and economic capacity of a district to rebound — which is itself a strategic asset: a district with hotels, jobs and visitors has a constituency and a fiscal stake in normality. The 2026 Swat attack (Kabal; 17 killed) is therefore not only a security setback but an economic attack on a recovery model; its aftermath — whether bookings collapse, whether security forces visibly protect the sector — will indicate the resilience of the recovery.

**Practical agenda.** Tourism protection (the Tourism Police; protective infrastructure; coordination with hotel associations); infrastructure resilience (roads, Gilgit-style check-in systems, weather risk); diversification beyond the top three destinations; and — critically — linking tourism development to merged-district employment (guides, transport, homestays, handicrafts), so that the peace dividend is visible where the conflict has been.

## 10.10 Agriculture, Livelihoods and Rural Economic Recovery

**The agricultural base.** KP's rural economy rests on: wheat, maize, rice (in the Peshawar valley), orchards (Swat, Hazara, Chitral — apples, peaches, citrus), livestock (a major income source, especially for the NMDs), and off-farm labour and remittances. The sector's constraints: smallholder fragmentation; water scarcity and irrigation inefficiency (with the Chashma Right Bank Canal proposed as a transformational project); market access constraints (roads, cold chains, credit); the collapse of horticulture exports at times of border closure and of transport disruption; and the catastrophic effects of floods (2022; 2025's Buner/Swat/Shangla destruction).

**Conflict effects.** Conflict and militancy damage agriculture through: displacement and abandoned land; extortion and "taxation"; the destruction of orchards as punishment (documented in Swat in the militant era); insecurity of transport; the loss of markets in the border belt; and the destruction of the livestock economy through operations and flooding.

**Programs.** Provincial and donor programs include KP-RETP (World Bank-supported), the AIP's agriculture components, livestock vaccination programs, and various value-chain initiatives (olive, honey, dry fruit in the NMDs). The evidence on post-operation agricultural recovery in Swat is broadly positive (orchards rehabilitated; markets restored), in the NMDs much weaker (capital destruction, unresolved land rights, weak marketing).

**Assessment.** Land and water rights regularization in the merged districts is the single most important agricultural policy task, and the most politically difficult: without documented rights, neither investment nor credit nor insurance can function, and disputes will continue to convert into conflict (Chapter 3). The second priority is *market access*: roads, cold storage, and border trade regimes that allow the NMDs' fruit and livestock to reach markets in Afghanistan, the Gulf and Karachi.

## 10.11 CPEC and Economic Zones in KPK: Economic Promise and Security Challenges

**Status.** The Rashakai Special Economic Zone (Nowshera) is KP's flagship CPEC industrial project: 1,000 acres; announced with expectations of 200,000 direct and indirect jobs and ~Rs 347 billion in investment; Century Steel as the first enterprise (with a planned investment raised from an initial $50 million to a reported $360 million); by September 2026, **22 enterprises in progress, ~Rs 68 billion in estimated investment, and ~3,000 expected jobs**, with a mobile-phone manufacturing unit inaugurated that month (cpecinfo 2021; Daily Ittehad 2026). Other KP elements: the Karakoram Highway realignment; hydel projects; and fibre-optic connectivity (a somewhat stalled CPEC component).

**The gap between promise and performance.** Rashakai's actual trajectory — from "200,000 jobs" to ~3,000 expected — illustrates CPEC's KP reality: infrastructure-heavy, employment-light, and slower than promised. Constraints include: security risk perceptions (which raise insurance and security costs); electricity pricing and connectivity; the federal-provincial division of industrial policy; land and utility provision delays; and the narrow export orientation of relocated Chinese firms (with currency and profit-repatriation constraints).

**Security challenges.** CPEC projects and personnel in KP face: attack risk (the 2024 Shangla attack killing five Chinese engineers was the most serious); the requirement for expensive protective deployments; labour-community tensions (wages, local employment quotas); and the broader geopolitical risk that any major incident forces project suspension.

**Assessment.** SEZs can contribute to normalization (jobs, skills, tax base, visible modernity), but their security requirements (guarded enclaves, protected convoys) can also *entrench* the geography of exception, producing "islands of development" disconnected from local economies. The policy target should therefore be: local employment quotas enforced, supplier linkages to local firms, and security arrangements that are visible but not socially corrosive.

## 10.12 The Development–Security Nexus: Theoretical and Empirical Analysis

**Three competing models.** (i) *Sequencing model* (security first): development cannot occur without security; therefore, establish security, then invest. This was the de facto Pakistani approach post-2009. (ii) *Development-first model*: poverty and injustice cause conflict; invest in development and security follows. This model's failure in the FATA of 2003–2019 is documented: development spending (often politically captured) without security and justice did not prevent the insurgency. (iii) *Co-production model (this study's position)*: security and development are mutually constituting — each enables and is enabled by the other — but they are *mediated by institutions*: specifically by (a) justice (the mechanism by which citizens decide the state is a better provider of order than armed actors), (b) fiscal institutionalization (predictable resources make services credible), and (c) local political accountability (which determines whether development is delivered or captured).

**Empirical evidence from KP.** Several natural comparisons support the co-production reading. *Swat post-2009*: security restoration plus investment plus tourism revival produced the most complete normalization in the province — but 2026's attack shows its vulnerability to external sanctuary dynamics. *The NMDs post-2018*: legal integration without *fiscal* integration produced neither security nor development. *Bajaur*: repeated operations since 2008 displaced populations with limited follow-through; the district remains violence-prone despite its market recovery. *Malakand's recovery vs the southern belt's deterioration*: where the state paired force with returning services and civil administration, violence fell; where it did not, it rose.

**The conflict-cost calculation.** Pakistan's cumulative economic losses from terrorism have been estimated at **USD 126.79 billion (2017–18 Economic Survey accounting)** to **USD 150–152 billion (parliamentary and official statements)** across the war-on-terror period, with some analysts estimating an annual GDP cost around 3 per cent; and the human cost is over 70,000 civilian/security deaths nationally (Economic Survey of Pakistan 2017-18; Arab News 2021; Stratagem 2015). For KP specifically, the losses are concentrated: destroyed industry and tourism (now recovering), displacement, the fiscal burden of security (Rs 170 billion annually on police alone, plus the military deployment costs), and — hardest to quantify — the foregone human capital of two generations. Conversely, the *dividend* of normalization is visible in the same data: tourism at 12.4 million visitors; Rashakai's investment; the resumption of markets. A defensible order-of-magnitude estimate — developed in Chapter 15 — places KP's annual conflict-related economic loss in the range of **Rs 400–800 billion** (security expenditures, foregone investment, displacement, and destroyed capital), against which the fiscal settlement of the merged districts (~Rs 192 billion per year promised) is not merely affordable but economically rational: it is a fraction of the cost of conflict.

**Policy conclusion.** Development is not a substitute for security, and security is not a substitute for development. The binding constraint in KP is *institutional*: the state must demonstrate, in the same districts where it fights, that it can protect, adjudicate, and deliver — simultaneously, durably, and with resources it has promised. Anything less will continue to produce what the data record: recovery followed by relapse.
