# CHAPTER 13 — COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS AND INTERNATIONAL LESSONS

## 13.1 Northern Ireland Peace Process: Power-Sharing, Decommissioning and Lessons for KPK

**The settlement.** The Good Friday/Belfast Agreement (1998) ended three decades of conflict (the "Troubles": ~3,500 deaths) through a three-stranded architecture: power-sharing in a devolved assembly; North–South and East–West institutions; and mutual concessions on prisoners, decommissioning and policing. Violence did not end in 1998 — dissident republicans and loyalists persisted, and the killings of the 2000s (including the 1998 Omagh bombing that killed 29) and the 2011 death of a police officer demonstrated the residual threat — but the political process held. Subsequent agreements (St Andrews 2006; the 2005 IRA decommissioning) consolidated it.

**Key mechanisms.** (i) *Inclusion of non-violent politics*: Sinn Féin and the loyalist parties were brought into constitutional politics; the state did not defeat the IRA militarily, nor did it demand ideological surrender — it demanded the end of violence and acceptance of the political process. (ii) *Police reform as a peace dividend*: the Patten Commission (1999) produced the most far-reaching police reform in the developed world — renamed service (PSNI), new recruitment quotas, human-rights-based training, an independent ombudsman and Policing Board. This is arguably the single most transferable element to KPK: the recognition that a security force seen as an instrument of one community cannot deliver normalization. (iii) *Prisoner releases and victim-centred legacy bodies*: early releases were traded for violence-ending; but legacy issues (collusion inquiries, victims' pensions, inquests) remain disputed, illustrating how *unresolved truth* can persist for decades. (iv) *Economic dividend and reconstruction*: investment (including EU peace programmes) helped normalize. (iv) *Third-party facilitation*: US, British and Irish governments as guarantors, plus independent monitors.

**Counterpoints.** Northern Ireland's conditions differ from KP's in crucial ways: the conflict was intra-provincial but within a single developed state; there was no cross-border sanctuary (though arms smuggling existed) and the "Kinahan" organized-crime overlap came later; and the British state's fiscal capacity dwarfed Pakistan's provincial budgets. Its principal relevance is therefore *not* the grand settlement but the three operational doctrines: police reform, inclusive political process, and sequenced prisoner/justice measures with monitoring.

## 13.2 Colombia's Peace Agreement with FARC: DDR Programs and Transitional Justice

**The settlement.** The 2016 Havana agreement between the Colombian government and the FARC ended a 52-year conflict (over 220,000 deaths). It comprised: (i) *rural reform* (land restitution, development); (ii) *political participation* (the FARC's transformation into a political party with guaranteed seats); (iii) *drugs policy* (crop substitution, voluntary eradication); (iv) *transitional justice* (the Special Jurisdiction for Peace, JEP; the Truth Commission; reparations); and (v) *DDR* — with around 13,000 combatants demobilized into reintegration zones, individual reintegration plans, income support, and monitoring.

**Outcomes and lessons.** (i) *DDR can work at scale, but its survival depends on security guarantees*: hundreds of former combatants were assassinated after demobilization (over 400 documented in the years following), and dissident factions (with coca-related interests) re-armed; the lesson for KP is precise: reintegration without protection (from former enemies, criminal groups and rogue state agents) fails. (ii) *Politics as the DDR's endpoint*: the FARC's conversion into a political party was contested domestically but provided a non-violent outlet for membership. (iii) *Partial implementation is corrosive*: delays in land reform and security guarantees drive dissidence and erode the public's faith in the agreement. (iv) *Justice with participation*: the JEP's mix of restorative and retributive sanctions is a model for contexts where blanket amnesty is politically impossible (as in KP, where victims' families would reject impunity) — but it requires enormous institutional investment and years of patience.

## 13.3 Sri Lanka's Post-LTTE Normalization: Military Victory and Political Reconciliation

**The case.** Sri Lanka's military defeat of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) in 2009 ended a 26-year civil war and eliminated the world's most effective insurgency's territorial control. The government's approach to post-war normalization combined: massive military presence in the north/east; infrastructure and economic development (especially in Colombo and the east); a Lessons Learnt and Reconciliation Commission (2011); and minimal political devolution. Reconciliation stalled; militarization persisted; the recommendations of accountability mechanisms were largely unimplemented; the 2019 Easter Sunday bombings (retaliatory-Islamic State-linked) and the 2022 economic collapse showed how the absence of a political settlement leaves structural vulnerabilities.

**Lessons for KP.** (i) *Military victory is not normalization*: Sri Lanka defeated the LTTE but did not end Tamil grievances; the absence of devolution and truth-telling produced a long-term legitimacy deficit that proved internationally costly. (ii) *Development without political settlement buys quiet for a while*. (iii) *The counterfactual of a "hard security" model* is that it can suppress insurgency but cannot produce durable peace — a warning equally applicable to any strategy of pure coercion in KP. Transposed to the province: even a successful campaign against the TTP would not automatically normalize Swat, the NMDs, or Kurram.

## 13.4 Iraqi Kurdistan's Security Transition: Peshmerga, Governance and Economic Development

**The case.** The Kurdistan Region of Iraq (KRI) built a functioning autonomous administration after 1991 (Safe Haven; no-fly zone), with its own Peshmerga forces, two-party system (KDP/PUK), and — from 2003 — substantial oil revenues. It has been more stable than central Iraq but faces: internal party-based security fragmentation (duplicated Peshmerga structures under KDP and PUK control); fiscal dependence on Baghdad transfers and oil; the 2017 independence referendum's disastrous aftermath (loss of Kirkuk; economic crisis); the ISIL war (2014–2017), in which Peshmerga fought; and long-standing governance deficits (corruption, delayed elections, patronage).

**Lessons for KP.** (i) *Autonomy/devolution can stabilize if it is fiscally real*: the KRI's stability rests on revenue-sharing (which it fought over) — an echo of the merged districts' fiscal shortfall. (ii) *Security-sector fragmentation along party lines entrenches instability*: the KDP–PUK duplication is a warning for any provincial force built on patronage — and for the NMD policing model (Chapter 7). (iii) *Internal political unity is a security precondition*: the KRI's biggest vulnerabilities were internal (2017 referendum; the 2013–15 budget standoff), not military — an analogy to the federal–provincial estrangement over KP's security (Chapter 11). (iv) *Oil/dependable rent economies are not a substitute for institutionalized statebuilding.*

## 13.5 Afghanistan's Ongoing Challenges: Lessons in What Not to Do

**The record, 2001–2026.** Afghanistan's post-2001 experience offers negative lessons unmatched in the contemporary record: (i) *DDR without a political settlement*: the Afghanistan Peace and Reintegration Programme (APRP) and earlier DDR efforts demobilized tens of thousands of combatants superficially while warlord networks — and their militias — retained power, ensuring that demobilization did not reduce armed politics (some DDR beneficiaries rejoined the Taliban). (ii) *Counter-insurgency dependent on foreign forces*: the ANSF collapsed within weeks of the allied withdrawal (August 2021), demonstrating that security forces built on external funding, airlift and logistics collapse when the patron leaves. (iii) *Corruption and parallel governance*: the Kabul government's legitimacy deficit — warlord capture, electoral fraud, and predatory local administration — handed the Taliban their strongest recruiting argument ("we deliver justice, they sell it"). (iv) *Sanctuary myopia*: the alliance's toleration of Taliban sanctuaries in Pakistan (2005–2021) and Pakistan's toleration of TTP sanctuaries in Afghanistan (2021–2026) show both how sanctuaries extend conflicts and how each side's short-term calculus lengthens the war. (v) *The peace process's design failure*: the US–Taliban Doha agreement (2020) excluded the Afghan government and traded withdrawal for promises rather than verification, producing the collapse of the government before any negotiated settlement.

**Lessons for KP.** The Afghan record is the study's principal caution against five fallacies: that security forces can be *rented* rather than built; that *reintegration* without politics and economics works; that *corruption* is a side-issue in counter-insurgency; that *sanctuaries* can be tolerated with impunity; and that *peace processes* designed to satisfy external actors will hold.

## 13.6 Mozambique and Sierra Leone: African Models of Post-Conflict Recovery

**Mozambique (1992 Rome General Peace Accords).** The accords between Frelimo and Renamo ended a 16-year civil war; UN-supported DDR demobilized ~90,000 combatants; multiparty elections (1994) brought Renamo into politics; and sustained growth followed. The success factors: a genuine mutual hurting stalemate; effective third-party mediation (the Sant'Egidio community, with UN and Italian). The cautionary suffix: Renamo's return to armed conflict (2012–2016) after protracted exclusion and electoral disputes in 2013–16, and the Cabo Delgado insurgency (from 2017) that revealed how a resource-rich periphery with weak governance can become a new conflict frontier. The KP relevance: demobilization success does not immunize against future insurgency if political exclusion and peripheral governance persist.

**Sierra Leone (1999 Lomé → 2002 end).** The rebel RUF's terror campaign (amputations, child soldiering) ended through a combination of British military intervention (2000), UN peacekeeping, DDR of ~70,000 combatants, a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and a Special Court (which tried Charles Taylor and others) — a case demonstrating that hybrid justice (truth commission + court) with sustained external involvement can consolidate peace. The KP applicability is limited by scale and geopolitics but the *model* — local truth-telling plus selective judicial accountability — is relevant to Kurram, Swat and the NMDs, where impunity is pervasive and grievance accumulates.

## 13.7 The Philippines' Mindanao Peace Process: Autonomous Governance and Security

**The case.** The Mindanao conflict between the Philippine state and Moro insurgencies produced two settlements: with the Moro National Liberation Front (1996) and the far more consequential 2014 Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro with the Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF), institutionalized through the Bangsamoro Organic Law (2018) and the establishment of the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region (BARMM, 2019). Key mechanisms: (i) *asymmetric autonomy* with a regional parliamentary government, fiscal arrangements (block grants, tax powers) and specified competences; (ii) *phased decommissioning* of the MILF's 40,000-strong force — with donors providing transition payments — implemented in stages that continued after 2019; (iii) *third-party monitoring* (the International Monitoring Team, IMT, with Malaysian, Indonesian, Japanese and other participation; the Independent Decommissioning Body; the Third-Party Monitoring Team reporting publicly); (iv) *militia normalization* (dealing with private armed groups); and (v) *economic development* (roads, social services).

**Outcomes and lessons.** Progress has been real (violence declined among the main groups; BARMM has governed since 2019) but partial: residual jihadist groups (BIFF, IS-linked factions) exploited the transition; clan politics and electoral violence persist; the 2022 elections tested the transition; and the normalization annexes (decommissioning, disbanding private armies, policing) lagged implementation. **KP relevance** — the closest structural analogy available: (i) *autonomy with real fiscal transfers* is the analogue of the merged-district settlement: promised transfers that are not delivered undermine the political class the settlement creates; (ii) *third-party monitoring with public reporting* — an approach entirely absent from KP's merger implementation — creates accountability and confidence; (iii) *phased, funded decommissioning of armed groups* is the analogue of a future TTP disengagement process, should one ever be possible; and (iv) the residual-jihadist problem (BIFF/ISKP-analogue) persists in every post-settlement environment, requiring sustained counter-terrorism even after a political settlement.

## 13.8 Chechnya/Dagestan: Russia's Counter-Insurgency Model (Critical Assessment)

**The model.** Russia's North Caucasus campaigns (two wars in Chechnya: 1994–96, 1999–2009; continuous operations in Dagestan and Ingushetia) produced a form of stabilization that combined: overwhelming force (massive artillery and air campaigns; "cleansing" operations — documented mass abuses); "Chechenization"/"Kadyrovization" — transferring security to a local strongman, Ramzan Kadyrov, with a large militia/police apparatus funded from Moscow; extreme coercion plus selective social subsidy; and the suppression of all independent political activity, media and civil society. The measured outcome: open insurgency in Chechnya was largely defeated; extremist violence shifted to Dagestan/Ingushetia in the late 2000s and declined substantially by the 2010s — while producing: thousands killed and displaced; mass human-rights abuses (documented by Memorial and others, including "disappearances," torture, and persecution of LGBT individuals); a repressive, personality-based regional regime; and export of violence (the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing by Chechen-linked brothers; ISKP recruitment from the Caucasus).

**Critical assessment and relevance.** The Chechen case demonstrates that *militarily effective* counter-insurgency is achievable through extreme coercion — and that its costs include the destruction of the rule of law, the corruption of the state, exported violence, and the impossibility of measuring "success" against anything but the absence of armed self-reporting. It is not a transferable model for Pakistan for three reasons: (i) Pakistan is not an authoritarian federation able to impose a Kadyrov-type equilibrium without dismantling its own constitutional order (though the 27th Amendment's centralization moves in that direction); (ii) the Chechen analogue would require a monolithic local enforcer with consolidated power — which no single faction in KP possesses; (iii) the model leaves the recruitment narrative intact and merely suppresses its expression, producing periodic recurrence. Its *one* legitimate lesson: local security forces with local legitimacy (however manufactured) outperform foreign ones — but only if "local legitimacy" is built through consent, not terror.

**Dagestan's specifics** are arguably more relevant to KP: a region with a mixed ethnic composition, a Salafi-jihadist insurgency operating from rural and mountainous sanctuaries, an ethnic and governance mix, and an insurgency that was ultimately suppressed by a combination of special operations, amnesties, and co-opted religious authorities — with recurring resurgences whenever the co-optation or coercion lapsed.

## 13.9 Comparative Framework: Key Variables for Successful Security Normalization

| Variable | Evidence from cases | KP position (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Political settlement** | NI, Colombia, Mindanao, Mozambique (1992) succeeded where settlements created non-violent outlets; Sri Lanka, Chechnya & Afghanistan failed without them | Absent — no settlement; TTP excluded from politics; PTM banned |
| **2. DDR/reintegration** | Colombia & Mindanao: effective with financing, security guarantees, political integration; Afghanistan: failed without politics; Cabo Delgado: reconstruction ≠ reconciliation | Nascent/marginal (Chapters 8) |
| **3. Police & justice reform** | NI: the single most consequential reform; Colombia: rural justice gaps drove dissidence | KP Act 2017 progressive; implementation partial; NMD policing unresolved |
| **4. Economic dividend & reconstruction** | NI/Mindanao: consequential; Sierra Leone: partial; Mozambique (Cabo Delgado): insufficient | Swat recovered; NMDs' fiscal shortfall catastrophic |
| **5. Sanctuary management** | Afghanistan/Pakistan: sanctuary prolongs; Colombia: neighbours cooperated | Acute (Afghanistan); unresolved |
| **6. Inclusion & local legitimacy** | Mindanao autonomy, NI inclusion, KRI devolution vs Sri Lanka/Chechnya exclusion | NMD local government absent; PTM/PTM-aligned youth excluded |
| **7. Transitional justice/victims** | Sierra Leone (TRC+SC); Colombia (JEP); NI (legacy unresolved → 25+ years of dispute) | Absent; impunity widespread; no victims' process |
| **8. Third-party guarantees & monitoring** | Mindanao (IMT/TPMT); NI (guarantors); Mozambique (Sant'Egidio) | Episodic (Qatar/Türkiye/China mediation) without institution |
| **9. Time horizon** | All successful cases: 10–25+ years of sustained attention | Policy horizon is election-cycle; no long-horizon framework |
| **10. State coherence** | KRI fragmentation, Afghan corruption: internal incoherence lethal; NI required London–Dublin–Belfast alignment | Federal–provincial estrangement is acute |

**Synthesis.** Across cases, no variable is sufficient alone, and the *sequencing varies* — but the recurring pattern is: a *political framework* (settlement, autonomy, or inclusion) + *security-sector reform* + *economic dividend* + *legitimacy mechanisms* (justice/truth) delivered over a decade or more, with third-party monitoring. Where any element is missing, normalization stalls even when violence declines.

## 13.10 Transferable Lessons and Contextual Adaptations for KPK

**Lessons that transfer directly.**
1. **Police reform is the load-bearing wall** (Northern Ireland). KP cannot normalize while its police are simultaneously victims, occupiers and ineffective — the Patten-style agenda (rights-based training, community accountability, representative recruitment, independent oversight, professional investigations) applies with modifications to the KP Police Act 2017 framework and the NMD policing question.
2. **Reintegration must be protected and financed** (Colombia, Mindanao): ex-combatants who are killed, excluded or impoverished become the next insurgency. KP's deradicalization programs need security guarantees, employment, and community acceptance.
3. **Third-party monitoring creates accountability** (Mindanao): an independent monitoring body with public reporting — for the merged districts' fiscal commitments and for any security arrangement with Afghanistan — would address the implementation-gap pattern that defines KP's policy history.
4. **Fiscal delivery is the political class's lifeblood** (Mindanao/BARMM, KRI): promised transfers not delivered discredit the settlement's local champions; KP's Rs 568 billion shortfall is precisely this failure.
5. **Justice must be visible, but need not be retributive-only** (Sierra Leone, Colombia): hybrid approaches (local truth processes; selective accountability; reparations) can substitute where full trials are impossible — relevant to Kurram and to state-abuse victims.
6. **The security sector cannot be a rented service** (Afghanistan): the police and courts must be built as indigenous institutions with public legitimacy, not as donor-funded expeditionary capacity.

**Lessons requiring adaptation (not transfer).**
7. **Peace agreements with insurgents** (NI, Mindanao): KP's TTP is not a political movement with negotiable demands comparable to the IRA or MILF; any negotiation must deal with a coalition whose factions split (JuA 2026) and whose sponsorship base is external — so the *negotiation design* must be decentralized, conditional, verified, and staged (cessation → confidence-building → disengagement → reintegration), and must not repeat the failed pattern of unverified deals (2004–2008; 2021–22).
8. **Autonomy as a solution**: KP's challenge is not secession but integration; the Mindanao analogy is *fiscal devolution and local self-government*, not sovereignty.
9. **International intervention**: no external force will provide security in KP; the "third party" roles are monitoring, mediation, financing and evaluation — and those are available.

**The synthesis for policy.** KP's optimal pathway is a *hybrid* of the Northern Ireland (police/justice/inclusion), Mindanao (fiscal autonomy and monitoring), Colombia (reintegration and transitional justice), and Sierra Leone (truth plus selective accountability) models, adapted to the conditions of an entrenched, sanctuary-supported insurgency within a nuclear-armed federal state. Chapter 15 translates this into an implementable roadmap; Chapter 14 establishes its strategic context.
