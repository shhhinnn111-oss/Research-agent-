# CHAPTER 12 — ROLE OF CIVIL SOCIETY, MEDIA AND NON-STATE ACTORS

## 12.1 Civil Society Organizations Working on Peace and Security in KPK

**The landscape.** KP's peace-oriented civil society comprises five clusters: (i) *PVE and peace-education organizations* (PAIMAN Alumni Trust, PEAD Foundation, Khwendo Kor, Aware Girls, and a network of smaller district-level groups); (ii) *human rights and legal-aid organizations* (the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan's KP chapters; the Legal Aid Society's local partners; women's legal-aid groups); (iii) *media and research organizations* (journalists' safety networks; research groups producing district-level reporting); (iv) *victims' and survivors' groups* (including families of the APS attack; displaced persons' associations such as Tehreek Mutasireen Tirah; and martyrs' family associations); and (v) *community-based and faith-based organizations* (welfare societies, zakat committees, mosque-linked charities, and youth clubs).

**Contributions.** Documented contributions include: mothers' networks and family-based counselling against radicalization (PAIMAN); peace education in schools and madrasas; psychosocial support for conflict-affected children; practical support to displaced persons; victim documentation (including the documentation of drone-strike and operation casualties); monitoring closed schools and girls' education restrictions; advocacy for de-mining; and the provision of the *evidence base* that policy discussions consume (including the district-level human development series by SDPI and the incident monitoring of civil-society-linked researchers).

**Constraints.** Five are structural: (i) *funding insecurity* — dependence on short project grants, with periods of abrupt suspension (including the 2018–2020 USAID/development-funding disruptions and the subsequent contraction of international development financing); (ii) *legal-administrative friction* — NOC requirements, registration conditions, and complex reporting mandated for foreign-funded organizations; (iii) *smearing and intimidation* — organizations critical of military conduct or government policy face campaigns, and staff face threats from both militants and (in some documented cases) state actors; (iv) *physical risk* — attacks on NGO offices, and the killing or abduction of aid workers; and (v) *shrinking space* generally (see §12.6).

**Assessment.** Civil society's comparative advantage in KP is *access* — to women, children, households and displaced communities that neither police nor army can reach — and its comparative weakness is *scale and sustainability*: the sector is small, project-driven, and politically exposed. A normalization strategy that fails to protect and finance it loses its most credible preventers.

## 12.2 The Jirga System: Traditional Conflict Resolution and Its Modern Relevance

**What it is.** The jirga is a deliberative assembly for dispute resolution — from small local *marakas* to district and grand jirgas — grounded in Pashtun custom (*riwaj*/*tor*), with procedures that vary by locality, sect and district. Its strengths are recognized: speed, accessibility, legitimacy, cost, and context-sensitivity; its verdicts often include compensation (*swara*, *nazrana*), restitution and reconciliation, and its proceedings can repair relationships in ways that courts cannot. Its most consequential recent successes are in Kurram (the 2011 truce ending a multi-year conflict that killed ~2,000; the 14-point accord of January 2025; the May 2025 *Aman Teega*), in Swat's de-escalation episodes, in Tirah's return negotiations, and in hundreds of localized feuds.

**Its pathologies.** Documented critiques: (i) *exclusion* — women and youth are generally excluded from decision-making; (ii) *punitive norms* — verdicts have historically included practices that violate constitutional and human rights (forced marriage/compensation involving women; collective fines); (iii) *enforcement* — jirgas cannot execute decisions against armed actors and depend on state power they often distrust; (iv) *capture* — military, religious and political actors have used grand jirgas for legitimation, and some proceedings become instruments of political theatre; and (v) *legal pluralism* — where jirgas adjudicate criminal matters, they displace the statutory system and the rights it guarantees.

**Reform directions.** Comparative practice (Chapter 13) and Pakistani proposals converge on: formalizing jirgas as *mediators* rather than *adjudicators* (a role adjacent to the DRC model); mandatory inclusion of women's *nominated representatives* in civil disputes involving women; a prohibition on verdicts violating statutory rights; vetting of members; state support for implementation; and explicit disentanglement from terrorism prosecutions (no jirga amnesty for grave crimes). None of this is easy in the tribal belt, but the alternative — either criminalizing the jirga (destroying legitimacy) or ignoring it (entrenching illegality) — has failed.

## 12.3 Tribal Elders and Maliks: Declining Influence and Security Implications

**The collapse.** Between 2004 and 2014, militants systematically targeted tribal notables: hundreds of *maliks* and elders were assassinated (with jirga massacres in the NMDs that killed dozens at a time), driven out, or forced to surrender their positions; the state, meanwhile, progressively suspended allowances and patronage as the Political Agent system wound down and the merger abolished it. Independent accounts describe a *generational erasure* of the intermediary class, with consequences that are still working through the system: absent interlocutors, absent community negotiators, and absent bearers of local memory and customary process.

**Who replaced them?** Four substitutes have emerged: (i) elected politicians (MPAs/MNAs from the merged districts) whose authority is electoral but not fiscal; (ii) *peace committee* figures (often younger, sometimes armed, and dependent on state security patronage); (iii) *religious* authority (clerics, madrasa heads) whose authority in some districts exceeds that of the state; and (iv) the militants' own "spokesmen" and enforcers — a role that completes the rupture of the old order.

**Security implications.** The absence of legitimate intermediaries has three effects: (a) *intelligence degradation* (no trusted local interlocutors to talk to); (b) *communication failure* (the state cannot negotiate or de-escalate locally without intermediaries); and (c) *transition risk*: post-operation settlements and return processes depend on local brokers whose legitimacy is contested — as in Tirah's 2026 return negotiations and Kurram's repeated accords, where jirgas succeed temporarily but their enforcement collapses without sustained state and community investment.

**Assessment.** Rebuilding legitimate intermediary capacity — through elected local government, formalized jirgas, protected elder networks, and women's and youth inclusion — is a precondition for the sustainability of any security gains. This is a *capability-building* task, not a nostalgic one; the old malik system was itself a colonial artifact, and its restoration is neither possible nor desirable.

## 12.4 The Role of Media: Conflict Reporting, Peace Journalism and Propaganda

**The media ecology.** KP hosts a dense media landscape: national outlets (Dawn, The News, Express Tribune, Geo, ARY, private TV) with substantial Peshawar bureaus; a vibrant regional press; radio (including Pashto broadcasts with wide rural reach); and a large online ecosystem. Its reports are indispensable to the historical record that this study relies upon.

**Practices and pressures.** (i) *Courage*: KP journalists have documented operations, displacement, casualties, refugee policy, and militant violence at enormous personal risk — KP and the merged districts have been among the world's most dangerous places for journalists, with dozens killed (including the 2021 killing of Shaheena Shaheen in Khyber and the long roster of casualties documented by press-freedom organizations). (ii) *Pressure*: reporting on military conduct, disappearances, and militant taxation has been restricted by official pressure, access denial, legal threats (PECA and ATA charges against journalists), and physical risk; the PTM ban further limited coverage through political prohibitions on the movement's activities. (iii) *Conflict-insensitive practice*: some coverage amplifies sectarian, ethnic and conspiracy framings; "Afghan" and "terrorist" are often conflated; the reporting of casualty claims by militant media (Umar Media claims of attacks and killing counts, republished widely) amplifies the militants' information objectives; and the construction of a *national security narrative* in some state-aligned outlets reduces space for evidence-based reporting on operations' civilian harm. (iv) *Peace journalism deficits*: solutions-oriented, victim-centred, non-sectarian reporting exists (e.g., reporting on Swat's recovery; the documentation of peace jirgas) but is not systematically supported.

**Assessment.** Media in KP is simultaneously a security actor (framing threat and policy), a restraint on abuses, and a vulnerability. The policy implications: protect journalists (transparent investigation of attacks on media); decriminalize professional reporting (resolve PECA/ATA misuse); and support peace journalism with training, safety and financing — especially in Pashto and the merged districts where trust deficits are deepest.

## 12.5 Social Media: Radicalization Tool or Peacebuilding Platform?

**The dual character.** Social media in KP plays both roles simultaneously. *(1) Radicalization and mobilization:* TTP and ISKP propaganda distribution (Chapters 3, 8); sectarian incitement (Kurram's 2024 escalation included a documented role for sectarian social media content, as the January 2025 agreement's clause on "those fomenting on social media" attests); recruitment through group chats; and — in a different register — the circulation of graphic images of violence that magnify terror's psychological effects. *(2) Peacebuilding and accountability:* social media has been central to peace mobilization (the 2022–23 Pashtun long marches; the Swat and Waziristan protest movements against militancy; village-level mutual-aid networks during floods; and displaced-persons' advocacy such as the Tirah committees). It has also been the principal channel through which the human cost of the conflict is documented and contested — including the #SwatSaniha mobilization after the June 2025 attack.

**Policy tension.** Pakistan's regulatory posture — platform blocking; PECA prosecutions for online speech; content takedown (1.3 million URLs by 2023) — is broad and insufficiently targeted: it has produced documented prosecutions of journalists and activists for lawful expression while failing to eliminate extremist content, which migrates to new platforms and channels. The counter-narrative and PVE fields need online presence and credible local voices, not blanket restrictions.

**Assessment.** No serious normalization strategy can ignore the information domain. The minimal credible framework: (i) targeted, reviewed, appealable removal of *incitement to violence* (with published criteria and transparency reporting); (ii) protection of lawful speech (repeal or reform of PECA's broad speech offences); (iii) support for Pashto/Urdu counter-narrative content produced by local credible voices; and (iv) digital literacy in schools and madrasas.

## 12.6 The Pashtun Tahafuz Movement (PTM): Demands, Dynamics and Security Discourse

**History and demands.** The PTM grew out of the Mahsud Tahafuz Movement (May 2014), founded by students to demand de-mining and compensation after operations in Waziristan; renamed in 2018, it became the largest Pashtun civil mobilization in decades through rallies across Pakistan demanding: an end to extrajudicial killings and enforced disappearances; removal of landmines; freedom of movement (checkpoint abuses); the release of the detained; and accountability for the human costs of counter-insurgency — with a rejected demand for a truth and reconciliation framework. Its mobilization capacity was demonstrated in 2018–2019 and again in the 2021–2023 "long marches" after enforced disappearances (the disappearance and killing of Nazimuddin and others).

**The ban and its context.** The PTM was proscribed in October 2024 under ATA Section 11B (Chapter 4); a prior attempt at legal action (2019) and provincial bans on its leadership predated it. The state's case against the PTM rests on: provocative rhetoric against state institutions; slogans offensive to the military's identity; alleged incitement; and the risk of ethnic polarization in a war zone. The movement and its defenders emphasize its non-violence, its rights-based demands, and the absence of any documented commission of terrorism by its leadership.

**Analytical assessment.** The PTM is the most important non-violent Pashtun political movement since the Khudai Khidmatgar, and its treatment is the clearest current test of whether Pakistan can distinguish between *militancy* and *dissent*. The ban has (a) removed a non-violent channel for grievance articulation; (b) criminalized significant parts of the Pashtun political spectrum, particularly youth; (c) complicated counter-insurgency's local intelligence and cooperation networks (the movement's supporters include the families whose cooperation the state needs); and (d) been widely read as confirming the movement's core claim (that the state treats Pashtun grievance as security threat). For normalization, the pragmatic question is not whether to accept all the PTM's rhetoric but whether a democratic state can afford to outlaw a non-violent political movement in the middle of a counter-insurgency: the evidence from KP says it cannot.

## 12.7 Religious Leaders and Institutions: Constructive and Destructive Roles

**Constructive roles.** Religious institutions in KP perform welfare functions (madrasas educating poor children), social mediation, and moral authority. Anti-militant religious voices have been consequential: the Paigham-e-Pakistan declaration (2018); wafaq board statements; clerics who officiated at the funerals of slain police and condemned terrorist attacks; the anti-polio-vaccine reversal by many clerics after erring in some areas; and the counter-narrative content used in deradicalization (Chapter 8).

**Destructive roles.** A subset of institutions and leaders has supplied ideological justification, recruits and logistics to armed groups (Chapter 4); the 2019 registration framework and its 2024 reversal illustrate the sector's political leverage; and sectarian clerics have stoked inter-communal conflict (Kurram; Hangu; parts of Peshawar).

**The policy choice.** The state's relationship with religious authority in KP oscillates between instrumentalization (partners in the counter-narrative) and confrontation (regulation). Neither pure approach has worked. The available evidence suggests a *conditional-partnership* model: fund and credential moderate, anti-militancy institutions; enforce transparency (financing, foreign students, curricula standards) through consultative mechanisms (the Punjab registration model); and hold accountable, through law, the institutions that cross into incitement — while avoiding blanket suspicion of the sector, which produces only defensiveness and non-compliance.

## 12.8 International NGOs and Development Partners in KPK

**Presence.** KP has hosted one of Pakistan's largest development-partner footprints: UN agencies (UNDP, UNICEF, UNHCR, WFP, UN Women, IOM, UNODC); IFIs (World Bank, ADB, IFAD); bilateral donors (US and UK historical funding; EU; Germany; Japan; China); and international NGOs (Save the Children, IRC, ACTED, Islamic Relief, and others). Programs have covered: displacement response; livelihood restoration; education; health (polio, nutrition); water and sanitation; governance; and peacebuilding/PVE (with UN Women, USIP and others funding pilot work).

**Contributions and limitations.** Contributions: humanitarian response at scale; technical assistance in justice and police reform; evidence and monitoring (including casualty documentation and human development data); financing for education and health; and PVE pilot programming. Limitations: (i) *short-termism and projectization* — donor cycles rarely align with a decade-long normalization requirement; (ii) *absorption and access* constraints (security, government NOC requirements); (iii) *coordination gaps* with provincial priorities and between donors; (iv) *political sensitivities* around monitoring casualties and civil-society support; and (v) the *financing shift*: the global decline of development assistance (and the diversion of funds to other crises) has reduced INGO presence in KP's conflict districts precisely as needs grew.

**Assessment.** Development partners cannot substitute for the state's role in security and justice; but they hold a comparative advantage in *education, health, livelihood and protection programs* — the soft infrastructure of normalization — and in *independent evaluation*, which KP's security sector almost entirely lacks. Their most valuable contribution would be a coordinated, decade-horizon program focused on the merged districts, conditioned on the government's fiscal delivery.

## 12.9 Youth Activism and Civic Engagement for Peace

**Forms.** Documented youth activism includes: the PTM's youth base and its marches; Swat's protest movements (against militancy and against military operations in different periods — including the Pasoon network's organizing, which produced the rally during which the 2 August 2026 Kabal attack occurred); student unions (long banned and only recently partially restored) and campus civic groups; sports and arts movements (cricket in the tribal districts; the recently established literary festivals); the digital freelancers who have made Peshawar and Swat hubs of online work; and village-level volunteer networks during disasters (the flood responses of 2022 and 2025).

**Constraints.** Youth civic engagement faces: high unemployment; the collapse of student representative institutions; the risks of protest under security laws; distrust of parties; and the lack of funded platforms. Militancy competes for the same cohort with offers of salary, status and purpose.

**Assessment.** Youth activism is both the primary recruitment pool and the primary peace constituency: the same cohort that militancy targets is the one that fills peace marches, volleyball courts, and freelance training programs. Policy should therefore treat youth engagement as a *security program*: sports and cultural facilities (a cheap, high-visibility intervention); funded internship/training pipelines; protected student organizations; and support for youth-led civil society.

## 12.10 Women's Organizations and Gender-Inclusive Security

**Organizations.** KP's women's movement includes: large national organizations with provincial programming (Aurat Foundation, more than four decades active); local providers (Khwendo Kor); women-led PVE groups (Aware Girls under Gulalai Ismail, which faced state pressure and whose leadership relocated); the KP Commission on the Status of Women; women's wings of political parties; and informal networks of female teachers, health workers and madrasa graduates.

**Roles in security.** Women's organizations have: delivered psychosocial support and family counselling; run peace education and interfaith programs; documented violence against women and girls; advocated on girls' education and school closures; supported displaced women; and engaged in cross-border dialogue (UN Women's 228-participant Afghan–Pakistani women peacebuilder network). The National Security Policy's gender commitment provides a formal basis; implementation is minimal.

**Assessment.** The evidence from Pakistan and internationally indicates that women's participation strengthens both prevention programs and community-level mediation (particularly in family and youth radicalization cases). In KP, women's participation is constrained by security, convention and funding — yet the female cohort (37 per cent literacy; five per cent secondary completion in the NMDs) is simultaneously the province's most under-utilized peacebuilding asset. The specific recommendations: fund women-led PVE organizations with long-horizon grants; create safe mechanisms (women's councils, female jirga auxiliaries) to participate in dispute resolution; expand female police recruitment and women's desks (Chapter 7); and protect female students and teachers with targeted security measures (routes, escort, secure campuses).

## 12.11 Private Sector and the Business Community's Role in Normalization

**The business voice.** KP's business community — chambers of commerce (Peshawar, Swat, D.I. Khan, Mardan), sector associations (marble, hotels, transporters, mining, pharmacy), and the freelancer/digital economy (a growing export sector) — has consistently articulated the costs of insecurity: extortion, KFR, transport disruption, insurance and security costs, and loss of tourism and investment. Business associations have supported relief (flood and displacement response), engaged in peace advocacy, and occasionally brokered locally.

**Business as a security actor.** Three roles are documented: (a) *employers* — the primary mechanism of reintegration (Chapter 8), though few firms hire ex-combatants, citing risk; (b) *advocates* — chambers have pressed for infrastructure (roads, electricity, border trade reopening) and for the release of held-up funds; (c) *targets and funders* — extortion and protection payments make businesses both victims and, involuntarily, financiers of armed groups (a dilemma most firms cannot resolve individually and which requires collective state action).

**Assessment.** The private sector's stake in normalization is large but unstructured. The most valuable policy moves are: (i) *public-private security compacts* (joint vetting of threats; coordinated protection of corridors; support for tourism routes with insured security); (ii) *employment incentives* for hiring at-risk youth and rehabilitees (subsidies, certification, assurances); and (iii) *transparency against extortion* (a confidential reporting channel with police protection, breaking the silence that allows extortion networks to operate).

## 12.12 Building a Culture of Peace: Long-Term Social Transformation

**What is meant by "culture of peace" in KP.** Not sentimentality, but a set of institutionalized practices and public attitudes: rejection of violence as a political instrument; acceptance of sectarian and ethnic pluralism; education that teaches critical thinking and civic values; commemoration of all victims; and the availability of peaceful channels (courts, elections, unions, media) for the grievances that currently find violent outlets.

**What would build it.** Seven tested components: (1) *curriculum and teachers* — civic education, critical thinking, anti-sectarian content, teacher training; (2) *memorialization and truth* — public recognition of all victims, including of state operations, with the APS memorialization being a precedent for national unity around victimhood; (3) *the arts and sports* — the most scalable counter-recruitment instruments (music, Pashto literary revival, cricket and football leagues in violence-affected districts); (4) *media pluralism* — peace journalism, local language content, journalists' protection; (5) *religious and tribal institutions* — constructive roles formalized and funded; (6) *youth and women's participation* — as above; and (7) *visible justice* — nothing builds a culture of peace more than seeing perpetrators convicted and victims compensated (Chapter 7).

**The counterfactual risk.** The alternative to a culture of peace is a culture of endurance: a society in which violence is understood as normal, revenge as honourable, and the state as an unreliable protector. KP has elements of both cultures today; the balance between them over the next decade will determine the durability of whatever security gains are achieved. That balance is not determined by military operations; it is determined by schools, courts, compensation payments, media, and the honesty of public accounting for what has happened.
