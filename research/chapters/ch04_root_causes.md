# CHAPTER 4 — ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS

## Introduction: From Lists to Systems

Root-cause analysis of KP's insecurity too often degenerates into lists: poverty, madrasas, the Afghan war, bad governance, Pashtun marginalization, "foreign hands." Lists are analytically useless because they cannot discriminate among drivers that (a) *create* conflict, (b) *trigger* it, (c) *accelerate* it, and (d) merely *correlate* with it. This chapter advances a four-layer model — **structural conditions, proximate triggers, accelerants, and sustaining mechanisms** — and maps the familiar catalogue of causes into it. The model's analytical payoff appears in the discussion of Kurram, Swat and the NMDs, where the same nominal cause (sectarianism, militancy, tribal conservatism) plays different roles in each layer.

The chapter's central argument is that KP's conflict is best explained by **institutional exclusion operating on an armed, networked periphery, within a geopolitically open borderland**. Grievance provides the *recruitment narrative*; sanctuary and weapons supply the *capability*; war economy and factional politics supply the *duration*; and the absence of a just, resourced, locally legitimate governance offer supplies the *recurrence*.

## 4.1 Political Marginalization and Governance Deficits

**The long exclusion.** For seventy years, FATA's residents were excluded from ordinary courts, taxation-as-citizenship, provincial legislation, police protection, and local government. The 25th Amendment ended the legal exclusion; the *practical* exclusion persists in measurable forms: the merged districts have the province's lowest human development indicators (SDPI 2025), the weakest road connectivity, the thinnest health infrastructure, and a judicial presence that remains skeletal (Chapters 5 and 10). Meanwhile, the settled districts of KP have experienced their own variant of exclusion: provincial governments have had limited control over security policy; federal agencies — the military, the Interior Ministry, NACTA — dominate counter-terrorism decision-making; and fiscal autonomy is constrained by the province's dependence on federal transfers and by withheld payments (the war-on-terror dues dispute is a running grievance; CM Afridi publicly cited delays in releasing constitutional dues in 2025–26) (Wikipedia 2026e).

**Measuring the governance deficit.** Several indicators triangulate. (i) *Local government*: KP held local elections in two phases in 2021–22, but the devolution of powers, funds and functions under the KP Local Government Act has been repeatedly delayed; in the NMDs, local councils remain largely absent, and the promised "transition from Political Agents to Deputy Commissioners" produced an administration with formal authority but no functional capacity to deliver services. (ii) *Justice*: district courts in the NMDs were established belatedly and unevenly; the Peshawar High Court has repeatedly intervened on the non-implementation of merged-district reforms. (iii) *Service delivery*: school closures lasting months in Kurram, Bajaur, Khyber, Mohmand and North Waziristan have been documented; health facilities and their staff are frequently attacked or withdrawn (EUAA 2026). (iv) *Citizen experience*: surveys and journalistic accounts consistently describe a state experienced as predatory (checkpoints, night raids, collective fines) rather than protective — a perception that militants exploit ("the state takes your boys; we give them dignity and a purpose").

**The mechanism.** Political marginalization does not produce violence directly. It produces *low-cost recruitment, low-information environments, and high legitimacy costs for the state*. Where citizens cannot seek justice, they seek protection; where they cannot seek protection, they accept the protection offered by armed actors, even at a cost. ICG's 2026 report captures the reciprocal dynamic: many KP residents "reject jihadism but also resent the military's heavy-handed tactics," creating a legitimacy space that counter-insurgency cannot enlist and militancy can inhabit (ICG 2026).

## 4.2 Socio-Economic Deprivation: Poverty, Unemployment and Inequality

**The macro trend.** KP's poverty rate rose from 28.7 per cent to 35.3 per cent in the FY2024-25 estimates — the second-highest provincial rate after Balochistan's 47 per cent, and well above Punjab's 23.3 per cent (Planning Ministry 2026; Economic Survey 2025-26). National poverty reached 28.9 per cent, and inequality rose to its highest level in 27 years (Gini-equivalent inequality index 32.7 nationally, 29.4 in KP). The drivers identified by the Planning Ministry include IMF-programme fiscal consolidation, energy tariff adjustments, inflation, disaster losses and weak growth — a policy mix that, as the World Bank's provincial estimates also suggest, pressed hardest on the poorer provinces (KP Youth Policy 2026).

**Labour market.** National unemployment reached 7.1 per cent (5.9 million people) in the FY2024-25 estimates, the highest in 21 years; youth unemployment (15–24) is substantially higher, and KP's educated youth are disproportionately affected: PIDE's March 2025 assessment found 31 per cent of educated youth jobless, with women constituting 51 per cent of the unemployed (KP Youth Policy 2026). The scale of the jobs deficit is illustrated by a single statistic: in mid-2025, 866,000 candidates applied for 16,454 teaching vacancies in KP — more than 52 applicants per post. In the NMDs, the combined effect of displacement, destroyed enterprises, and missing banking and market infrastructure means formal employment is rare and informal work is fragile.

**Inequality and spatial disparity.** KP's internal inequality is as important as its position relative to other provinces. The Hazara and Peshawar divisions concentrate industry, services and education; the southern districts and NMDs have lower literacy, worse health outcomes and thinner infrastructure. SDPI's district-level Human Development Index series shows KP's overall HDI falling from 0.650 to 0.623 (2019–2023), with only two districts improving on score while 27 declined (SDPI 2025).

**Mechanism and caution.** The mechanism connecting deprivation to militancy is *relative*, not absolute: it is the combination of rising expectations, unemployable education, and visible inequality — not merely poverty — that produces the "nothing to lose but dignity to gain" recruitment pool. The caution is equally important: **poverty does not make people violent**. Most poor residents of KP never join armed groups; many resist them. The empirical claim this study supports is narrower and defensible: socio-economic deprivation *lowers the cost* of recruitment and *raises the value* of the material benefits (salary, status, protection) that armed groups can offer, particularly for young men in the NMDs and southern belt.

## 4.3 Educational Deficiencies and the Madrasa Ecosystem

**Formal education.** KP's literacy rate stands at 51 per cent (with a wide gender gap — 37 per cent female versus 65 per cent male), below the national 60 per cent and Punjab's 66 per cent (Economic Survey 2024-25; KP Government 2024). The province has the second-highest number of out-of-school children, and learning outcomes are weak even among enrolled students. In the NMDs the disparities are stark: only 37 per cent of girls attend primary school and ~5 per cent reach secondary level (KP Youth Policy 2026). School infrastructure gaps, teacher absenteeism, insecurity-driven closures, and cultural constraints on girls' education interact.

**The madrasa ecosystem.** Pakistan's madrasa sector is estimated at roughly 30,000–35,000 institutions (with counts varying by registration method), teaching between two and four million students, with the largest concentrations in Punjab, KP and the former FATA. The sector is organized into five umbrella *wafaq* boards (Wafaq-ul-Madaris Al-Arabia — Deobandi; Tanzeem-ul-Madaris — Barelvi; Wafaq-ul-Madaris Al-Salafia — Ahl-e-Hadith; Rabita-tul-Madaris — Shia; and Ittehad Tanzeemat-ul-Madaris — a coordinating body) plus thousands of unaffiliated institutions. In KP and the tribal belt, madrasas have historically performed three functions: (i) free education and boarding for poor families (a genuine welfare function); (ii) ideological formation, including for jihadist networks; and (iii) social mobility where state education is absent or weak.

**The regulation saga.** Pakistan's attempts to regulate the sector have a specific and illuminating history: the 2019 agreement under which madrasas registered with the Ministry of Education (with roughly 18,000 registering) was reversed in October 2024, when the JUI-F secured legislation (the Societies Registration (Amendment) Act) allowing madrasas to register as charitable societies under a colonial-era law with minimal oversight of curricula, funding or foreign students (NYT 2025; Adaamir 2024). President Zardari raised international-compliance concerns (FATF); the government proposed re-registration under the education ministry; and the Ittehad Tanzeemat-e-Madaris has demanded gazette notification of the 2024 law. In early 2026, Punjab reached an agreement with the wafaq boards on *online* registration with the provincial charity commission — a model KP has not replicated at scale (The Nation 2026b). The practical result: after two decades of declared intent, a substantial share of madrasas in KP operate outside effective oversight of funding, curricula or foreign students.

**Assessment.** The evidence does not support the crude claim that madrasas are terrorist nurseries. It supports three more precise claims: (a) a subset of madrasas has historically provided recruitment, ideological socialization and logistics for armed groups (the Haqqania and Kandahar-circle lineages being the best-known examples, as well as the TNSM network in Malakand); (b) unregulated madrasas receiving foreign funding and enrolling foreign students present specific risks (Chapter 9); and (c) the *opportunity cost* of an education system that produces neither employable skills nor critical literacy is a structural driver of vulnerability to radicalization, regardless of institution type. Regulation without financing and quality reform will remain a paper exercise; conversely, a serious reform offer — curriculum modernization plus teacher training plus a transition path for graduates plus facility funding — is one of the highest-leverage interventions available (Chapters 8, 15).

## 4.4 Tribal Social Structures, Honour Codes and Conflict Resolution Traditions

**Social organization.** Pashtun social structure in the frontier belt — *qawm* (descent-and-solidarity group), *jirga* (assembly), *melmastia* (hospitality), *nanawatai* (asylum/refuge-seeking), *badal* (reciprocal justice/vendetta), *ghairat* (honour), *tor* (customary law applied through the jirga) — supplied both the governance system that the colonial state chose to work through and the conflict-resolution system that communities relied on when the state was absent (Ahmed 1983; Barth 1959; Banerjee 2000).

**The double-edged legacies.** These institutions are neither inherently violent nor inherently peaceful; they are conflict-management technologies with known pathologies. On the constructive side: jirgas have repeatedly ended violent cycles (the 2011 Kurram truce after ~2,000 deaths; the 2025 14-point agreement; Bajaur's tribal jirgas disavowing shelter to militants in 2025; the Tirah tribal committees negotiating return corridors). On the destructive side: (a) customary *riwaj* (custom) has been used to legitimize collective punishment, forced marriage and the exclusion of women from inheritance; (b) the honour/vendetta complex provides escalation structures that sectarian and militant entrepreneurs can *capture* — the Kurram conflict is the paradigmatic case; (c) jirga verdicts cannot be enforced without state backing and therefore fail against armed actors; and (d) the merger's dismantling of the malik system removed intermediaries who, whatever their faults, had brokered between state and society.

**The Malik and intermediaries: decline and consequences.** The malik system — state-supported tribal notables — was systematically destroyed by the militants between 2005 and 2014 (thousands of maliks and elders were assassinated or driven out; the 2008–2013 period saw the killing of prominent tribal leaders across the NMDs, including attacks on jirga meetings that killed dozens) and then sidelined by the merger's abolition of the Political Agent's patronage structures. The result is an *intermediary vacuum*: old intermediaries discredited or dead; elected local representatives (where they exist) without fiscal or police authority; and new "peace committee" figures who are vulnerable and often unelected. The vacuum is filled by the militants' own intermediaries (the local commanders, "spokesmen", and tax collectors) and by vigilante figures. Rebuilding legitimate intermediaries — through elected local government with real budgets, plus credible, protected elder representation in dispute resolution — is a core requirement of normalization that policy has not yet embraced (Chapter 15).

## 4.5 Religious Extremism and Ideological Radicalization

**Theological-political lineages.** KP and the tribal belt host several distinct radical traditions that are often conflated in policy discourse: (i) *Deobandi-jihadist* networks (the Haqqania/Peshawar *jihadi* milieu; TNSM; TTP's ideological core), which draw on a scholarly tradition that has also produced anti-militant clerics; (ii) *Salafi-jihadist* currents (ISKP and its local sympathizers, historically concentrated in Bajaur, Mohmand and parts of Kurram), ideologically hostile to Deobandis and Sufis; (iii) the *TNSM/Barelvi-jihadi* hybrid of Malakand, whose moral-reform program captured Swat's local grievances; (iv) *sectarian anti-Shia* networks (LeJ/SSP lineages), which overlap with both (i) and (ii); and (v) *apolitical Deobandi and Barelvi conservatism*, which supplies the ecosystem of seminaries, mosques, and moral policing in which radicalization incubates — often without active support for violence.

**What actually radicalizes?** The KP evidence suggests a *pathway model* with four interacting elements: (a) *grievance narratives* (state violence, humiliation at checkpoints, drone casualties, prison abuse, anti-Muslim foreign policy); (b) *identity and honour* (defence of community, protection of honour, revenge for kin); (c) *network recruitment* (family, madrasa classmates, village commanders, online contacts); and (d) *material incentives* (wages, status, protection, escape from unemployment). Radicalization is socially embedded: recruits generally join networks they already trust. Preventing terrorism therefore depends as much on disrupting *networks* and offering *alternatives* as on defeating ideas in the abstract.

**The state's own role.** Pakistan's religious policy has been contradictory: it has used religious discourse for state legitimation (laws on blasphemy, the constitutional declaration of Islam as the state religion, and the state's historic sponsorship of jihad in Afghanistan and Kashmir) while periodically attempting to roll back extremism. Critics, including within Pakistani scholarly and human rights communities, argue this inconsistency — employing extremists tactically, then fighting them later — produced the Frankenstein dynamic repeatedly invoked in the literature. This study treats that critique as a *hypothesis about incentives*, supported by specific historical episodes (Kashmir-focused groups' differential treatment; the 2004–2008 deals; Hafiz Gul Bahadur's earlier status as a state-aligned militia), while noting that it does not fully explain the current insurgency's scale and autonomy.

## 4.6 Historical Grievances and the Legacy of Colonial Frontier Policies

Three colonial legacies are structurally causal.

**First, legal dualism.** The FCR created two classes of Pakistani citizenship in effect for seven decades. Its structures — collective responsibility, no appeal, punitive demolition, and the Political Agent's fiat — are remembered; the 2024–2026 persistence of FCR-style practices (reported detentions under "40 FCR" joint-liability logic) shows the legacy is not merely memory (Haq 2024; GRR Journal 2024).

**Second, the Durand Line's non-recognition.** Afghanistan's refusal to accept the boundary (and successive Afghan governments' support, at least rhetorically, for Pashtun self-determination) supplied both a diplomatic irritant and a sanctuary framework. Pakistan's counter-moves — strategic depth, support to Afghan Islamists, and cross-border intervention — embedded KP in the conflict dynamics of a neighbouring state (Chapter 9).

**Third, the "good tribal" bargains.** Purchased loyalties (allowances, local militias, and tolerance of smuggling) created a frontier political economy whose collapse — when allowances were suspended or when the state tried to disarm militias after 2001 — destabilized precisely the actors the state had relied on. The trajectory of Hafiz Gul Bahadur (from a state-tolerated North Waziristan commander to a principal insurgent) illustrates the pattern of client-to-antagonist conversion.

## 4.7 Geopolitical Exploitation: Proxy Wars and External Interference

**The layers of geopolitics.** (i) *The Afghan layer*: the sanctuary granted to the TTP by factions within the Afghan Taliban (and the Taliban's denial of control); the ISKP sanctuary in Afghanistan; and the period 2021–2026 in which Pakistan's leverage over Kabul collapsed (Chapters 2, 9). (ii) *The India layer*: Pakistan's longstanding allegation that India's intelligence services support Baloch and (occasionally) TTP-linked militancy; India's denial; the issue recurs in Pakistani crisis discourse (and was invoked during the 2025 escalation). Independent verification is rare, and this study treats the claim as an allegation contested by the party accused rather than an established fact. (iii) *The Iran layer*: Iranian interests in Shia protection in Kurram and its regional competition with Pakistan over Afghanistan; Zainebiyoun-linked networks in the border belt. (iv) *The China layer*: CPEC's security risk premium; Chinese pressure on Pakistan (and, conversely, Chinese mediation between Islamabad and Kabul in 2026); Chinese interest in mineral development in the NMDs. (v) *The US/global layer*: residual counter-terrorism interests, the post-withdrawal loss of intelligence and ISR support that Pakistan had enjoyed during the Zarb-e-Azb era, the Afghanistan-specific visa and resettlement pipeline, and FATF-linked regulatory pressure.

**Mechanism: opportunity, not instruction.** The analytically defensible position is that geopolitics chiefly supplies *opportunity structures* — sanctuaries, weapons, funding, diplomatic cover — rather than directly *commanding* KP's conflict. Pakistan's own history of using non-state actors in Afghanistan and Kashmir establishes that the instrument has been used by multiple states in the region; the current insurgency is supplied, however, by a genuinely autonomous domestic-ideological core and by the Afghan sanctuary, not solely by foreign instruction.

## 4.8 Weak State Writ and Institutional Fragility

**The composite problem.** A state's ability to provide security depends on the interaction of force, intelligence, justice, revenue and legitimacy. In KP each element is deficient in a distinct way: force is concentrated in the military with police overloaded; intelligence is fragmented (military intelligence; ISI; IB; CTD; district police; Special Branch) and the KP CTD suffers resource and personnel gaps (19 per cent vacancy in sanctioned posts in 2026; equipment shortfalls acknowledged even in official data); justice is slow (ATCs are clogged; 2,273 anti-terror cases pending as of November 2024; 657 death sentences awarded between 2019 and 2022 with no executions during that period, per the NAP review literature); revenue is hostage to federal transfers; and legitimacy is contested by both the PTM-aligned rights movement and religious-political parties (Chapters 7, 11).

**Policing the periphery: the NMD's unresolved model.** The absorption of the Khasadar and Levies forces into the KP Police (2019 legislation; a force of roughly 28,000 personnel split between ~15,600 Levies and ~12,000–15,000 Khasadars, with figures reported variously) produced an institution with three incompatible inheritances: the Levies' light border-policing doctrine; the Khasadar's communal, unpaid, and locally embedded model; and the KP Police's urban-focused, standardized force structure. The result is documented: inadequate training and equipment; personnel "posted" to the NMDs from the settled districts who are unaccountable to local communities; and local officers without the authority or protection to act. This is the single most specific institutional gap in KP's security architecture (Chapter 7).

**The civil–military disconnect.** ICG's 2026 report (94 interviews, Sept 2025–Feb 2026) documents that the military conducts counter-insurgency with limited coordination with provincial police; that army commanders shape internal-security policy while the police bear the losses; and that provincial authorities have criticized both the conduct of operations and the withheld resources (ICG 2026; Dawn 2024a). This disconnect is not an accident of personalities; it is structural — the provincial police depend on the province for budget and on the centre for strategy, with no joint command-and-accountability mechanism.

## 4.9 Ungoverned Spaces and Geographic Terrain

**The terrain issue.** KP's mountainous border zones — Tirah's high pastures, the Shawal and Razmak ranges, the Bajaur and Mohmand hills, and the Kurram's passes — are not "uninhabited"; they are *thinly governed and seasonally transited*. Militants use escarpment sanctuaries, caves, seasonal pasture routes and cross-border valleys; state forces face mobility, visibility and communications constraints; and the climate (snow, fog) determines the tempo of violence (winter lulls; spring escalations — hence the TTP's "spring offensive" framing).

**Ungoverned space is relational.** It is more useful to think in terms of a *governance gradient* than a binary. In KP, space grades from fully governed (Peshawar's urban core) to partially governed (southern settled districts, where state presence is contested at night) to thinly governed (Tirah's upper valleys, south Waziristan's border strips) to non-governed (sanctuary pockets in Afghanistan). Policy must therefore specify the mechanism by which each gradient segment is to be moved — and must not assume that "clearing" a district translates into a governance outcome (Chapter 6).

## 4.10 The Drug Economy and War-Economy Dynamics

**Structure of the war economy.** KP's conflict economy has six revenue streams: (i) smuggling and trade diversion (fuel, consumer goods, vehicles, livestock, currency); (ii) extortion and "taxation" of commerce and transport; (iii) narcotics transit and local retail (with links to the broader Afghan opiate and synthetic-drug economy); (iv) mining and natural resource rents (marble, chromite, coal, antimony, and sand/gravel), including illegal mining and the coercion of contractors; (v) kidnapping for ransom and, occasionally, hostage-taking; and (vi) "project rents" — the capture of public works contracts, employment and NGO/government projects in conflict districts. A seventh, less-discussed stream is the revenue from *security contracting itself*: private armed escorts for transport, mining, and development work, and informal taxation by militias and police checkpoints alike.

**Greed, grievance and the war economy's true role.** The Collier–Hoeffler framework helps explain why the conflict has lasted, not why it began. KP's insurgency did not arise from lootable resources, and its fighters were not principally profit-seekers. But once armed actors are established, the war economy: (a) funds operations more reliably than donations; (b) generates private constituencies for conflict (smugglers, contractors, checkpoint officials, militia commanders) whose interests exist independently of ideology; (c) corrupts the state institutions and private actors with which it interacts (documented in multiple cases of police and customs complicity in smuggling routes); and (d) makes *peace* costly for specific actors — a fact any settlement must confront by providing transition pathways (Chapter 15).

**The state's smuggling crackdowns.** The 2025–26 anti-smuggling drives (and the creation of provincial and federal task forces; the curbs on "non-customs paid" vehicles and fuel) coincided with — and were partly justified by — the TTP's reliance on smuggling rents. But they also deepened the economic crisis of the border communities, including the tens of thousands who depend on the trade. Normalization policy must be explicit about this trade-off: convert the border economy rather than merely repress it (border markets, legal crossings, tariff-based trade, and employment substitution).

## 4.11 Identity Politics, Pashtun Nationalism and the PTM Movement

**The Pashtun question.** Pashtun political identity has been mobilized in three distinct registers since 1947: the *nationalist* register (Pakhtunistan, the ANP and allied parties); the *religious-political* register (JUI-F and Deobandi political networks); and, since 2014, the *rights/human-rights* register of the Pashtun Tahafuz Movement (PTM), which began as the Mahsud Tahafuz Movement (May 2014) demanding de-mining and compensation after Waziristan's operations, and grew into a national movement after its 2018 renaming. The PTM's demands are specific and documented: de-mining; freedom of movement and removal of checkpoints; accountability for extrajudicial killings and enforced disappearances; a truth and reconciliation process; and the release of detainees (PTM 2018; ICG 2022; Aaj 2024).

**The ban and its consequences.** On 6 October 2024 — days before a scheduled "Pashtun National Jirga" — the federal government proscribed the PTM under Section 11B of the Anti-Terrorism Act, citing "activities prejudicial to peace and security," "anti-national narratives," and "anarchy" (The News 2024; The Hindu 2024; Business Recorder 2024). The KP government then froze its activities; Section 144 was imposed to block a Khyber district gathering; and clashes between police and PTM supporters followed (October 2024). Since then, human rights organizations have documented continuing restrictions on Pashtun civil society, including detentions of PTM-affiliated activists without charge (HRW/Amnesty/HRCP reporting, cited in Countercurrents 2026). Simultaneously, the movement's leadership has faced repeated prosecutions (Ali Wazir, Manzoor Pashteen) and bans on entry (Balochistan's 90-day prohibition in August 2024).

**Analytical assessment.** The PTM ban is a case study in the counterproductive dynamics of securitization. The movement has, in fact, been non-violent (its signature tactic is the sit-in and the "peace march" preceded by a peace jirga), and its demands — de-mining, accountability for disappearances, safe mobility — overlap with the practical requirements of any normalization strategy. By criminalizing it under the anti-terrorism law, the state: (i) removed a channel for non-violent grievance articulation precisely as the insurgency escalated; (ii) conflated peaceful critics with armed actors, weakening the state's claim to distinguish between them; (iii) alienated parts of the anti-militant constituency whose cooperation the counter-insurgency needs; and (iv) shifted the movement's surviving activism to informal, less accountable forms. The counter-argument — that the movement's rhetoric is inflammatory, that some figures have made irresponsible statements, and that its mobilization intersects with ethnocentric narratives that the TTP also seeks to exploit — has some basis but does not justify terrorism-law proscription of a non-violent movement.

## 4.12 Intersectionality of Root Causes: A Systems-Thinking Perspective

**The four-layer model.** Layering the causes produces the study's diagnostic framework:

**(1) Structural conditions** (make the system vulnerable; change slowly): legal exceptionalism and its legacy; geographic proximity to a collapsing borderland; the absence of industrial employment and formal education quality; sectarian-Muslim communal geography; low state legitimacy; and Pakistan's own use of non-state actors as strategic instruments.
**(2) Proximate triggers** (set episodes in motion): operations and their displacement effects; drone strikes; assassinations of elders; land disputes (Kurram); electoral and representational shocks (FATA's representation and its subsequent disappointment); negotiated truces that fail; and the 2021 Afghan regime change.
**(3) Accelerants** (increase the scale and speed of violence): the Afghanistan sanctuary; weapons surplus; drone and IED technology diffusion; the Islamic State's provocation and sectarian competition; factional splits (JuA 2026) and umbrella competition; online radicalization; and fiscal shocks (poverty rising 28.7→35.3 per cent in KP).
**(4) Sustaining mechanisms** (keep violence going even when causes persist elsewhere): war economy; weak justice; police–military bifurcation; displaced-person grievance; the intermediary vacuum; and the credibility deficit of state promises (merger funding, compensation, return guarantees).

**Intersectionality in practice: three mini-cases.** In *Kurram*, a structurally old sectarian-land conflict was triggered by a convoy attack, accelerated by armed-group competition and cross-border sanctuary, and sustained by a weak justice system and closed roads. In *Swat*, structural factors were comparatively benign (high literacy, tourism economy), which is why the 2007–2009 insurgency required the TNSM's ideology plus state collapse of presence; the 2025–26 resurgence is driven primarily by the *accelerants* (sanctuary, factional expansion, technology) rather than by revived local structural causes (NSBRRI 2026). In *North Waziristan*, all four layers are active simultaneously, which explains why it has been the single most violent district almost continuously since 2004 — and why a military operation alone (Zarb-e-Azb) could not end violence there without the other layers being addressed.

**Policy implication.** Interventions targeted at a single layer fail predictably. Kinetic operations address triggers but not sustaining mechanisms; development projects address structural conditions but not triggers; deradicalization addresses accelerants in part; and only the simultaneous treatment of all four layers — sequenced by what each can realistically achieve in what time — produces movement toward the normalization thresholds defined in Chapter 1. This is the analytical foundation of the roadmap in Chapter 15.
