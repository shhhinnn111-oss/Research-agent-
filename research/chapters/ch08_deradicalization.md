# CHAPTER 8 — DERADICALIZATION, REHABILITATION AND COUNTER-NARRATIVE STRATEGIES

## 8.1 Understanding Radicalization Pathways in the KP Context

**From models to evidence.** The global literature on radicalization offers stage models (pre-radicalization, identification, indoctrination, action), social-movement accounts (networks, identity, framing) and structural accounts (relative deprivation, state violence). KP's evidence supports a *relational* model with five empirically observable components:

1. **Exposure and grievance.** The overwhelming majority of KP recruits encountered militancy through family, village, madrasa or workplace networks (not through ideology first). Grievances most frequently cited in interviews and testimonials: deaths or humiliation of relatives in operations; detention and torture; drone strikes; the inability to obtain justice; and material deprivation.
2. **Identity and honour framing.** Recruitment narratives translate private grievances into collective ones (Pashtun honour; defence of Islam; revenge for kin — *badal*).
3. **Network linkage.** Combatants described joining because a relative, teacher, or friend was already involved — the "friend-of-a-friend" mechanism. This is why network disruption and rehabilitation are intertwined; and why surveillance-led prevention fails to address the relational core.
4. **Material incentives.** Income, status, and protection matter, especially for young men in the NMDs and southern belt.
5. **Ideological justification.** Ideology typically consolidates rather than initiates commitment, providing language, rules of conduct, and afterlife promise.

**Diversity of pathways.** Distinct profiles are documented: child soldiers recruited or handed over by families (the Sabawoon cohort); adolescent volunteers seeking status or acting on peer pressure; adult fighters motivated by revenge or ideology; sectarian recruits drawn through LeJ/SSP or ISKP networks; and, increasingly, self-radicalized online actors (Chapter 3). Programs that do not tailor interventions to profiles will fail at scale.

**Gender dimension.** Women's roles in KP's conflict are simultaneously under-recognized and analytically important: as victims (targeted violence, widowhood, displacement); as prevention actors (mothers' networks, female teachers and health workers, and women's peace organizations); and, in rare documented cases, as facilitators or recruiters. Ignoring women's roles in either direction produces bad policy — either a protection gap or a security blind spot (Chapter 12).

## 8.2 Pakistan's Deradicalization Framework: Policy and Institutional Architecture

**Policy lineage.** Deradicalization entered Pakistan's formal security discourse through the National Action Plan's provisions against extremism and terrorism (including the rehabilitation of willing militants) and the 2021 NAP revision; the National Internal Security Policy and the National Counter-Terrorism Narrative (developed under NACTA) provide the conceptual frame; and the provinces — mandated by NAP to build counter-extremism capacity — established rehabilitation programs. Notably, Pakistan has never adopted a *single national deradicalization policy* with a legal mandate, budget line, standards, and evaluation framework; the practice is a mosaic of military-run, provincial, and NGO programs.

**Institutional map.**
- **Military-run programs** in Malakand/Swat: **Sabawoon** (juveniles), **Mishal** (adults), **Sparlay** (families of detainees), plus parallel vocational facilities in Swat, Mingora, D.I. Khan and Bajaur; documented predecessors/follow-ons including *Rastoon* and *Pythom*; funding historically from the army with some international support.
- **Provincial programs**: KP's CTD and Home Department manage rehabilitation for detainees and de-radicalization modules in prison settings; the KP government has run (and intermittently funded) vocational training for surrendered militants; the KP cabinet approved reform measures from time to time (including, in 2026, police and digital reforms).
- **Punjab programs**: the Punjab CTD's deradicalization centre (Naway Sehar-type facility) and a pilot program that reported rehabilitating 300 members of proscribed organizations from 15 districts (with a Rs 9.33 million allocation).
- **NACTA**: mandated to coordinate counter-narrative and deradicalization work; has produced the National Narrative document and coordinates counter-financing; capacity has been constrained (its own 2018 report found roughly three-quarters of approved posts unfilled), and it received a new military-appointed head in 2026 (Major-General Faisal Naseer) with a mandate to strengthen coordination (CRSS 2019; TFT 2026).
- **Religious/community instruments**: the **Paigham-e-Pakistan** declaration (January 2018), a fatwa/declaration by a broad assembly of scholars (with 1,800+ signatories claimed) rejecting terrorism and suicide bombing; subsequent anti-militancy statements by wafaq boards; and mosque-level counter-narratives.
- **NGO/CSO programs**: PAIMAN Alumni Trust's mothers' counter-radicalization work; PEAD's peace education; Aware Girls and other women-led organizations; Khwendo Kor and others in KP; with international support (UN Women, EU, USIP, and others) — all chronically under-funded and now constrained by the contracting civic space.

## 8.3 The Sabawoon Centre: Model, Methodology and Outcomes for Juvenile Militants

**Origins and design.** Sabawoon ("dawn") was established in Swat in 2009 by the army's humanitarian wing (with a civilian police/psychologist component), in the aftermath of Rah-e-Rast, for *juvenile* detainees who had not committed grave offences and were deemed low-to-medium risk. It became the flagship of Pakistan's rehabilitation effort and is one of the most-studied programs of its kind in South Asia.

**Methodology.** Four components: (i) *basic education* (literacy, numeracy, and "corrected" religious education including instruction on Islamic pluralism and the illegitimacy of suicide attacks); (ii) *vocational training* (auto mechanics, carpentry, electronics, masonry, poultry/horticulture, computer skills); (iii) *psychological counselling and psychotherapy* (individual and group; addressing trauma and identity); and (iv) *social and family counselling* (family therapy; reintegration planning; monitoring after release). The curriculum explicitly aimed at "de-indoctrination" through engagement with the students' own religious vocabulary and exposure to national/cultural identity markers.

**Outcomes.** Reported figures: induction of **~226 juveniles across seven batches (2009–2015)**; **~143 reintegrated** after completing programs; officials claim a **99 per cent success rate** with low recidivism; independent academic assessments (NUST's *Journal of Peace and Security*, 2021) found that most reintegrated graduates pursued small businesses, jobs, or further education, while acknowledging **(a)** **higher-than-claimed risk of recidivism — with 10–15 re-institutionalized on the advice of monitoring teams**, and **(b)** **no independent evaluation with access to program data**. A separate journalist-academic field study (2023) could not verify the *Sparlay* centre's existence at all, and documented **difficulties faced by graduates in starting businesses or finding jobs**.

**Critical assessment.** Sabawoon's design is genuinely strong by international standards — particularly its combination of education, vocational skills, psychosocial care, family work, and post-release monitoring, mirroring the better-regarded international programs (Chapter 8.11). Its weaknesses are equally instructive: (i) *scale* — 226 juveniles over six years is minuscule relative to thousands of detainees and tens of thousands of at-risk youth; (ii) *transparency* — no independent evaluation, no published recidivism study with methodology; (iii) *aftercare deficit* — graduates report no material support, ongoing surveillance, travel restrictions, and community stigmatization; (iv) *the cohort question* — the program's beneficiaries were largely juveniles who surrendered or were lightly implicated, whereas current cohorts include harder cases; and (v) *outcome attribution* — the claimed success may reflect selection (low-risk intake) as much as intervention efficacy.

## 8.4 The Mishal and Sparlay Centres: Adult Deradicalization Programs

**Mishal** was designed for *adult* low-risk detainees deemed capable of reintegration, combining religious re-education, vocational training, and psychological support in a residential setting; **Sparlay** was designed for the *families* of detainees (mitigating the secondary radicalization risks and economic pressures that affect combatant households). Documented claims: **Mishal reintegrated approximately 400 individuals**; broader claims place the total number of ex-combatants who passed through the Swat rehabilitation system at **~2,200–2,500**; the KP Provincial Government earmarked funds (Rs 4.4 million in one budget cycle; the de-radicalization pilot received Rs 9.33 million per one account).

**Evidence of effectiveness.** The published record is thin: program claims are reported by officials; independent studies report graduates' complaints in a consistent pattern — no financial or material start-up support; inability to find jobs; surveillance (graduates described being followed and questioned for travel); a requirement to report to security forces; and community rejection, especially where the community knew the individual's history. The 2023 field study (Journal of Dialogue, Public Knowledge Project) found that *all twelve* graduates interviewed faced difficulties starting enterprises, and reported stigma and monitoring. This is the strongest available independent evidence — and it points to a **reintegration failure** rather than a *re-radicalization* failure: programs may change beliefs, but without economic and social re-entry, the graduates remain a vulnerable population.

**Sparlay's fate.** The field study's inability to verify Sparlay's existence (despite visiting two centres) suggests either consolidation under other names or quiet discontinuation — a microcosm of the absence of transparency in the sector.

## 8.5 Madrasa Reform and Regulation: Progress, Resistance and Gaps

**The policy record.** Pakistan's madrasa regulation efforts have a 25-year lineage: registration drives (2002, 2005), the 2019 framework agreement under which madrasas registered with the Ministry of Education (roughly **18,000 registered**; with commitments to bank accounts, foreign-student regulation, and curriculum goals), and then **reversal**: in October 2024, after JUI-F pressure, the **Societies Registration (Amendment) Act** allowed madrasas to register under the colonial-era Societies Registration Act as charitable bodies — a framework with minimal oversight of curricula, funding or foreign students. President Zardari objected publicly on FATF-compliance grounds; the government proposed re-registration under the education ministry; the Ittehad Tanzeemat-e-Madaris demanded gazette notification of the 2024 law. Punjab's January 2026 agreement (online registration through the Punjab Charity Commission, negotiated with the wafaq boards) represents a practical alternative model — transparent, consultative, digitized — but it has not been replicated in KP at scale (NYT 2025; Adaamir 2024; The Nation 2026b).

**The KP-specific picture.** KP hosts one of the densest madrasa networks in Pakistan, including the most politically significant institution (Darul Uloom Haqqania, Akora Khattak, associated with numerous Afghan Taliban leaders, and whose head, Maulana Hamid-ul-Haq Haqqani, was killed in the February 2025 ISKP bombing). Two gaps are salient: (i) *unregistered institutions* — a substantial (though unquantified) share of KP madrasas, especially small village institutions, remain outside any registry; and (ii) *financing* — foreign funding of madrasas was a central FATF-era concern; documentation remains incomplete, and the October 2024 legal change reduced the financial-transparency requirements that the 2019 framework had introduced.

**What reform would require.** The international and Pakistani evidence converges on five requirements: consultative (not unilateral) design; digitized, low-friction registration; curriculum modernization with religious-authority buy-in; financial transparency (bank accounts, audited sources, foreign student reporting); and *positive* incentives — faculty salaries, facilities, libraries, science labs, and student scholarships — without which regulation is experienced as an assault on the poor's only educational option.

## 8.6 Counter-Narrative Development: Religious, Cultural and Media-Based Approaches

**Instruments.** (i) *Religious counter-narratives*: Paigham-e-Pakistan's anti-terror fatwa and its dissemination; wafaq board statements; the Sabawoon curriculum's use of religious reasoning against suicide attacks and for pluralism. (ii) *National Narrative*: NACTA's framework, developed with academic and religious input, positioning the state's struggle against extremism in national and Islamic terms. (iii) *Media and digital*: state counter-terrorism messaging, social-media campaigns, blocking of extremist content (over 1.3 million URLs blocked by 2023); religious programming on state media. (iv) *Cultural counter-narratives*: Pashtun cultural production (poetry, music, TV) reclaiming religious and cultural identity from militant monopolization; memorialization of victims, including the APS tragedy's annual observances.

**Effectiveness.** Three observations. First, *the state's credibility problem caps counter-narratives*: messaging from institutions perceived as coercive or self-serving is discounted precisely by the audiences at risk. Second, *Pashto-language, locally credible messaging is scarce* — most content is Urdu, urban, and state-framed. Third, *the most effective counter-narrative vehicle is often not messaging at all* but demonstrable state protection and justice — Swat's post-2009 recovery (schools reopened, tourists returned) did more to discredit the TNSM's narrative than any campaign. The policy implication is that counter-narrative work should be *embedded in credible local institutions* (mosques, madrasas, schools, jirgas, radio) rather than centralized in state communications.

## 8.7 Role of Religious Scholars and Ulema in Countering Extremist Ideology

**The anti-militant ulama.** A significant body of Pakistani religious opinion has opposed armed militancy: Paigham-e-Pakistan; the Fatwa of the Wafaq-ul-Madaris; statements by Barelvi and Deobandi scholars; and victims' advocacy (the families of APS; the relatives of clerics killed by militants). In KP, the murders of anti-Taliban clerics and tribal elders were systematic in 2005–2014, and the 2025 killing of the Haqqania head — whatever his complex legacy — illustrated that even establishment-aligned clerics are targets.

**The limits.** (i) *Ambiguity*: some scholars have historically justified jihad in Kashmir/Afghanistan while condemning attacks inside Pakistan — an inconsistent position that militants exploit and that undermines the clarity of the counter-narrative. (ii) *Political instrumentalization*: religious parties (JUI-F most prominently) mediate state-religious relations while maintaining independent positions on operations, madrasa reform and sectarian groups. (iii) *Structural incentives*: madrasa economics — fee-free education and community prestige — create institution-level resistance to curriculum modernization.

**Practical recommendation.** The state's most credible route to religious counter-narratives runs through *standing, funded partnerships with anti-militant scholars and institutions* — not ad hoc endorsements — including protection for clerics, media platforms for anti-militancy sermons, and inclusion of scholars in deradicalization delivery (as Saudi Arabia and Singapore have done with their credentialed religious authorities).

## 8.8 Reintegration into Society: Employment, Education and Psychosocial Support

**The evidence.** As noted, the strongest independent evidence (interviews with twelve graduates, 2023) documents that rehabilitees face: no start-up capital; no job placement; employer reluctance; surveillance and travel restrictions; community stigma; and the psychological burden of untreated trauma. The NUST study found comparatively better outcomes (education continuation, self-employment) for the Sabawoon cohort, with monitoring.

**Why reintegration is the decisive phase.** Deradicalization literature is consistent: programs succeed or fail at *aftercare*. Individuals who return to unemployment, humiliation and surveillance have strong incentives to rejoin armed networks — not necessarily from renewed belief, but from material and social exclusion. Conversely, employment plus community acceptance (with monitoring that is respectful rather than punitive) is strongly associated with durable disengagement.

**Design elements supported by evidence.** (i) *Individual reintegration plans* (education, vocational certification, job placement, small-business grants); (ii) *family support* (stipends conditioned on school attendance; the Sparlay concept); (iii) *psychosocial services* (trauma treatment, especially for juveniles; the Sabawoon model); (iv) *protected, time-limited monitoring* with clear exit criteria rather than indefinite surveillance; (v) *community reconciliation* (jirga-mediated acceptance ceremonies; religious endorsement of re-entry); (vi) *employer partnerships* (subsidized apprenticeships; certification); and (vii) *documented release/clearance* so graduates can travel, work and access services without permanent suspicion.

## 8.9 Women and Youth-Focused Deradicalization Programs

**Women.** Two distinct program types exist globally and partly in Pakistan: (i) *mothers' programs* (PAIMAN's work with mothers of at-risk and radicalized youth in KP and FATA — among the most cited Pakistani PVE innovations); and (ii) *women's peace networks* (UN Women's Pakistan programme reported engaging **228 Afghan and Pakistani women peace activists** in dialogue processes contributing to cross-border peace discussions, alongside NACTA partnerships on women's leadership in peacebuilding). Women's PVE work benefits from unique access (to households and youth), but is constrained by: security risks, cultural gate-keeping, and funding that is short-term and project-based.

**Youth.** Youth-focused interventions include: the KP government's skill and employment programs (Youth Impact Challenge grants expanded to Rs 2 billion; the KP Youth Employment Program for IT skills; digital internships); the KP Youth Policy 2026's revision process (which itself documents that female education and employment lag, with only 37 per cent of women literate versus 65 per cent of men and women making up over half of unemployed youth); and the *Peace Education* work of CSOs. The critical gap is *prevention at scale*: school-based critical-thinking curricula, school safety, girls' education protection (from attacks and closures), and mental-health services are all underfunded relative to the risk.

**Assessment.** Both categories are extremely high-leverage and chronically marginal in Pakistani counter-radicalization spending. The KP Youth Policy's own drafting acknowledges that "youth programmes cannot catch up with demand."

## 8.10 Community-Based Approaches to Preventing Violent Extremism (PVE)

**Practice in KP.** A network of Pakistani CSOs operates PVE programs in KP: PAIMAN (mothers' networks, youth dialogues, peace education); PEAD (peace education and community dialogues); Aware Girls (women's empowerment and youth leadership in KP — with its founder subject to travel restrictions and legal harassment in earlier years); Khwendo Kor (women's and girls' development); the Khpal Kor and similar institutions; and district-level peace committees and jirgas (Chapter 12). Government-adjacent efforts include the "Terrorism-Free" district initiatives and, in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa context, peace jirgas and local *aman* committees supported by the administration and army.

**What works.** The international evidence base on community-based PVE (CVE) points to: locally-led (not externally imposed) programming; credible local voices (religious, tribal, teachers); multi-sector services (education, livelihoods, mental health) rather than messaging alone; addressing state-society grievances as part of prevention; and rigorous evaluation. Pakistan's KP practice aligns with the first three and lacks the fourth and fifth: there are almost no published *outcome evaluations* of Pakistani PVE programs, and civic-space contraction (the PTM ban; restrictions on activists; NOC regimes for NGOs) limits the very actors best positioned to deliver prevention.

**Assessment.** Community-based PVE in KP is at an inflection point: demand has never been greater (the 2021–26 recruitment environment), the practice is small, and the political space for independent civil society has narrowed. Without a deliberate re-opening of space and sustained financing, the state will be left relying on the least effective prevention instrument — coercion — while the recruitment pipeline continues to refill.

## 8.11 International Best Practices in Deradicalization

| Programme / country | Key design features | Transferable elements for KP |
|---|---|---|
| **Saudi Arabia** — Counseling Programme / Etidal & the Muhammad bin Nayef Center (PRAC) | Credentialed religious authority; family involvement; aftercare with employment and marriage support; deradicalization vs. disengagement distinction | Religious-authority partnership; aftercare contracts (employment, family); clear release status |
| **Singapore** — Religious Rehabilitation Group (RRG) | Volunteer scholars, individually tailored religious counselling; community (mosque) ownership; separate aftercare group for families | Islamic-credentialed counselling; mosque-based community ownership; family support group |
| **Denmark** — Aarhus model | "SSP" multi-agency approach: police, schools, social services, health; mentoring; exit programs; no criminalization of returnees where legally possible | Multi-agency local teams; mentoring; school and municipal involvement |
| **Nigeria** — Operation Safe Corridor (DDR for repentant Boko Haram) | Vocational training, psycho-spiritual counselling, community reintegration, follow-up | Reintegration plus Community Reintegration Committees; documented clearance certificates |
| **Colombia** — DDR with FARC (2016) | Individual reintegration plans; income support; transitional justice; truth-telling as social reintegration | Transitional justice link; individual plans; public truth mechanism |
| **Indonesia** — deradicalization programs | Government plus religious organizations; individual assessment; family and community | Family-centered programming; religious-organization delivery |

**Synthesis of design principles.** (1) Credible religious authority; (2) individual assessment and tailored plans; (3) family centrality; (4) multi-agency local delivery (police + social services + schools + health); (5) aftercare with real economic content; (6) community acceptance strategies and truth/reconciliation mechanisms where feasible; (7) clear legal status on release; (8) monitoring with exit criteria; and (9) independent evaluation with published results.

## 8.12 Critical Assessment: Are Pakistan's Deradicalization Programs Working?

**What can be established.** (a) Pakistan has *models* — the Sabawoon design is sound and internationally recognized; (b) the programs are *small* relative to need (hundreds, not thousands, of beneficiaries, against a recruitment pool of tens of thousands and a caseload of thousands of detainees); (c) the *evidence base is weak* — claims of 99 per cent success rates lack methodological support, and independent research documents reintegration failures and unverifiable claims; (d) *aftercare* is the principal weakness; (e) *geographic coverage* is concentrated in Swat/Malakand, missing the current epicentre (the southern belt and the NMDs), where programs have been initiated only intermittently; (f) *financing* has never been institutionalized in a budget line with accountability; and (g) the legal framework for detention and release of alleged militants creates perverse incentives (indefinite detention vs. premature release without genuine program completion), which deradicalization programs cannot fix from within.

**The verdict.** Pakistan's deradicalization infrastructure is *promising in design, marginal in scale, unverified in outcome, and misaligned with the current threat geography*. It is not, however, a failure of concept; it is a failure of political commitment and institutional investment. In a counter-insurgency that is demonstrably attrition-limited (Chapter 6), scaling deradicalization, rehabilitation and reentry is not a soft option; it is one of the few instruments that can reduce the enemy's replenishment rate.

**Priority recommendations (developed in Chapter 15).**
1. Enact a **provincial deradicalization and reintegration statute** establishing a KP Rehabilitation and Reintegration Authority with a legal mandate, defined admission criteria, judicial oversight of detention, published standards, independent evaluation, and a ring-fenced budget.
2. **Relocate and expand capacity to the current threat geography**: centres in Bannu/D.I. Khan and the NMDs (Bajaur, Khyber, North Waziristan), with mobile teams for the southern districts.
3. **Mandate independent evaluation**: a university-led, five-year longitudinal study with published recidivism, employment and psychosocial outcomes (with data-protection safeguards).
4. **Fix aftercare**: grants, apprenticeships, employer partnerships, psychological services, and clear release certification — the single most important determinant of durability.
5. **Employ the families**: expand a Sparlay-type program, with stipends tied to children's schooling and mothers' participation.
6. **Build the counter-narrative base locally**: fund KP-based Pashto-language media, mosque networks, and scholar partnerships; end the conflation of religious conservatism with extremism.
7. **Reform the legal pathway**: create a statutory juvenile-justice and rehabilitation track; end the practice of indefinite preventive detention of juveniles; and link deradicalization completion to judicial release mechanisms.
8. **Reopen civic space for PVE**: a transparent, rights-based NGO registration and NOC regime, and the withdrawal of the PTM proscription in favor of lawful political engagement (Chapter 12).
