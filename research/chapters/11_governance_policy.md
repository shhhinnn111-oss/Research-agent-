# CHAPTER 11 — GOVERNANCE, POLICY FRAMEWORKS AND LEGAL ARCHITECTURE

## 11.1 The National Action Plan (NAP): Objectives, Implementation and Assessment

**Origins and content.** The NAP was adopted in December 2014 in the aftermath of the Army Public School (APS) attack (141+ killed, 132 of them children) — Pakistan's most consequential terrorism event, whose political effect was comparable to 9/11 for the United States or the 2005 London bombings for the UK. The original twenty points combined kinetic measures (military operations, counter-terrorism forces), legal measures (military courts through the 21st Amendment; proscription of armed groups; prosecution), regulatory measures (madrasa reform; choking terrorist financing; curbing hate speech and the use of media/internet), and political-administrative measures (reconciliation in Balochistan; FATA reforms; Karachi operation; provincial coordination through NACTA). The 2021 revision reduced the framework to fourteen points and added emphases on the cyber domain, criminal-justice reform, and merged-district reform.

**Implementation record (assessed).** *Strong:* kinetic operations (Zarb-e-Azb and successors); counter-financing (FATF-era compliance: asset freezes, Schedule IV designations, thousands of accounts blocked; Pakistan's 2022 exit from the FATF grey list); proscriptions (65 organizations listed, 4 under observation, and thousands of individuals under Schedule IV at latest public counts); cyber takedowns (over 1.3 million URLs blocked by 2023); police/CTD capacity expansion (with provincial budgets rising); and military courts' throughput (thousands convicted, though with fair-trial concerns). *Weak/partial:* madrasa reform (reversed in 2024); terrorism trials (2,273 pending as of November 2024; 657 death sentences awarded 2019–2022 with no executions during that period); merged-areas reform (fiscal failure, Chapter 5); reconciliation in Balochistan (rated unsatisfactory in successive reviews); and the soft/preventive agenda generally (deradicalization, counter-narrative, reintegration). *Negative:* restrictions on media and civil society adopted under NAP rubric (journalist harassment; NGO NOC regimes; the PTM ban).

**Assessment.** NAP's architecture was sound and its kinetic implementation real; its central deficiency was *asymmetry*: the measures that could be delivered by force and administrative order were implemented; those requiring political bargaining, fiscal commitment and institutional development were not. The ISPR's own public position — that the NAP "has not been fully implemented" — aligned with this study's reading (Pakistan Connect 2025; CSUN/CUNI dissertation evidence; TFT 2026).

## 11.2 National Internal Security Policy (NISP): Evolution and Effectiveness

**Evolution.** The NISP (first issued 2014, followed by the National Security Policy 2022–2026) sought to frame internal security as an integrated enterprise: counter-terrorism; counter-extremism; border management; cyber security; and the "whole-of-nation" approach conjoining economic, human and military security. The 2022 National Security Policy was notable for its explicit "comprehensive" framing — including a commitment to gender security and economic security as core national-security elements.

**Effectiveness.** The policies articulated the right ambitions but suffered from three defects: (i) *no binding implementation mechanism* — they lacked legal mandates, budgets, and accountability for line ministries/provinces; (ii) *no public monitoring* — performance reported episodically and selectively; and (iii) *no political consensus mechanism* — the policies were federal-executive documents, and they were overtaken from 2022 by the PTI–federal conflict, which made a national consensus on internal security impossible in the period when it was most needed.

**Assessment.** NISP's value is as an *inventory of intentions* against which KP's current arrangements can be measured (and found wanting): border management without a border regime; cyber security without data protection; counter-extremism without scale; and gender security without implementation.

## 11.3 The Provincial Security Architecture: Apex Committees, Security Briefings and Coordination Mechanisms

**Institutions.** The provincial security architecture comprises: (i) the **Provincial Apex Committee** (chaired by the Chief Minister, with the Corps Commander, Chief Secretary, IGP, and other stakeholders), the principal civil-military coordination forum, replicated at district level in some cases; (ii) the **Home and Tribal Affairs Department**, the administrative anchor; (iii) the **KP Police** and CTD (Chapter 7); (iv) the **provincial cabinet's security-relevant committees**; (v) ad hoc **jirga/peace-committee structures**; and (vi) **federal mechanisms** operating in the province (the National Security Committee; NACTA; the military's operational HQs).

**Performance.** The Apex Committee has been the site of both coordination and conflict: coordination in discrete episodes (operations, refugee repatriation planning, Kurram crisis management); conflict in the public disputes over Azm-e-Istehkam (2024) and Operation Sarbakaf (2025), where the provincial government's opposition was overridden by federal/military decisions; and in the post-October 2025 government of CM Sohail Afridi, whose public positions have included opposition to military operations and demands for withheld funds (Wikipedia 2026e; Dawn 2024a; Paradigm Shift 2025).

**Assessment.** The architecture has no *published doctrine* — no document defines who decides what, under what legal authority, with what oversight, and with what reporting obligations. Coordination therefore depends on personalities and on the balance of power between Peshawar and Rawalpindi — a fragile basis for a decade-long campaign. The recommendation (Chapter 15) is a *statutory provincial counter-terrorism coordination framework* with defined roles, published metrics, and legislative oversight.

## 11.4 The Role of the Provincial Assembly and Legislative Measures

**The record.** The KP Assembly has been an active legislator on security-adjacent matters: the **KP Police Act 2017**; the **Levies and Khasadar** legislation (2019); amendments to the local government law; the extension of provincial laws to the merged districts; the **KP Universities/Education** laws; and various criminal-justice measures. It has also functioned as a forum for opposition demands on security (the PML-N white paper of September 2026; JUI-F's positions on operations; the opposition's challenges to the October 2025 chief-ministerial transition).

**Limits.** Three constraints have limited legislative influence: (i) *policy primacy of the executive/military*: war and internal-security decisions are made federally, with the Assembly informed rather than consulted (the Azm-e-Istehkam episode); (ii) *capacity*: few members have security expertise; committee systems are weak; and there is no dedicated, resourced security committee with research support; and (iii) *politicization*: post-2022, provincial-national politics turn nearly every security question into a partisan dispute (operations; refugee policy; the PTM), making sustained oversight difficult.

**Assessment.** A stronger Assembly role is not merely a democratic nicety; it is a functional requirement: legislative authorization and oversight protect the police from political interference, give operations legitimacy, and force the publication of the metrics (casualties, compensation, convictions) that accountability requires.

## 11.5 Judicial System and Rule of Law in KPK: Challenges and Reforms

**Structure and performance.** KP's judiciary comprises the Peshawar High Court (with circuit benches), district and sessions courts, and special courts (ATCs; anti-corruption; accountability; family; consumer). Documented problems: massive pendency and delays (national ATC pendency of 2,273 cases as of November 2024 being the counter-terrorism subset); inadequate court infrastructure and security; insufficient judges (vacancy rates in district judiciary); poor investigation quality (the binding constraint on convictions); legal-aid scarcity; witness intimidation; and the *informal justice* parallel system — jirgas, *riwaj*, and *qisas/diyat* settlements — which handles the bulk of disputes in the merged districts and much of rural KP. The Peshawar High Court has been notably active on merged-district rights (the NFC shortfall litigation; the Sehat Card petitions; the October 2025 chief-ministerial transition case).

**Counter-terrorism adjudication.** ATCs try cases under the ATA; their conviction rates are low nationally; appeals face delays; and the *military court* alternative has absorbed a significant number of cases (May 2023 events; terrorism charges against civilians). The 21st Amendment's military courts were repeatedly extended; the 27th Amendment's constitutional restructuring has shifted the appellate landscape. At the same time, the constitutional right to fair trial (Article 10A, inserted in 2010) and the documented complaints about military-court procedures (no charge sheets, verdict documents withheld until after appeal deadlines — matters litigated in the PHC in 2025) raise a structural conflict between counter-terrorism's demand for speed/certainty and the constitution's demand for due process. The study's position: legitimate counter-terrorism requires *lawful* convictions, and the long-run legitimacy of the state's campaign depends on resolving this conflict through an effective civilian system (protected judges, witness protection, forensic capacity, and specialized procedures), not through avoidance.

## 11.6 Anti-Terrorism Legislation: Strengths, Weaknesses and Abuses

**The statute.** The Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (as repeatedly amended — 2001, 2004, 2009, 2013, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020s) defines terrorism broadly, establishes ATCs with special procedures (in-camera trials; restricted adjournments; admission of certain confessions; bail restrictions), creates the legal basis for proscription (Schedule I) and for "Fourth Schedule" restrictive measures, and provides for the CTDs. Supporting laws include PECA (2016, amended 2025), the Fair Trial Act 2013 (surveillance warrants), the Investigation for Fair Trial Act, and the KP Police Act.

**Strengths.** Swift procedures; specialized courts; robust proscription powers; asset-freezing and financing tools; and the legal wherewithal for CTD operations — the essential legal infrastructure of a counter-terrorism campaign. International benchmarking (FATF) relied on these powers, and Pakistan's exit from the grey list (2022) reflected the system's formal compliance.

**Weaknesses and abuses.** Five are documented. (i) *Definitional breadth*: the ATA's terrorism definition can capture ordinary crimes, protests and political expression (a pattern visible in May 2023 prosecutions and in cases against journalists and activists). (ii) *Procedural erosion*: in-camera hearings, confessions, and restricted appeals reduce defendant safeguards; the Supreme Court/Lahore High Court have struck down elements at times. (iii) *Selective application*: groups and individuals are treated unequally according to political context (the PTM case is the sharpest recent example; Kashmir-focused groups' historical differential treatment is the oldest). (iv) *Preventive detention practice*: detention without charge under security laws is widely documented (and contested) in KP, including of individuals later released without proceedings. (v) *Military courts' expansion*: the transfer of civilian cases to military courts raises due-process issues that have been litigated and criticized (with two Supreme Court judges resigning in protest over the 27th Amendment's judicial restructuring, warning that it subordinated the judiciary to the executive).

**Assessment.** The legal architecture is adequate for prosecution but has been used for *governance by exception* — treating security as a domain exempt from ordinary rights and oversight. This produces short-term operational freedom and long-term legitimacy costs that the counter-insurgency demonstrably cannot afford (Chapters 6, 12).

## 11.7 National Counter Terrorism Authority (NACTA): Mandate, Performance and Critique

**Mandate.** NACTA (NACTA Act 2013) is charged with coordinating national counter-terrorism policy: threat assessment; coordination of federal and provincial agencies; proscription/Schedule IV maintenance; counter-financing (the National Task Force with 27 member organizations); and counter-narrative/deradicalization coordination.

**Performance.** Achievements: the establishment of Counter-Financing of Terrorism (CFT) units in provincial CTDs; rationalization of proscription lists and freezing of assets (thousands of accounts); coordination of the National Narrative document; and a convening function. Deficiencies: chronic under-staffing (the agency's own 2018 report found approximately three-quarters of approved posts unfilled; subsequent years did not resolve the professional-staffing deficit); limited budget authority; the absence of trained analysts in CT, financing and strategic assessment; and a *structural* limit: NACTA coordinates agencies that do not report to it and provinces that have their own mandates. The appointment in 2026 of a serving Major-General (Faisal Naseer) as head was presented as an effort to give coordination "teeth" — an understandable response to the capacity critique, but one that also underscores the pattern of military leadership of nominally civilian coordination bodies.

**Critique and reform.** The credible reform agenda for NACTA is: (i) professionalize the secretariat with competitively recruited civilian analysts; (ii) give it statutory authority to require data from provinces and agencies, plus a public annual threat report; (iii) separate its coordination role from operational mandates; and (iv) place counter-narrative and deradicalization functions in a dedicated, funded directorate with evaluation capacity. Without these, NACTA will remain, in the assessment of successive Pakistani and international reviews, "a good idea, poorly developed and executed."

## 11.8 Local Government System and Security Governance at the District Level

**Legal framework.** KP's local government system (the KP Local Government Act 2013, replaced/amended in 2019 and after) provides for district, tehsil and village/neighbourhood councils with devolved functions (some services, minor development). Elections were held in 2021–22 (PTI leading; JUI-F strong in parts; independents prominent) with by-elections in 2023 and 2026.

**Security governance at the district level.** District security governance operates through the Deputy Commissioner (administration, relief, coordination), the District Police Officer (policing), the district intelligence coordination committee, and — where they exist — elected councils and peace committees. The district is the level at which: relief and compensation are distributed; curfews and Section 144 orders are imposed; peace jirgas are convened; and, in principle, local buy-in is built. Its weaknesses: resource dependence on the province; absence of real budgetary authority for councils; politicized DC–DPO relations; and, in the NMDs, the absence of functioning local government altogether.

**Assessment.** Local government is the missing tier in KP's security architecture. Comparative evidence (Chapter 13) shows that local legitimacy — not merely force — determines whether cleared areas stay cleared. Elected local bodies with security-relevant functions (dispute resolution, community policing oversight, relief coordination, and local development budgets) create the intermediaries and accountability that KP lacks. The repeated deferral of meaningful local government in KP (both settled districts and NMDs) is, in this study's assessment, one of the least-examined contributors to the province's recurrent insecurity.

## 11.9 Political Party Dynamics and Their Impact on Security Policy

**The party map.** KP's politics features: PTI (dominant in provincial government since 2018 and hegemonic in the merged districts; in sustained conflict with the federal government and the military establishment since 2022–2023); JUI-F (strong religious-political network, particularly in the southern districts and parts of the NMDs; ambivalent or opposed on operations; pivotal on madrasa policy); ANP (Pashtun nationalist; historically supportive of operations against the TTP in 2009, now critical of military-led policy); PML-N (rising in some districts; author of the 2026 security white paper against the provincial government); PPP (in government federally with PJDP allies; weaker in KP); Jamaat-e-Islami (urban vote; strong on social services); PTM (proscribed; significant popular resonance among Pashtun youth). 

**Effects on security policy.** Three dynamics matter. (i) *The PTI–establishment conflict* has made counter-terrorism cooperation between the province and the centre conditional and contested; the KP government's opposition to Azm-e-Istehkam and its demands for withheld funds are the practical expression. (ii) *Federal-provincial partisanship*: a PTI-governed province, a PML-N-led centre, and an army-led strategy produce a triangular blame game in which responsibility for failures is diffused (and intelligence-sharing degrades, per ICG 2026). (iii) *Electoral incentives on operations*: operations and displacement are locally unpopular; opposition to operations wins votes in the affected districts — an incentive structure that will persist until security policy delivers *protective* dividends that constituents can see (funded compensation, rebuilt homes, protected schools).

**Assessment.** Political competition, properly channelled, is not an obstacle to normalization but a condition of it — provided that (a) security policy has a legislative and accountability framework within which contestation can occur, and (b) the fiscal settlement removes zero-sum fights over resources. The status quo — where security policy is commanded federally but costed provincially, and where the province's democratically elected government is excluded from strategic decisions — converts security into a partisan battleground at the exact moment unity is required.

## 11.10 Federal–Provincial Coordination: Achievements and Tensions

**Achievements.** Formal coordination exists: the National Security Committee; NACTA; the Apex Committee mechanism; federal fiscal transfers (NFC); and inter-provincial police cooperation (though undocumented). Specific cooperative successes include: the 2014–2018 campaign coordination; counter-financing and FATF compliance; and refugee repatriation planning.

**Tensions.** Four are structural: (i) *fiscal*: the merged districts' shortfall; withheld dues; the provinces' 2026 agreement to forgo incremental FBR collection to the federation; and the general under-funding of provincial security mandates; (ii) *policy authority*: operations are decided federally; the province's elected government bears the political cost; (iii) *legal/administrative*: federal agencies' role in the NMDs' administration (SAFRON legacies; federal PSDP projects) versus provincial authority; and (iv) *political*: the PTI-federal conflict; the refugee-repatriation policy (federal decision; provincial implementation); and the dispute over the October 2025 chief-ministerial transition (PHC adjudication).

**Assessment.** Federal-provincial coordination in KP is not a technical problem; it is a *constitutional design* problem: the province is expected to deliver internal security (a provincial subject) in a conflict whose strategy is set federally and whose fiscal burden is only partially recognized. The remedy — a statutory fiscal and coordination compact with defined responsibilities, guaranteed transfers, and joint public reporting — is discussed in Chapter 15.

## 11.11 The Role of the National Security Committee and National Security Adviser

**The NSC.** The National Security Committee (chaired by the Prime Minister; comprising the service chiefs and senior ministers; supported by the National Security Division and an NSA/Adviser on National Security) is Pakistan's apex security policy forum. In KP's security, the NSC's role has included: endorsing Azm-e-Istehkam (2024); authorizing/adjusting the Afghan policy (including the resort to cross-border operations in 2026); and framing the counter-terrorism narrative ("Fitna al-Khawarij" terminology; the "hostile agencies" framing). The NSC publishes little; its decisions are conveyed through ISPR and press releases.

**Critique.** The NSC's opacity and its military-heavy composition (with the 27th Amendment's CDF reforms further centralizing military authority) limit both civilian and provincial input into decisions that determine KP's fate. Comparative practice suggests two reforms: (i) formal inclusion of provincial chief executives in decisions on operations within their provinces; and (ii) public summaries of decisions and frameworks — not operational detail, but decision-affecting frameworks. Absent both, federal–provincial estrangement is structural, not accidental.

## 11.12 Policy Coherence and Implementation Gaps: A Critical Assessment

**The implementation gap as the master variable.** Across every domain examined in this chapter, the pattern is the same: articulate policy; partial implementation; reversal or erosion. The causes are identifiable: (i) *no binding mechanism* (policies without statutes, budgets or reporting); (ii) *fiscal fragility* (programs dependent on discretionary allocations that evaporate under fiscal pressure — the merged districts being the outstanding example); (iii) *political discontinuity* (each government re-announces rather than continues); (iv) *institutional interest conflicts* (madrasa boards versus the state; federal agencies versus the province; cops versus CTD versus military); and (v) *absence of evaluation* (almost no policy in the KP security domain has been independently evaluated; programs survive on claims, not evidence).

**Three policy deficits that most urgently need repair.** (1) *The fiscal deficit in security policy*: KP fights a national counter-insurgency on provincial resources; the merged districts lack the resources promised at merger. (2) *The accountability deficit*: no reliable public data on operations, casualties, convictions, or program outcomes; no functioning parliamentary oversight of security. (3) *The legitimacy deficit*: policy instruments that criminalize political dissent (PTM; journalists) and collective punishment practices undermine the state's claim to be protecting the very populations it asks to help.

**Design principle for the repair.** The consistent lesson of this chapter is that KP's governance system is strong on *command* and weak on *legitimacy, accountability and fiscal predictability*. A normalization framework must therefore invert the current bias: fewer operational announcements; more statutory, funded, measurable commitments; and a public, annual normalization report by the provincial and federal governments jointly — with the KP Assembly and the public as its audience.
