# CHAPTER 2 — HISTORICAL EVOLUTION OF SECURITY CHALLENGES IN KPK

## 2.1 Pre-Partition Era: The Tribal Frontier and British Colonial Security Architecture

The security architecture that shaped Khyber Pakhtunkhwa until 2018 was designed in the nineteenth century, and its logic — spatial exception, collective responsibility, purchased loyalty, and punitive expedition — outlasted the empire that produced it by seven decades. British consolidation began with the annexation of Punjab (1849) and the gradual extension of control to the frontier, culminating in the demarcation of the Durand Line with Afghanistan in 1893 (ratified in 1894 and formalized in later instruments) — a boundary that Afghanistan has never formally recognized and that structures frontier politics to this day.

**Institutions of indirect rule.** The apparatus that emerged comprised: (i) the *Political Agent (PA)* in each agency, an executive fusing judicial, administrative and intelligence functions; (ii) the *malik* system — state-recognized tribal intermediaries paid allowances and used as conduits of policy and, when necessary, of punishment; (iii) the *jirga* as the recognized dispute-resolution institution, bound to state purposes through quotas and inducements; (iv) paramilitary forces — *Khasadars* (tribal constabulary) and *Levies* — raised locally, lightly armed, and structurally incapable of confronting organized insurgency; and (v) the **Frontier Crimes Regulations (FCR) of 1901**, which codified collective punishment (including the ruinous "40 FCR" joint-liability clause), barred appeals, and excluded the ordinary criminal law and superior courts from the tribal areas (Ahmed 1983; Haq 2024). Along the settled frontier, the *Sandeman system* exchanged allowances for order; recurrent uprisings — including the Tirah campaign (1897–98), the Mohmand campaigns, and numerous punitive expeditions — established a doctrine of "butcher and bolt": columns would burn villages and withdraw, producing temporary compliance and durable grievance.

**The settled districts and the rise of Pashtun nationalism.** In the settled districts that became the North-West Frontier Province (NWFP; a Chief Commissioner's province from 1901, given partial representative institutions in 1932 and a Ministry in 1937), a different politics developed. Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgar (Red Shirts, founded 1929) fused Pashtun nationalism with a Gandhian non-violence and Congress alignment, winning provincial power in 1937 and presenting an alternative to both the colonial state and the later Pakistani state's approach to the frontier. The 1930 Qissa Khwani bazaar firing (Peshawar), and the Garhi and Ram Bagh shootings of 1930–31, gave the frontier a martyr tradition of its own. The 1947 referendum on NWFP's accession to Pakistan — held under conditions that Congress and the Khudai Khidmatgar boycotted, and excluding a "Pakhtunistan" option — bequeathed an unresolved legitimacy dispute between Pashtun nationalists and the Pakistan state that persists, in altered form, in the Pashtun Tahafuz Movement (Chapter 12).

**Analytical significance.** Three features of the colonial architecture proved path-dependent. First, *spatial legal dualism* — a governed core and an exception zone — created incentives for armed non-state actors of every kind to locate in the exception. Second, *purchased stability* (allowances, subsidies, and subsidies for intermediaries) created a political economy in which the withdrawal or fluctuation of payments could be destabilizing. Third, *punitive practice* generated cycles of vendetta and provided mobilizing memories for later generations. The colonial frontier was not simply pre-modern disorder; it was a *designed order of managed instability*, and its costs were transferred to post-independence Pakistan intact.

## 2.2 Post-Independence Period (1947–1979): Governance of the Tribal Belt

**Continuity of the exception.** Pakistan retained FATA's special status and the FCR practically unchanged. The tribal agencies were administered by federal Political Agents reporting to the Governor and, from the Zulfikar Ali Bhutto era, through the federal law-and-order machinery; the tribal areas had no representation in provincial politics in ordinary terms, no ordinary courts, and no taxation beyond minor revenue arrangements. The Frontier Regions were attached administratively to adjacent settled districts. Paradoxically, this preserved a permissive political space for Afghan-led irredentist claims: Afghanistan's refusal to recognize the Durand Line and the "Pakhtunistan" claim (pressed by Kabul at the United Nations and by proxy in the 1950s–1970s) made the Pashtun belt a theatre of bilateral contest, and the Pakistan People's Party's slogan that Pushtuns would decide their own fate "on the banks of the Indus" as good a summary of the state's strategic approach to the belt (Yusufzai 2019; Ahmad 2010).

**Domestic politics and the frontier.** NWFP's post-1947 politics fluctuated between Pashtun nationalist mobilizations (the 1948 arrest of Bacha Khan; the Red Shirts' marginalization), religious parties (the Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam, which formed the provincial government in alliance in the 1970s under Mufti Mahmud), and the Pakistan People's Party (which won loyalty through land reform rhetoric and patronage). The 1973 Constitution gave NWFP a full provincial assembly and integrated FATA's representation into the National Assembly by nominated and, later, limited-electoral means. Crucially, the constitution retained the FCR's exclusionary logic (Article 247), preserving the exception zone inside a federal constitution.

**Security posture.** Through the 1950s–1970s, the frontier was quiet by later standards: the state's principal coercive concerns were cross-border Afghan intrigues, smuggling (a structural frontier activity that financed both elites and bandits), and periodic tribal disputes. Two developments of long-term significance occurred: (i) the emergence of the *khassadar-levies-model* of light frontier policing, which persisted after 2018; and (ii) the expansion of religious education — the Darul Uloom Haqqania at Akora Khattak (founded 1947) and thousands of smaller madrasas — creating an educational infrastructure that, in the 1980s, became a recruitment and ideological conduit (Chapter 4).

**Assessment: the long peace and its hidden liabilities.** The 1947–1979 period is often romanticized as stability. It was stability purchased by the exclusion of four to five million citizens from rights and courts; by allowances that made tribal politics an auction of loyalties; and by the deliberate non-development of FATA's economy. When the exogenous shock of the Afghan war arrived, the frontier had no institutions capable of absorbing it.

## 2.3 The Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989): Jihad Infrastructure and Its Legacy

**Pakistan as front-line state.** The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan (December 1979) transformed the frontier into the logistical and ideological centre of a multinational jihad. Under General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq, Pakistan joined the United States, Saudi Arabia and other partners in a program of support to the Afghan resistance. The Inter-Services Intelligence's Afghan Bureau became the managing agency; seven Afghan mujahideen parties based in Peshawar received arms, funding and training support; the CIA's Operation Cyclone supplied weaponry — culminating in the Stinger programme — that entered the region in volumes and types that later armed new conflicts (Coll 2004; Yousaf and Adkin 1992; Riedel 2014). Saudi and Gulf funding financed religious institutions and networks that would re-emerge as Salafi-jihadist infrastructure. Estimates of the US programme's scale range to several billion dollars, alongside matching Saudi contributions.

**Effects on KPK.** Five consequences are traceable and consequential. (1) *Madrasa expansion*: the number of madrasas in Pakistan expanded dramatically during the 1980s; the KP and FATA belt became the sector's densest habitat, with Afghan and later Pakistani cohorts (Zaman 2002). (2) *Weapons diffusion*: Kalashnikov-pattern rifles, machine guns, RPGs, and later heavier systems entered the tribal economy and never left. (3) *Militant infrastructure*: training camps, logistics networks, and fundraising channels were established in FATA and Malakand, initially for Afghanistan. (4) *Refugee crisis*: Pakistan hosted approximately three million Afghan refugees at the peak, most in KP and FATA; the camps around Peshawar became a parallel society with its own politics, and their economies became entangled with smuggling and with the jihad (Chapters 9 and 10). (5) *Ideological normalization*: jihad as an instrument of state policy in Afghanistan was absorbed into religious and educational institutions in KP, creating a reservoir of legitimacy for later armed projects.

**The end of the war and its unfinished business.** The Geneva Accords (1988), Soviet withdrawal (1989), and the collapse of the Najibullah government (1992) did not demobilize the infrastructure that had been built. Weapons, cadres, logistics networks, madrasa networks, retired commanders, and a generation socialized into armed politics remained — and were available, as subsequent events showed, for redirection toward Kashmir, toward Afghanistan's civil war, toward sectarian conflict, and eventually toward the Pakistani state itself.

## 2.4 The Afghan Civil War and the Taliban Rise (1989–2001): Spillover into KPK

**From internecine war to the Taliban.** The post-1992 Afghan civil war — a five-faction conflict in which Pakistani policy was deeply entangled — devastated Kabul and southern Afghanistan. In 1994–96, the Taliban movement emerged from Kandahar, drawing on madrasa networks in Afghanistan and Pakistan (notably Balochistan and KP) and receiving Pakistani logistical and diplomatic support; by September 1996 it controlled Kabul, and by 2001 it governed most of the country (Rashid 2000; Riedel 2014). For KP and FATA, three spillovers were immediate: fighters and commanders circulated across the border; madrasa recruitment for the Taliban drew heavily on Pakistani Pashtun students; and the so-called "tribals" became the state's instrument of informal foreign policy (Chapter 9).

**Sectarianization of the frontier.** The 1980s also incubated sectarian organizations: Sipah-e-Sahaba Pakistan (1985, from Jhang in Punjab) and its more militant offshoot Lashkar-e-Jhangvi (1996) organized anti-Shia violence, initially directed at Shia professionals and later at whole communities; Shia militias and Iranian-supported networks proliferated in response. Kurram's Shia-majority Parachinar suffered attacks for decades; the tribal agency became a theatre in which local land disputes, regional sectarian networks, and the Afghan conflict interlocked (The Diplomat 2024; Chapter 3). By the late 1990s, the province had experienced multiple mass-casualty sectarian attacks, and a violent extremist infrastructure existed independent of the Afghan theatre.

**Musharraf, the Kashmir jihad, and the pivot.** The Kargil episode (1999), the Musharraf coup, and the state's tolerance of Kashmir-focused organizations (Jaish-e-Muhammad, Lashkar-e-Taiba, Harkat-ul-Mujahideen) maintained an ecosystem of armed non-state groups on Pakistani soil. The 9/11 attacks and the US ultimatum forced a strategic pivot: Pakistan joined the war on terror, severed formal support to the Taliban, and permitted the US access that enabled the defeat of the Taliban regime in late 2001. That decision — and its inconsistent implementation, especially in the tribal belt — set the stage for the insurgency.

**Assessment.** By 2001, KPK and FATA hosted: Afghan and Pakistani jihadist networks; sectarian organizations with armed wings; madrasa networks with a recruitment function; a weapons economy; and a political culture in which state and non-state armed actors had been entangled for two decades. The 2001–2008 period would convert this infrastructure into an insurgency — but the insurgency's raw materials were all present before 9/11.

## 2.5 Post-9/11 Era (2001–2008): The War on Terror Comes to KPK

**The fragile state–militant equilibrium (2001–2005).** Pakistan's initial approach combined cooperation with the United States (arrests of al-Qaeda figures; raids in FATA) with accommodation of the Taliban's remnants and tolerance of Kashmir-focused groups. In FATA, operations were episodic — South Waziristan (2002–2004), Bajaur and Mohmand — and alternated with peace agreements: in Shakai (April 2004) with Nek Muhammad, and subsequently with Baitullah Mehsud (Sararogha, February 2005) and others. These agreements typically traded reduced military pressure and payments for commitments to stop harbouring foreign fighters and attacking the state — commitments that were systematically violated. The pattern — operation, agreement, regroup, violation — became a template later analysed as producing "accidental guerrillas": heavy-handed operations combined with patronage deals discredited cooperating tribal elders, exposed them to reprisal, and made state promises unreliable (Kilcullen 2009).

**The emergence of the TTP (2007).** In December 2007, an umbrella of militant factions formed the *Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan* under Baitullah Mehsud, with the aim of establishing an Islamic state in Pakistan; its founding coalitions included factions in South and North Waziristan, Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Orakzai, Kurram and the settled districts of Swat, Dir and Shangla (Chapters 3 and 6). The Lal Masjid operation in Islamabad (July 2007) had a catalytic effect: the government's storming of the madrasa-mosque complex triggered retaliation across the province and FATA, and the TTP's cadres expanded through the Malakand Division, where the movement had local roots.

**Swat: from movement to insurgency.** Swat's insurgency grew from the neo-Taliban campaign of Maulana Fazlullah, who from 2007 used FM radio ("Mullah Radio") to promote a moral-reform program that moved from sermons on music and education to beheadings, attacks on barber shops, bans on girls' schooling (including the January 2009 destruction of schools and the shooting of Malala Yousafzai in October 2012, after the military campaign began), and the destruction of shrines. The Tehrik-i-Nifaz-i-Shariat-i-Muhammadi (TNSM), led by Sufi Muhammad, pressed for the imposition of Sharia; the February 2009 "Nizam-e-Adl Regulation" conceded Sharia-based courts in Malakand, but the arrangement collapsed within weeks as the militants expanded into Buner, Dir and Shangla — the "Buner" crisis that triggered the April–May 2009 offensive.

**Bajaur and the first big counter-offensive.** In August 2008, the military launched a major operation in Bajaur (Operation Sherdil), which killed hundreds of militants by official claims, displaced hundreds of thousands, and became a rehearsal for the 2009 campaigns. By 2008–09 the province had suffered mass-casualty suicide bombings (the Islamabad Marriott in September 2008; attacks on Pakistani security establishments), and the ANP-led provincial government (2008) faced an existential security crisis, losing in the period many of its senior figures to targeted killings.

**The IDP crisis and the 2009 turn.** Operation Rah-e-Rast (May 2009) in Malakand displaced an estimated 2–2.5 million people in weeks — the largest displacement in Pakistan since 1947 — and produced a nationwide political consensus (a consensual parliamentary resolution) supporting the operation. The military-led campaign, coupled with local resistance to the TTP's violence, broke the Swat insurgency by mid-2009, though sequels — Waziristan's Operation Rah-e-Nijat (October 2009), and rolling operations through 2010–2014 — followed (Chapter 6).

**Assessment of 2001–2008.** The period demonstrates the costs of a strategy that alternated between accommodating armed groups and mounting episodic operations. The state's inability to hold cleared terrain, its reliance on unreliable patronage, and its tolerance of sanctuary infrastructure in Afghanistan (from which the Afghan Taliban and, later, the TTP operated) meant that each campaign's gains were reversible. The most important positive development was the emergence of broad public opposition to the TTP within KP — an anti-militant political constituency that later made possible both the 2009 operations and the Swat recovery (Chapters 6 and 12).

## 2.6 The Peak of Militancy (2008–2014): TTP, Swat Insurgency and the Darkest Years

**The war's geography.** In the years after 2009 the TTP and its allies — including Lashkar-e-Jhangvi in sectarian violence and factions in Orakzai, Kurram and Khyber — carried out high-tempo campaigns: the 2010 attacks on Ahmadi mosques in Lahore and the Data Darbar shrine; the June 2011 attack on the Mehran naval base in Karachi; the August 2012 Kamra airbase attack (Chapter 6); the 2013 attacks on Christian targets in Peshawar (All Saints Church) and on ANP leaders (Bashir Ahmad Bilour, 2012) and tribal elders; and, the terminal event of the period, the December 2014 Army Public School attack in Peshawar, in which 132 schoolchildren and approximately 149 people were killed.

**Drone war and its consequences.** US drone strikes in the tribal areas peaked in 2008–2013, with several hundred strikes and hundreds to thousands of claimed deaths (estimates vary widely). The strikes degraded TTP and al-Qaeda leadership (Baitullah Mehsud was killed in 2009; Hakimullah Mehsud in November 2013), but they also generated intense anti-American mobilizations (notably the PTM's pre-history in the Waziristan protests of 2014), created civilian-harm grievances, and at times provided openings for TTP narratives of victimhood. The 2015 killing of Fazlullah's successor lineages and the 2016 killing of Mullah Akhtar Mansour in Balochistan also reshaped the Afghan Taliban's leadership.

**Sectarian and anti-minority violence.** The period 2008–2014 also saw sectarian and anti-minority violence at scale: Shia processions (the Ashura attacks), the Parachinar attacks, Hazara victimization, and the Kurram conflict, in which approximately 2,000 people died between 2007 and 2011 before the 2011 jirga-brokered truce (Chapter 3). For KP's Shia communities, security became a permanent emergency — visible in the fortified bazaars of Parachinar and Hangu.

**Contestation and the emergence of the 2014–2017 turnaround.** By 2013–2014, the cost of inaction had become politically unsustainable: the Peshawar APS attack produced a national trauma and a political consensus. The government's 2014 National Action Plan (NAP, twenty points; revised in 2021 to fourteen points) married military operations with judicial, financial, media and madrasa measures. Zarb-e-Azb (June 2014 — July 2017) in North Waziristan displaced about one million people, dismantled militant infrastructure and, by official claims, killed some 3,500 militants; the follow-on Khyber I–IV operations in the Khyber agency and operations elsewhere continued the campaign; Operation Radd-ul-Fasaad (2017) declared a nationwide consolidation campaign. Militant violence fell substantially: by 2018–2020, Pakistan recorded its lowest terrorism fatalities in over a decade (Chapter 6).

## 2.7 Military Operations and Their Aftermath (2014–2020)

**The 2014–2017 campaign.** Zarb-e-Azb was qualitatively different from prior operations: it was (i) pre-announced and population-warned, (ii) sustained over years rather than weeks, (iii) accompanied by security-force sweeps across the country, and (iv) executed with an explicit *hard* centre of gravity — physically clearing militant infrastructure and denying it space — rather than a hearts-and-minds emphasis. Its successes were real: the TTP lost its principal territorial base, leadership attrition accelerated, and the incidence of mass-casualty attacks declined. Its limitations are equally documented: the recovery and reconstruction phase was slow and incomplete; the displacement (about one million people from North Waziristan alone) and its economic disruption generated grievances; and the campaign's extension into the border control regime (the fencing of the Durand Line from 2017, largely completed by 2021) shut down licit mobility along a border on which border communities' livelihoods depended (Chapter 9).

**The NAP era and institutional reforms.** The 2014 NAP's twenty points included military courts (established by the 21st Amendment, initially for two years and extended repeatedly), prosecution of terrorism cases, countering hate speech and financing, madrasa regulation, and NACTA's strengthening. Implementation was uneven: kinetic provisions were pursued energetically; madrasa registration was resisted by religious parties; counter-financing made progress (FATF compliance and the 2018–2022 process were consequential); and judicial reform lagged, producing what critics described as a correctional default: mass detention with low conviction rates (Chapter 7).

**Political economy of the post-2014 recovery.** The post-2014 period saw genuine reconstruction in Swat and parts of Malakand: roads (including the Swat expressway), rebuilt bazaars, restored tourism, and school reconstruction. Its unevenness — the contrast between Malakand's recovery and the tribal districts' continued deprivation — would become politically explosive after 2018, when the merger promised to close the gap (Chapter 5).

## 2.8 The Current Phase (2020–2026): Resurgence, FATA Merger and Emerging Dynamics

**The Afghan withdrawal and the TTP revival (2020–2022).** The Doha agreement (February 2020) and the US withdrawal (August 2021) transformed the TTP's operating environment. With the Afghan Taliban in power and TTP leadership hosted in Afghanistan, Pakistani officials negotiated — with Afghan Taliban hosting/mediation — a short-lived ceasefire (November–December 2021); it collapsed, and formal talks ended in November 2022. From 2022 onward, TTP attacks resumed an upward trajectory; by 2023, attacks had risen 17 per cent year-on-year (306 attacks, 23 suicide bombings) and major attacks included the January 2023 Police Lines mosque bombing in Peshawar (over 100 killed, 86 of them police) and the July 2023 Bajaur bombing. The TTP publicly declared its campaign against the Pakistani state in 2023 and escalated.

**The merger moment (2018–2026).** The 25th Amendment (May 2018) merged FATA into KP, extending the provincial assembly, formal courts, and police; adding 23 seats to a 145-member assembly; and committing — through parliamentary resolutions — 3 per cent of the divisible pool for ten years to bring the merged districts to parity. Implementation, however, was slow; the fiscal commitment was met at a fraction of its value (Chapter 5); and the security environment in the merged districts deteriorated as the TTP re-established presence. By 2025–2026 the merged districts and adjoining southern settled districts were the epicentre of violence — 40 per cent of all TTP violence since 2021 occurred in the seven NMDs, and nearly 60 per cent of TTP violence occurred in the south of the province (ACLED 2025).

**State response, 2024–2026.** Azm-e-Istehkam (June 2024) was announced as a reinvigorated national counter-terrorism campaign with kinetic and diplomatic components; it faced immediate opposition from the KP government (PTI), JUI-F, ANP, PTI-affiliated and PTM circles, and questions about resourcing and political consensus. Operation Sarbakaf (July 2025) targeted TTP hideouts in Bajaur's Lowi Mamund tehsil and displaced an estimated 100,000 people (Arab News/Paradigm Shift 2025). The October 2025 escalation between Pakistan and Afghanistan produced a Qatari/Turkish-brokered Doha ceasefire and an Istanbul monitoring mechanism (November 2025) that failed to hold. On 21–26 February 2026, cross-border clashes escalated into Operation Ghazab-lil-Haq, and on 4 March 2026 the TTP announced its spring offensive (Operation Khyber). In 2026, KP recorded its highest first-half police losses on record (122 killed), the deadliest single police-complex attack since 2023 (Kohat, 18 September 2026), and continuing expansion of operations into districts previously quiet (Swat, Kohat, Karak) (Chapters 3 and 6).

**Political transitions and the provincial state.** KP's provincial government changed hands in October 2025 when Chief Minister Ali Amin Gandapur resigned on the instructions of Imran Khan and Sohail Afridi (from Khyber District) was elected on 13 October 2025. The new Chief Minister adopted a posture critical of federal policy — opposing military operations in KP, blaming federal "flawed policies" for the resurgence, and demanding the release of withheld war-on-terror dues and constitutional payments (Wikipedia 2026e; Business Recorder 2026). The federal–provincial estrangement over counter-terrorism strategy is, per the International Crisis Group's 2026 analysis, itself a strategic variable: it degrades intelligence coordination between the army-led campaign and the provincial police (ICG 2026).

## 2.9 Lessons from History: Patterns, Cycles and Recurring Failures

Seven historical patterns are analytically load-bearing.

**1. The system of exception transfers costs forward.** Colonial indirect rule, FATA's continuation of the FCR, and the post-2001 doctrine of "military-first" governance created a legal and institutional vacuum that armed actors filled. Each attempt to end the exception — the 2018 merger; the extension of courts; the creation of NMD policing — has been undermined by under-resourcing and by the simultaneous requirement to fight a war in the same space.

**2. Sanctuaries set the clock.** The A–Z of KP's militancy — the anti-Soviet infrastructure, the Afghan Taliban's Peshawar-based parties, al-Qaeda's post-2001 sanctuary in FATA, the TTP's post-2021 sanctuary in Afghanistan — is a history of sanctuary creation and denial. No campaign has produced durable quiet while a sanctuary remained (Chapter 9).

**3. Suppression decays without political and fiscal follow-through.** Every kinetic success — Swat 2009, Waziristan 2014–17 — has decayed within five to seven years in the absence of completed governance, justice and economic transition. This is the central empirical regularity that Chapter 6 tests and Chapter 15 operationalizes.

**4. Negotiations without verification and enforcement become force multipliers for the stronger party's opponent.** The 2004–2008 agreements, the 2021–22 talks, and repeated local *aman* (peace) deals with militant factions were entered without effective monitoring, and each collapse strengthened the militants' position. Treating talks as a tactical pause rather than a strategic instrument has discredited negotiation as an option.

**5. Displacement is a security variable, not merely a humanitarian one.** The 2009 Swat displacement, Zarb-e-Azb's million-person displacement from North Waziristan, Sarbakaf's 100,000 in Bajaur, the Tirah displacement of 2025–2026 (33,800 families registered by January 2026), and the Kurram crisis have each produced: (i) humanitarian emergencies; (ii) grievance narratives about compensation and return; (iii) a population vulnerable to militant recruitment through hardship and humiliation. Repetition of large-scale operations without pre-positioned recovery financing has converted each military gain into a longer-term political liability.

**6. The intra-Pashtun politics of the frontier is decisive.** Where communities resisted militancy — the Malakand lashkars in 2009, Swat's Pasoon movement and protest traditions, anti-militant jirgas in Bajaur and Mohmand — violence fell. Where state policy alienated cooperative communities — through collective punishment, closure of civic space, or the proscription of political movements (the PTM ban of October 2024) — it narrowed the anti-militant coalition (Chapters 4 and 12).

**7. Federal–provincial estrangement is a weapon for insurgents.** When Karachi (or Islamabad) and Peshawar are at odds — over funding, over operations, over refugees — the counter-insurgency is fragmented and the state's messaging is split. From 2023 to 2026 this divergence has widened: the KP government publicly opposed Azm-e-Istehkam and Sarbakaf-style operations; the federal government and military argued for decisive force; and the police, provincially controlled, absorbed the casualties (ICG 2026; Dawn 2024a).

Taken together, these patterns yield the study's historical thesis: **the province's violence is best explained not as a series of discrete campaigns but as an equilibrium — periodically disturbed by force, repeatedly restored by the failure to build the three monopolies.**
