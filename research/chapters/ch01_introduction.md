# CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION AND CONTEXTUAL FRAMEWORK

## 1.1 Background and Context of KPK's Security Landscape

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa occupies a position in Pakistan's political imagination that is simultaneously strategic and stigmatized. It is the province through which every conqueror's road to the subcontinent ran — Alexander, Mahmud of Ghazni, Babur, and in the imperial imagination the Khyber Pass as the gateway of invasion — and it is the province that has absorbed the largest share of Pakistan's post-2001 conflict. Between 2001 and 2026, no other Pakistani province has experienced comparably sustained exposure to militant violence, military operations, mass displacement, and the militarization of everyday life. The cumulative TTP-linked death toll recorded by the South Asia Terrorism Portal since 23 December 2007 stands at 11,165, of which KP and the former FATA account for the overwhelming majority (SATP 2026c).

Three foundational facts frame this study. First, **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's violence is not a deviation from its political history but a product of it.** The colonial administrative system known as indirect rule — the Frontier Crimes Regulations (FCR), the Political Agent, the malik system, and collective punishment — froze the tribal belt in a legal and political exception that survived Pakistani independence by seventy years. When the state finally ended that exception through the 25th Amendment in 2018, it did so without a fiscal transfer that would have made the extension of governance real (Chapters 4 and 5).

Second, **KP's security environment is inseparable from Afghanistan's.** Every major inflection in the province's threat landscape — the anti-Soviet jihad, the Afghan civil war, the Taliban emirate, the post-9/11 war on terror, and the consequences of the Taliban's return to Kabul in August 2021 — began on the western side of the Durand Line and worked its way eastward. The 2026 outbreak of direct Pakistan–Afghanistan hostilities formalized what had been an asymmetric problem into an interstate one (Chapter 9).

Third, **security outcomes in KP have moved in cycles rather than in a line.** The province witnessed a low-intensity insurgency in the mid-2000s, full-scale insurgency in Swat and the tribal agencies in 2007–2009, an intensive counter-offensive in 2009–2017 that produced genuine improvement, and a deterioration from 2021 that, by 2025, had returned Pakistan to its worst year of terrorism-linked fatalities since 2015 — with KP absorbing 68 per cent of the national toll (CRSS 2025b). Understanding why suppression has repeatedly failed to become settlement is the central analytical task of this monograph.

## 1.2 Geographical, Demographic and Ethno-Linguistic Profile

**Geography.** Khyber Pakhtunkhwa covers approximately 101,741 km² (excluding the disputed frontier areas), rising from the Peshawar valley (~300 m elevation) to the Hindu Kush, Karakoram and Himalayan highlands, with peaks above 7,000 m in Chitral. Its western boundary with Afghanistan — a segment of the 2,611–2,640 km Durand Line — runs across some of the most rugged terrain in the world, historically traversed by passes rather than roads: Khyber, Kurram, Gomal, Tochi, and the high passes of Bajaur and Mohmand. It borders Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir to the north-east, Punjab and Islamabad Capital Territory to the east and south-east, and Balochistan to the south. The province is seismically active, highly exposed to climate hazards (glacial lake outburst floods, flash floods, landslides), and dependent on a narrow corridor — the Peshawar valley — for much of its population and infrastructure.

**Demography.** The 2017 census recorded 35.5 million residents in KP including FATA (5.0 million); the 2023 digital census recorded approximately 40.86 million for KP as constituted today (with the proviso that the census was the first to include the merged districts as part of the province). The KP government portal cites a population of about 40 million — 17 per cent of Pakistan's total — with roughly half under the age of 25 (KP Government 2024). Peshawar, Mardan, Swat, Abbottabad, Dera Ismail Khan, Kohat and Nowshera are the largest settled districts; the merged districts (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Orakzai, Kurram, North Waziristan and South Waziristan) add roughly five million residents across 27,220 km². Urbanization is accelerating but remains below national averages; the province's demographic profile is a *youth bulge* in the strict sense — a large cohort entering the labour market in an economy that cannot absorb it (Chapter 10).

**Ethno-linguistic composition.** Pashto is spoken by roughly three-quarters of the population; Hindko (Hazara division), Khowar (Chitral), Kohistani, Saraiki (in the southern districts), Urdu and Punjabi minorities complete the picture. The province is not ethnically homogeneous: Hazara Division is predominantly Hindko-speaking and hosts a large Shia Hazara community with a distinct historical experience of persecution and self-defence (the 2020 Machh and 2013 Quetta bombings targeted Hazaras outside KP, but Hazara insecurity has been acute within KP as well); Chitral is Ismaili-majority with a distinctive governance tradition; Parachinar in Kurram is one of the few Shia-majority areas in the province and has been the theatre of the most lethal sectarian conflict in recent Pakistani history (Chapter 3).

**Political geography.** KP's political economy is organized around five axes: (i) the settled districts of the Peshawar valley and Hazara, economically integrated with Punjab and Islamabad; (ii) Malakand Division, the site of the 2007–2009 insurgency and of post-2009 recovery, now again contested; (iii) the tribal districts/newly merged districts, the frontier zone with the weakest governance and highest violence; (iv) the southern districts (Dera Ismail Khan, Tank, Bannu, Lakki Marwat, Karak, Kohat, Hangu), which since 2023 have become the insurgency's new operational centre of gravity; and (v) the Afghanistan border itself, an instrument of leverage, commerce and conflict.

**Strategic significance.** The province hosts the Khyber Pass and the Torkham crossing (Pakistan's principal land gateway to Afghanistan and Central Asia), the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor's western alignment and the Rashakai Special Economic Zone, major hydel potential, the Afghan refugee population, and a substantial share of the country's military infrastructure, including Corps Headquarters Peshawar and numerous cantonments and training establishments.

## 1.3 Statement of the Research Problem

The literature on Pakistani militancy is rich on causes and campaigns but thin — and increasingly dated — on *normalization as an end-state*. Three deficits motivate this study.

*First, the analytical deficit.* Post-2021 scholarship has emphasized the Taliban's return and the TTP's sanctuary, correctly, but this has crowded out equally important variables: the fiscal collapse of the FATA merger, the institutional bifurcation of the counter-insurgency (military-led operations; provincial police exposure), the collapse of judicial extension, the failure of deradicalization to scale, and the political estrangement between the federal government and KP's provincial administration in 2023–2026. A mono-causal, "Kabul-sponsorship" account cannot explain why violence expanded from the border districts into settled southern districts and, by 2026, into Swat and Kohat — or why Pakistan's most violent districts include districts with no Afghan border at all.

*Second, the empirical deficit.* Public data on KP security are disaggregated inconsistently, and official figures often diverge from independent monitors and from each other. There is, for example, no publicly available, audited time-series on (a) civilian casualties from state operations in KP; (b) conviction and sentencing outcomes in KP anti-terrorism courts by year; (c) the merged districts' fiscal absorption capacity; (d) police personnel posted to, versus sanctioned for, the NMDs; or (e) the outcomes of deradicalization cohorts beyond program self-reports. This study assembles what exists and marks what does not.

*Third, the policy deficit.* Pakistan has produced a succession of strategic documents and named operations — the 2014 National Action Plan and its 2021 revision, the National Internal Security Policy, Radd-ul-Fasaad, Khyber I–IV, Azm-e-Istehkam (2024), Sarbakaf (2025), Ghazab-lil-Haq (2026). Each was announced as comprehensive. None has been accompanied by a public, measurable normalization framework with sequenced political, judicial, fiscal and socio-economic milestones. The research problem, in short, is not simply "why is KP violent?" but "**what configuration of force, governance, fiscal transfer, and political legitimation would move KP from episodic suppression to durable normalization — and what has obstructed each attempt?**"

## 1.4 Research Questions

The study addresses ten primary questions, each mapped to chapters.

1. **Drivers.** What are the primary and secondary drivers of insecurity in KP, and how have their relative weights changed across the 2001–2011, 2012–2017, 2018–2021 and 2021–2026 phases? (Chapters 2, 4)
2. **Kinetic efficacy.** To what extent have large-scale military operations (Rah-e-Rast, Rah-e-Nijat, Zarb-e-Azb, Radd-ul-Fasaad, Khyber I–IV, Azm-e-Istehkam, Sarbakaf) achieved *sustainable* normalization as distinct from temporary suppression, and what explains the decay of their gains? (Chapter 6)
3. **Merger.** How has the 2018 FATA merger altered security dynamics in the merged districts, and which institutional gaps — fiscal, judicial, policing, political — remain binding? (Chapter 5)
4. **Development–security nexus.** Is the relationship between socio-economic development and security in KP linear, conditional, or reflexive — that is, does development produce security, security produce development, or do they co-produce each other — and what does the evidence from KP's post-2009 districts show? (Chapter 10)
5. **Deradicalization.** How effective are Pakistan's deradicalization and counter-narrative instruments (Sabawoon, Mishal, Sparlay, Rastoon and successors) in the KP context, on what evidence, and with what limitations? (Chapter 8)
6. **Afghanistan.** What is the causal architecture of the Afghanistan factor in KP's security, and what minimal, verifiable cross-border arrangements could reduce the sanctuary dividend without a comprehensive settlement? (Chapter 9)
7. **Governance and rule of law.** Which governance and rule-of-law reforms — apex coordination, local government, judicial extension, police professionalization, anti-terrorism law safeguards — are most strongly associated with reductions in violence in KP's own record? (Chapters 7, 11)
8. **Comparative lessons.** Which transferable lessons from other conflict transitions are applicable to KP, and which are not, given KP's specificity (intra-state but cross-border insurgency; nuclear-armed state; federal structure; no comprehensive peace process)? (Chapter 13)
9. **Society and peace.** What roles do civil society, media, religious institutions, jirgas, women and youth play in sustaining or undermining normalization, and how has the shrinking civic space (including the proscription of the PTM) affected those roles? (Chapter 12)
10. **Futures and economics.** What are the most plausible security futures for KP through 2035, what early-warning indicators distinguish them, and what is the economic cost of insecurity versus the dividend of normalization? (Chapters 14, 15)

Two subsidiary questions run throughout: how do **gender dynamics** affect normalization (Chapters 8, 10, 12); and what is the **cost of insecurity** to KP's economy and public finances (Chapters 10, 15)?

## 1.5 Significance and Relevance of the Study

The study's significance is both scholarly and policy-relevant.

**Scholarly.** KP presents a rare configuration in conflict studies: a well-documented, decades-long insurgency in a middle-income, nuclear-armed federal state, in which the state has repeatedly alternated between accommodation, military campaigns, merger-as-integration, and externally-directed diplomacy. It is therefore a natural experiment for testing propositions about counter-insurgency's political primacy, the conditions under which legal integration is substantive rather than formal, and the relationship between cross-border sanctuaries and internal violence. The study contributes an original dataset synthesis and an original analytical framework (the "three monopolies" model advanced in the Abstract and Chapter 16).

**Policy.** Three audiences are addressed. For Pakistan's federal and provincial governments, the study offers a phased roadmap with financing mechanisms and institutional responsibilities (Chapter 15). For international partners (UN agencies, IFIs, and bilateral donors), it identifies entry points — the merged districts' fiscal settlement, justice-sector completion, education and deradicalization — where external assistance is plausible and consequential, and it cautions against financing purely kinetic approaches. For regional actors and mediators, it analyses the 2025–2026 Afghan–Pakistan escalation and the design of verification mechanisms (Chapter 9).

**Moral and human.** The study insists on the human dimension. KP's losses include thousands of police officers, soldiers, tribal elders, religious scholars, journalists, health workers, teachers, and civilians; millions of displaced persons; and communities — Kurram's Shia, Swat's returnees, Tirah's displaced families, Hazara's Shia, women targeted for education — whose suffering is under-recorded. Analysis that abstracts these losses into "security trends" dishonours the evidence itself.

## 1.6 Scope and Delimitations

**Included:** the whole of KP, with detailed treatment of the merged districts and, where relevant, spillover into Islamabad and Balochistan; the historical period from the colonial frontier to October 2026; all major threat categories; state and non-state actors; Afghan and Indian regional factors as they bear on KP; domestic and international policy frameworks.

**Excluded or de-emphasized:** detailed military operational history of Pakistan's eastern border or Kashmir (referenced only where it affects KP's security economy and force allocation); classified counter-terrorism capabilities; and forensic attribution of specific attacks where evidence is not public — the study attributes responsibility only where it is claimed by the group, officially attributed by Pakistani authorities, or independently assessed by credible monitors, and it flags the basis of attribution in each case.

**Delimitation note.** The study does not attempt a comprehensive account of Balochistan's insurgency (a distinct conflict with distinct actors), except where it intersects with KP's threat landscape through the TTP's expanded footprint, operations in Balochistan attributed to KP-based networks, and Pakistan's national counter-insurgency architecture (azm-e-Istehkam's national framing; NACTA's national coordination).

## 1.7 Conceptual and Theoretical Framework

The study is theoretically plural but analytically disciplined: each framework is used for the question it can answer, and none is treated as a master key. Figure 1.1 maps frameworks to chapters in narrative form.

**(a) Human security (UNDP).** The 1994 UNDP formulation (economic, food, health, environmental, personal, community and political security) is used as the *evaluative* standard for normalization, translated into KP terms: freedom from violent death; freedom from displacement; access to livelihood; education; and protection from arbitrary state or non-state coercion. It corrects a persistent distortion in security policy assessment — measuring success by body counts of militants rather than by the security experienced by residents.

**(b) Conflict transformation (Lederach).** Lederach's distinction between the *presenting problem* (violence) and the *relational substrate* (the patterns of exclusion, domination, and retaliation that regenerate violence) structures Chapter 4's root-cause analysis and Chapter 15's roadmap. Applied to KP: military campaigns address the presenting problem; the relational substrate includes the unresolved Kurram land-and-identity conflict, the exclusion of the tribal belt from fiscal federalism, the Pashtun–state estrangement documented by the ICG (2026), and sectarian securities dilemmas. Transformation requires platforms where these relations can be renegotiated — which is precisely what the current architecture lacks.

**(c) Counter-insurgency doctrine (Galula, Kilcullen, Trinquier and their critics).** Three propositions are tested against KP's record: Galula's primacy of political over military action ("80 per cent political"); Kilcullen's warning that heavy-handed manoeuvre in peripheral tribal zones creates "accidental guerrillas" by pushing neutral communities into the insurgency; and population-centric doctrine's requirement that protection of civilians be the operational centre of gravity. Chapter 6 assesses Pakistan's operations against these propositions and finds a decisive failure on the first (political purpose), partial success on the second (learning from 2009 civilian-harm episodes), and episodic rather than systematic compliance with the third.

**(d) State-building and governance theory.** The study adopts a "monopoly of violence plus governance quality" model: security is produced not only by coercion but by legitimate institutions that citizens choose to use — courts that convict, police that respond, tax offices that are not predatory, and local government that allocates. KP's merged districts are the critical case: legal integration without institutional capacity produced what might be called *paper sovereignty* (Chapter 5).

**(e) Securitization theory (Copenhagen School).** Securitization — the move that lifts an issue from politics into existential "security" framing — explains a great deal of KP's post-2001 history: the framing of the Pashtun belt as a security space produced emergency law, mass detention, curfews and evictions; the framing of the TTP restored "militancy" to the centre of national politics in 2024–2026 and produced Operation Azm-e-Istehkam; and the proscription of the Pashtun Tahafuz Movement in October 2024 under Section 11B of the Anti-Terrorism Act is the clearest illustration of securitization applied to a *political* movement (Chapter 12).

**(f) Political economy of conflict.** The war economy — cross-border smuggling, narcotics, illegal mining, extortion, kidnap-for-ransom, and the rents associated with military contracting and border regulation — is analysed as a system with its own beneficiaries. The study resists the reduction of KP's conflict to criminality but tests the proposition that war-economy actors matter more in *duration* than in *initiation*.

**(g) Greed versus grievance (Collier–Hoeffler).** The model's core insight — that opportunities and lootability can sustain conflicts whose causes are grievances — is applied in Chapter 4 (§4.10) to explain the persistence of a conflict whose drivers have changed over twenty years, and its limits are noted: KP's fighters are not primarily loot-seeking; recruitment narratives are ideological and reactive; and the "greed" mechanism operates chiefly at the level of armed group finance and elite networks, not rank-and-file motivation.

**(h) Whole-of-nation approach.** Used normatively in Chapter 15: the sequencing of force, regulation, fiscal transfer, service delivery and political inclusion, with explicit coordination mechanisms (and the honest observation that Pakistan has repeatedly announced whole-of-government approaches without whole-of-government budgets).

**(i) Resilience and community-based security.** The study treats community resilience — peace committees, jirgas, tribal lashkars, women's networks, and local media — as a security *capability*, not a substitute for the state, drawing on the KP record (Chapter 12) and comparative practice (Chapter 13).

## 1.8 Definitions of Key Terms

**Normalization.** In this study, the condition in which violence is at levels consistent with ordinary criminality in a comparable society; institutions of coercion (police, courts, prisons) operate as the ordinary providers of security; the population's dominant expectation is that disputes will be resolved by law rather than force; and the state does not require exceptional emergency powers to maintain order. Normalization is a continuum with four thresholds: *suppression* (attacks reduced by force), *consolidation* (reconstruction and return), *institutionalization* (indigenous police/justice capability under civilian authority), and *normalization* (violence legible as crime, politics no longer conducted under existential threat framing). Most analyses of KP conflate the first with the fourth.

**Security.** Following the human security tradition, security is defined as the condition of being free from chronic threats of violence and coercion, and free from fear of losing life, livelihood, or physical integrity — whether the source is militancy, criminality, or arbitrary state action.

**Militancy.** Organized, armed, non-state political violence in pursuit of objectives that are framed ideologically (religious, ethno-nationalist, or revolutionary). The term is used descriptively without implying legitimacy.

**Insurgency.** A contested struggle between a state and non-state actors seeking to control territory, populations, or the state itself, using irregular force and political mobilization. In KP, the TTP's campaign since 2021 approximates insurgency in the border districts (parallel taxation, checkpoints, judicial impositions, and territorial contention) and terrorism in the settled districts.

**Terrorism.** Deliberate violence (or threat of violence) against non-combatants or symbolic targets, designed to produce psychological effects beyond its immediate victims, in service of political objectives. The study applies the term to attacks by both non-state actors and, where the evidence supports it and international law so defines it, does not shrink from examining state conduct — while noting that "state terrorism" is a contested category in Pakistani law and discourse.

**Counter-terrorism (CT) and counter-insurgency (COIN).** CT denotes measures against terrorist networks — intelligence, policing, prosecution, disruption, financing; COIN denotes a political-military campaign to defeat an insurgency and restore legitimate governance. Much of Pakistan's policy conflates them, with the effect that strategies designed for CT (targeted raids, arrests, prosecutions) are applied to an insurgency problem (territorial and popular contention), and vice versa.

**Deradicalization, disengagement and rehabilitation.** *Deradicalization* denotes change in beliefs; *disengagement* denotes change in behaviour (cessation of violence) without necessarily abandoning beliefs; *rehabilitation* denotes the social, psychological and economic restoration of the individual. Programs that achieve only disengagement may reduce violence in the short term while leaving the underlying belief system intact — a crucial distinction for evaluating the KP programs (Chapter 8).

**Peacebuilding.** Sustained external and internal support to institutions and relationships after violence to prevent relapse — distinct from peacemaking (ending the fighting) and peacekeeping (monitoring).

**State-building.** The construction of legitimate, effective institutions capable of providing security, justice, revenue and services; distinguished from *state formation* (any process producing a state) and from *regime-building* (strengthening incumbents).

**Newly Merged Districts (NMDs) / erstwhile FATA.** The seven former tribal agencies (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Orakzai, Kurram, North Waziristan, South Waziristan) plus six Frontier Regions merged into KP by the 25th Amendment (2018). "Tribal districts" and "merged districts" are used interchangeably in Pakistani discourse; this study prefers NMDs except where historical fidelity requires "FATA."

**Alignment structures.** Local tribal militias raised by the state or communities — peace committees, lashkars, *chagha* (village defence) groups — used in KP and historically in FATA. Their double-edged nature (effective defence; entrenchment of communal division; exposure to militant reprisal) is analysed in Chapters 3 and 12.

**Jirga.** The Pashtun assembly-based dispute-resolution institution, ranging from small local jirgas to grand (Loya) jirgas with quasi-judicial functions. Its status and function have been transformed by the merger and by militancy; a central question in Chapter 12 is whether reform can preserve its legitimacy dividend while ending practices — collective punishment, exclusion of women and youth — inconsistent with rights.

**Sanctuary and the "sanctuary dividend."** The military advantage accruing to an armed group from a protected rear area outside the state's reach (here, Afghan territory plus Pakistan's own historically ungoverned tribal belt). The study refers to the *sanctuary dividend* as the specific increment of capability the TTP derives from Afghan territory, and treats its reduction as a necessary but not sufficient condition for normalization.

**Whole-of-nation approach.** Pakistan's own official idiom, adopted in policy documents since 2014, for a comprehensive strategy in which "all elements of national power" are applied. The study uses the term critically, noting that the resources committed to its non-military elements have consistently been an order of magnitude smaller than those committed to force (Chapter 11).

**Key figures of reference.** To avoid ambiguity, the study uses "TTP" for the umbrella and names factions (Hafiz Gul Bahadur Group/HGB, Ittehad-ul-Mujahideen Pakistan/IMP, Jamaat-ul-Ahrar/JuA, Lashkar-e-Jhangvi) where sources distinguish them; "ISKP" for the Islamic State–Khorasan Province; "PTM" for the Pashtun Tahafuz Movement (proscribed in October 2024); "NACTA" for the National Counter Terrorism Authority; "CTD" for provincial Counter-Terrorism Departments; "ISPR" for the Inter-Services Public Relations; "NMDs" for Newly Merged Districts.
